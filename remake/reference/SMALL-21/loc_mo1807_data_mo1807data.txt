Summary of Bridge and Setting

This bridge carries vehicular traffic over fowler Creek on the Interurban Road, 1.7 miles south of Camden
Point in Platte County. Built in 1912-13 for use as an electric railway bridge, it is a two-span filled
concrete arch structure of the Luten design. Each span is 47.5' in length; the overall bridge length is 95'.
The 18.1' wide structure has a 16.4' roadway width. The bridge is 23' in height over the wooded creek
bottom, which is surrounded by farmland. A farmstead with a nineteenth century house is located on the
northeast side of the bridge and creek.

The Interurban Road follows 16.5 miles of the original 45.88-mile route of the St. Joseph line of the
former Kansas City, Clay County and St. Joseph (KCCC&SJ) Railway. The road extends from the north
edge of the town of Ferrelview on the south to the south edge of the town of Dearborn on the north.

The 1913-33 electric interurban railway was an important transportation system that operated in northwest
Missouri because it was the first of its kind to provide fast, high-speed passenger and freight service
between Kansas City and the areas north of the Missouri River. It also was one of the state's longest
electric interurban railways. To successfully compete against existing steam railroads, the railway's owners
financed the construction of the straightest, shortest possible route and established several stations along
the route. Carrying out the plan required cutting across the rugged, hilly terrain of a rural region dotted
with small towns, farms, and woodland. The company operated two lines: one to St. Joseph; the other
to Excelsior Springs. Over those lines, the KCCC&SJ provided an efficient travel connection between the
two largest cities of the region, Kansas City and St. Joseph; at the same time, it served as an important
component of the development of growing suburban communities in Platte, Buchanan and Clay Counties.

The railway ceased operations in 1933 due to financial difficulties and was sold at auction in 1934. A
salvage company purchased the metals and, in Platte County, had completed its salvage work before
December 1935. The concrete bridges were left in place after the tracks were removed; many of them were
converted, along with the old railway road bed, into a farm-to-market road between 1935-40.

History of the Kansas City, Clay County and St. Joseph Railway

The KCCC&SJ Railway, constructed between 1911-13 by the Wyandotte Construction Company, Kansas
City, was the first high-speed electric interurban service to connect Kansas City with towns and cities
located north of the Missouri River in northwest Missouri. The idea for such a line had been considered
for some time, but no plan had been carried out previously because the cost of construction was
considered too great to make a railway a profitable venture. The main obstacle was the need to construct
a new bridge over the Missouri River. Although the steam railroads that operated in the region had
bridges over the river, none was willing to grant trackage rights to a new, competing electric system.
However, the construction of the Armour-Swift-Burlington (ASB) Bridge, completed in 1909, changed
the investment climate. The builders of the ASB Bridge also had purchased a large tract of land on the
river's north side with plans of development. They recognized interurban service would complement their
development plans, thus, granted trackage rights on the new bridge. Eastern capitalists (primarily banking
houses) then were persuaded to finance the formation of the railway company and to build the interurban.
The electric rail service encouraged population growth in existing communities along its route. Two new
towns, Glenaire and Northmoor, were established. 1

 The company secured franchises from the counties and cities it would serve with the Platte County Court
 granting a 200-year franchise on September 11, 1911. Under terms of the franchise, the company agreed
 to purchase needed right-of-way from landowners (between 66' and 100' wide) and to build all necessary
 bridges. 2

 The railway's route originated in downtown Kansas City and, after crossing the Missouri River, divided
 into two lines: one to Excelsior Springs, the site of a popular mineral water spa of the era located in Clay
 County; the second ran to St. Joseph, the second largest city in the region. The Excelsior Springs line,
 26.45 miles in length, was completed first, with the first car running on January 21, 1913; the 45.88-mile
 St. Joseph line was opened five months later, on May 5. 3 The total mileage, 72.33, comprised nearly half
 the total number of miles built in Missouri during the height of the interurban construction era ( 1893-
 1915). 4

The company purchased its electric power from the Metropolitan Street Railway Company, Kansas City,
which transmitted the current to the KCCC&SJ transformer station on the north side of the river. From
there, the power was sent to three substations on the two lines and from the substations to the overhead
lines that powered the cars. 5

Passenger stations and shelter stations were built along the line at several locations. A 1927 schedule
listed forty-one stops between Kansas City and St. Joseph; most were at small shelters. Small stations,
operated by local individuals, were located in Platte County at Ferrelview, Keerville, Camden Point and
Dearborn. The train cars also stopped for anyone who flagged them down along the route. 6

The KCCC&SJ ran on regular one-hour schedules from early morning until midnight, the last train carried
passengers back along the St. Joseph route from late movies in downtown Kansas City. The service
attracted a variety of customers, giving them speedy acce&<; to and from the region's major metropolitan
area. For urban workers who lived in the developing suburbs, it provided transportation to jobs in an era
before automobiles became affordable to the working class. Farn1ers used the train to transport produce
to the Kansas City and St. Joseph markets. Students rode it to schools. It also provided a means of
transportation to shopping and entertainment centers of the area. 7

The interurban enjoyed moderate success during its early years of operation, although it never achieved
the kind of financial return that was expected of it by investors. It was the coming of the automobile, the
construction of hard surface roads and the financial effects of a number of lawsuits filed against the
company, however, that caused the company's net earnings to decline in the mid- I 920s. 8 By 1930, with
the onset of the Depression, the company's position worsened and, in September that I
                                                                                       year, it defaulted on
bonds. Two months later, in November, a federal judge placed the railway into a receivership. Soon
after, the receiver filed a petition with the Missouri Public Service Commission (PSC), asking for
permission to discontinue service. The PSC granted that permission on February 12, 1933. A judge in
the U.S. District Court for the Western Division of the Western District then set the date the railway's
operation would end: March 10, 1933. 9 The last cars on both lines ran that day. 10

Several efforts were made to revive the railway, but all failed. One of the most successful attempts was
headed by Ben M. Achtenberg of Kansas City, an attorney who saw the need for keeping the fast rail
connection in operation. While many of those who used the KCCC&SJ expressed great support for its
continuation, there was very little financial support given except by Achtenberg. He succeeded

in obtaining a. certificate of convenience and necessity from the PSC, granted on July 7, 1934 after
hearings were conducted in April that year in Jefferson City. 11 However, Achtenberg was unable to raise
sufficient capital by the time the railway was sold at a court-ordered auction that began on August 21,
1934. The Hymen-Michaels Salvage Company of Chicago offered the winning bid, $112,525 for the
railway's equipment, excluding stations, electric substation structures and rolling stock. 12 Within days,
salvage crews began the removal of the metals from the railway (primarily iron from tracks and bridges
and copper wire from the railroad's electric system.). The St. Joseph line was dismantled first, but the
entire salvage project was completed by the end of 1935. 13

The Bridge

The fowler Creek Bridge is one of the KCCC&SJ Railways' 108 Luten design concrete bridges, built by
the Wyandotte Construction Company from standard engineering plans originally drawn by Daniel Luten. 14
The project's chief engineer, Robert P. Woods, had built Luten arch bridges for similar interurban systems
in other parts of the United States, including in Indiana. He used the same plans to build the KCCC&SJ
In addition to the Luten design concrete arch bridges, six steel bridges were constructed for the line. 15

Located on the project's alignment and profile maps at the 22-mile point, the two-span bridge is among
the reinforced concrete arch structures required for crossing larger waterways. 16 All the railway's bridges
were designed to carry concentrated loads of 100 tons. Both the structure and the deck are of concrete.
The cost of a Luten arch was determined by the formula: I(S+2)(3F+W=10). S=span in feet; F=profile
fill; W=Subgrade width in feet; !=Unity for these prices: cement, $1.25 per yard; sand, $1 per yard; stone,
$1 per yard; lumber, $2 per feet; steel, $1.75 per hundredweight; labor, $2 per day; excavation, $.50 per
yard and superintendent, $.50 per hour. 17

No historic photographs have been found that identify the fowler Creek Bridge. However, there are
photographs identified as KCCC&SJ bridges (but not named as specific sites), that appear to be very
similar to the fowler Creek structure. Photographs published in a book about Daniel Luten arches include
four built for the KCCC&SJ None is the fowler Creek Bridge, but one, located at Liberty on the Excelsior
Springs line, is quite similar. 18 The photos were published as examples of the 108 concrete bridges.

No county road records were found that contain information about specific work projects performed on
the bridge since the time it was converted from a railway to a vehicular structure. The bridge is not
posted. However, signage that denotes it as a single-lane bridge is set at both north and south approaches.
The roadway and bridge deck have asphalt surfaces at the present time.

The Wyandotte Construction Company

The Wyandotte Construction Company was formed in 1910 for the sole purpose of constructing the
KCCC&SJ Railway. Its principals were George Townsend, Walter Kirkpatrick and Ward S. Arnold. The
company is not known to have constructed any other project and ceased operations when its assets were
dissolved in June 1913. 19

The company controlled and oversaw the entire interurban project, subcontracting some of the work. 20
Some of the workers along the St. Joseph line were farmers who needed to subsidize their income because
of a crop failure in 1912. Local workers also were hired in numbers to help with the grading and bridge-
building phases of the project, in part, because of transportation curtailments of the day. Horses and mules
were used for the heaviest work. However, when it was time to lay ties and rails, crews from Kansas City
rode hand-pump cars to the site, carrying with them the materials needed for the day's work. In all, an
estimated 1,500 men worked on the construction project. 21

Daniel Luten

Daniel B. Luten was a nationally-recognized civil engineer of the late nineteenth and early twentieth
centuries. Granted a number of patents for his bridge designs, Luten particularly was known for his work
with reinforced concrete bridges and arches. He is credited with overseeing the construction of at least
12,000 bridges across the U.S. during his career. He also taught, published professional papers and
articles and presented illustrated lectures to professional engineering groups and university engineering
departments. 22

Robert P. Woods

Robert P. Woods was the one individual who is most closely associated with the KCCC&SJ Railway.
As chief engineer, he was responsible for the construction of the railway. Two decades later, he was the
individual who oversaw its operations cease, auctioned and dismantled. Before he came to Kansas City,
Woods had overseen the construction of similar interurban systems in other parts of the country and had
worked on various other types of engineering projects in the U.S. as well as in Mexico. He was hired by
the KCCC&SJ Railway Company as the project's chief engineer in 1910. In 1920, he was named its
general manager. Woods was named the railway company's receiver by a federal judge in 1930. After
the KCCC&SJ was auctioned, Woods became Street Railway Commissioner of Kansas City, Missouri. 23

The Interurban Road

After the railway was dismantled in Platte County, the ties, at least one iron bridge and the concrete
bridges and culverts were left behind. The Platte County Court purchased the iron bridge from the salvage
company in March 1935.24 No record has been found of county purchase or acceptance of the fowler
Creek Bridge or of any of the concrete bridges and culverts on the road. County court records do show
however, that, between 1935-41, petitions were filed by landowners in the area who asked the county to
convert the railway road into a farm-to-market route which is today locally known as the Interurban Road.
By early 1935, part of the road project had begun. 25 The bridges along the 16.5 mile route were converted
in that project, but exact dates of the various phases of construction, most specifically for the fowler Creek
Bridge and its immediate area, have not been determined.

The Project

Housing development has increased over the years along the Interurban Road which now carries more
traffic than in previous years. The Jowler Creek Bridge has been designated as a single-lane bridge in
recent years.

In an update to the county commission in 1993, the county engineer recommended the bridge be replaced
with a modern structure wide enough for two lanes of traffic. 26
