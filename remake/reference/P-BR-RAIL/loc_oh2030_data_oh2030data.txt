Significance:                The Ohio History Connection, which serves as the State Historic
                             Preservation Office, determined the NS bridge over Stony Creek
                             (Bridge N-647.74) eligible for listing in the National Register of
                             Historic Places in May 2020. The structure is significant at the local
                             level under Criteria A and C. The structure is a representative example
                             of a series of brick, stone, and concrete arches erected in the late
                             nineteenth (ca. 1888) and early twentieth century (1909 and 1934).
                             The SV likely built the oldest component of Bridge N-647.74, a stone
                             arch culvert, while completing improvements to the line in the late
                             1880s. The improvements campaign included the replacement of
                             wooden bridges with new iron or stone structures along the company’s
                             entire alignment from Portsmouth to Columbus, Ohio.2 Subsequent
                             additions—the west expansion of the inlet in 1909 with a second
                             masonry arch and the east expansion of the outlet in 1934—occurred
                             under the N&W’s ownership of the bridge (July 1890–1981).

                             In the late nineteenth century, prior to the widespread use of reinforced
                             concrete, railroad companies typically constructed arch bridges and
                             culverts across the United States of stone, a material known for its
                             durability. Often, stone arch bridges were built in areas where quality
                             stone was readily available, such as in Waverly, Pike County, Ohio,
                             approximately 9 miles south of the bridge site. 3 No sources explicitly
                             state that Waverly stone was used in the construction of Bridge N-
                             647.74, but given the bridge’s proximity to the source it is a possibility
                             local stone was used. The concrete extensions of the bridge
                             demonstrate typical modifications railroad companies made to stone
                             structures as part of improvements and/or repairs across the nation in
                             the early twentieth century. Of the stone arch bridges constructed in
                             the United States during the late eighteenth and early nineteenth

Description:                    The bridge at Milepost N-647.74 consists of three adjoining culverts
                                built ca. 1888–1934. The bridge carries a single mainline track of the
                                NS railroad across Stony Creek in Franklin Township, Ross County.
                                The NS railroad runs northwest to southeast at the site of the bridge,
                                which is situated in a narrow, forested corridor in a rural part of the
                                county. Stony Creek flows west to east at the bridge site and then
                                immediately into the Scioto River to the east. Three Locks Road
                                (County Highway 205) makes a bend to the west approximately 0.10
                                mile southwest of Bridge N-647.74 and intersects with Stoney Creek
                                Road (County Highway 199) approximately 0.20 mile southwest of the
                                bridge.

                                The bridge measures approximately 64′ in length from the face of the
                                inlet headwall (west elevation) to the face of the current outlet
                                headwall (east elevation); the wing walls on the east side of the
                                structure extend an additional 26′-6″ east of the outlet wall. The oldest
                                of the bridge components is a 16′ long stone arch built by the SV ca.
                                1888, which was designed to carry a single track. In 1909, the N&W
                                added a second arch to the west (inlet) side of the 16′ long arch, to
                                allow for double tracking over the crossing. The third component of
                                the current structure is a concrete box culvert east of the ca. 1888 arch,
                                added in 1934. In 1934, when N&W completed the addition of the
                                concrete box culvert, the double tracks were shifted to the east. The
                                second track was removed sometime after 1960, returning the
                                alignment to a single track over Bridge N-647.74. The single track lies
                                on a graded slope approximately 4′ to 6′ above the headwall of the
                                bridge.

                                1875, and the line was extended south to Chillicothe by July 1876. The

                                 Chillicothe, the present-day county seat of Ross County, was platted in
                                 August 1796, the same year that Zane’s Trace, the earliest road cut
                                 through the area, reached the town. 6 The land comprising present-day
                                 Ross County was initially a part of Adams County, which was
                                 established in July 1797. In August 1798, Ross County was created. 7
                                 In May 1800, Congress named Chillicothe the seat of the territorial
                                 government. In early 1803, Ohio achieved statehood, and Chillicothe
                                 was named the state capital. The state capitol moved to Zanesville
                                 from 1810–1812, then returned to Chillicothe briefly before moving to
                                 Columbus permanently in 1816. Franklin Township was established in
                                 eastern Ross County on September 9, 1806, and its boundaries were
                                 adjusted to their present configuration in 1814. 8

                                 Following statehood, agriculture quickly became the center of the
                                 regional economy, for the soils of the Scioto River valley were ideal
                                 for the cultivation of staple crops such as corn and wheat. Flatboats
                                 first traveled down the Scioto River from Chillicothe to New Orleans,
                                 Louisiana, in 1803, transporting grain, flour, and livestock. Flatboats
                                 also traveled north to Circleville, Ohio, and Columbus carrying
                                 agricultural products. The first cattle drive took place in 1804 and
                                 traveled east from the Scioto Valley over the Allegheny Mountain
                                 Range to Baltimore, Maryland. 9 Over the following decades, cattle of
                                 the Scioto Valley became highly sought after, and the local cattle
                                 industry thrived as a result.

                                 Prior to the arrival of rail transit, canal construction was the first large-
                                 scale infrastructure project in Ohio. The state’s General Assembly
                                 passed the first act related to canal construction in 1821. The Ohio and
                                 Erie Canal, completed 1825–1832, traveled through Ross County, and
                                 extended through the eastern portion Franklin Township before turning
                                 southwest to cross into Pike County. The State Dam across the Scioto

                             In the 1850s, railroads expanded across the state and quickly began
                             posing competition to the existing canal system. After 1851, the state’s
                             canal system struggled to break even, under both state ownership and a
                             brief period of operation by private lessees in the 1870s. During the
                             same period, total rail mileage in the state grew from approximately
                             300 miles in 1850 to over 5,600 miles in 1880.11

                             In 1877, the SV began to extend its line south from Chillicothe. The
                             SV ran parallel to the Ohio and Erie Canal on the east side of Franklin
                             Township. A railroad station known as both “State Dam” and “Three
                             Locks” was located there. Other SV stations in the township in 1880
                             included Stony Creek, the county bridge, and Higby. In 1880, Higby
                             was the only station in the township without a station house, though a
                             post office was located in the community. 12 In 1881, the SV was
                             completed to Coal Grove, Ohio, slightly east of Portsmouth, providing
                             uninterrupted service between the Ohio River and Columbus. 13 The
                             SV primarily transported agricultural products of the Scioto Valley,
                             and the extension to Coal Grove provided access to iron manufacturing
                             and lumber traffic along the Ohio River.

                             In February 1890, the Scioto Valley and New England Railroad
                             (SV&NE) acquired the SV, and, a few months later, in July 1890, the
                             N&W acquired the SV&NE. The N&W named Robinson
                             superintendent of the “Scioto Division,” the name ascribed to the
                             original SV route within the N&W system. The N&W’s origins date to
                             1836, when the City Point Railroad was chartered in Virginia. Over
                             the next forty-four years, the future N&W took shape through over 200
                             mergers. Those companies were consolidated under the Norfolk &
                             Petersburg Railroad (N&P) by the 1860s. Following the Civil War, the
                             N&P merged with two smaller railroads—the Southside and the
                             Virginia & Tennessee—to form the Atlantic, Mississippi & Ohio
                             Railroad (AM&O). By 1881, the AM&O had gone bankrupt, and E.
                             W. Clark & Company, a bank in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania,
                             purchased the N&P and renamed it the N&W. Frederick J. Kimball, a
                             partner in E. W. Clark & Company, took leadership of the N&W and
                             promptly merged it with the Shenandoah Valley Railroad, which ran
                             between Hagerstown, Maryland, and Roanoke, Virginia.

                             Kimball, who had an interest in geology, was reportedly the catalyst
                             behind the N&W opening the Pocahontas Coalfields in Virginia and
                             West Virginia, and led the charge to build a rail connection with the
                             SV&NE (the SV’s “Ohio Extension”) to provide a route to
                             Columbus. 16 The N&W completed its Ohio Extension in 1892,
                             providing service to Columbus. In 1901, the N&W achieved service to

                              nationwide, roadway improvements, and the construction of highways

                              Chillicothe. The route of present-day U.S. Highway 23 was one such

                              Trail. U.S. Highway 23 serves as the primary north-south road through
                              the county, running from Portsmouth north to Columbus and on to
                              Toledo, Ohio. U.S. Highways 35 and 50 run northwest to southeast,
                              and east to west, respectively, through Chillicothe. Beginning in the

                                 While the N&W had historically traversed primarily rural areas of the
                                 county, by the mid twentieth century small communities that once
                                 were the site of railroad stations, such as Higby, Pride, and Three
                                 Locks, no longer served as stops. Though small pockets of residential
                                 development are located near Bridge N-647.74, vestiges of the canal
                                 and railroad activity of earlier times are limited. The Pride School
                                 closed in the late 1960s but remained extant until 2001 when a fire
                                 destroyed it. 21 By the late 1990s, no towns or established businesses
                                 were present in Franklin Township.22

                                 As railroad mergers became increasingly common in the 1970s, the
                                 prospect of a merger between the N&W and Southern Railway arose.
                                 In 1982, the Interstate Commerce Commission approved the
                                 acquisition of the two lines by the newly formed Norfolk Southern
                                 Corporation. The NS and Southern continued operating under their
                                 historic names until 1990, when the N&W became a subsidiary of the
                                 Southern. In 1999, the system grew again after the NS and CSX
                                 purchased and split the Conrail system. As of 2005, the NS system
                                 totaled over 21,100 route miles of railway in twenty-two states;
                                 Washington, D.C.; and Ontario, Canada.23 NS continues to operate the
                                 former SV alignment through Ross County.

                                 Bridge N-647.74, in its current configuration, has been in continuous
                                 operation since 1934. In the late-2010s, structural inspectors identified
                                 stone failure within the arch culvert, including missing and damaged
                                 stones in the arch ring and interior, and across the inlet (west)
                                 elevation. Cracking and spalling are present on the concrete
                                 components of the structure. Project engineers have advised that most
                                 of the 100 year old stone likely would not survive the deconstruction
                                 and reconstruction necessary for rehabilitation. To stabilize the stone
                                 and brick arch culverts and repair the concrete box extension, the NS
                                 proposes installation of a corrugated steel arch liner with a reinforced
                                 concrete base that extends the width of the existing bridge, providing
                                 support for all three components. As part of the proposed project,
                                 concrete encasements would be constructed at the inlet and outlet
