# Town-Siting Relative to Water — General Principles

(This is a cross-check/context file — general geomorphological and planning principles only. Per-city population-tier siting details are covered by other research agents' work; this file doesn't duplicate that.)

## Outside-of-bend vs. inside-of-bend siting

The cut-bank/point-bar asymmetry described in meander_geometry.md has direct town-siting consequences that recur across real Midwestern river towns:

- **The outer bank (cut-bank side) of a bend** is typically higher, firmer ground — the river has spent centuries eroding down into it, which tends to leave a modest bluff or at least a distinctly higher, better-drained bank than the inside of the bend. It's also where the water is naturally **deepest close to shore**, which historically mattered enormously for steamboat landings, mill races, and any use that wanted deep water right at the bank without needing a long approach channel. This combination (solid ground + deep water access) is why historic river towns disproportionately sit on the outside of bends.
- **The inner bank (point-bar side)** is low, sandy/gravelly, frequently reworked by the river, and floods first and worst — historically used for farmland, pasture, or left wild, rarely built on for permanent structures. In a modern context this is exactly the kind of ground a floodplain map/zoning ordinance would flag as unbuildable or park-only.

## Floodplain avoidance in building placement

- The operative real-world concept is the **Base Flood Elevation (BFE)** — the elevation the 100-year flood is expected to reach — with building codes typically requiring a structure's lowest floor to sit at or above BFE plus a **freeboard margin**, commonly on the order of **1–3 ft** in many U.S. jurisdictions (this is general regulatory practice, not something narrowly sourced in this pass — treat as a planning convention rather than a single hard citation).
- The **ordinary high-water mark (OHWM)** is the legal/physical reference line for where a waterbody's regular influence ends — construction setbacks are typically measured from this line, not from the water's edge at low flow, which is why buildings along a real river/lake often look "set back" from the water by a visible margin of open ground, parkland, or a levee even in dry conditions.
- **Levee setback practice** (modern precedent, e.g. Orting, WA and the Atchison County / Missouri River levee districts): rather than levee the river tight against its historic channel, modern flood engineering increasingly moves the levee **inland**, giving the river room to spread out in its natural floodplain during floods, and moving vulnerable structures out of that reclaimed floodway. The general lesson for town-siting logic: **older/legacy development sits close to the water (sometimes too close), while intentional or redeveloped areas set back further and use the reclaimed near-water land as parkland or floodable green space.**

## Industrial-legacy waterfronts vs. amenity waterfronts

Real pattern, repeated across many Midwestern and American river towns:

1. **Industrial-legacy downtown waterfront**: the riverfront was historically given over to mills, rail yards, warehouses, and shipping because water access was the primary transportation/power asset. This produces a waterfront lined with large utilitarian structures, rail corridors running parallel to the bank, and the town's "nice" residential fabric set back from and often turned away from the water.
2. **Amenity/park waterfront**: post-industrial (or newer-planned) towns reclaim or design the waterfront as a park, walking path, and public gathering space, with the town's better real estate deliberately fronting onto it. This is the now-common redevelopment arc — old industrial riverfronts (rail yards, mills, warehouses) get cleared and rebuilt as parks and mixed-use residential once the industrial use declines.

**Design-relevant conclusion:** for a station map, a plausible downtown can pick either archetype (or show the transition — e.g., a working industrial/rail stretch of riverfront near a bridge crossing, transitioning into a reclaimed park stretch nearer the lake), but should **not** show expensive/dense buildings on the low, inside-of-bend point-bar ground, and should generally site its oldest/densest core on outside-of-bend high ground near the river's deepest, straightest reach (the classic "landing" spot).
