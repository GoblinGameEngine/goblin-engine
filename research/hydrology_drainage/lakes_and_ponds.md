# Lakes That Straddle a River, and Tributary-Fed Ponds

## Lakes formed by a river widening (the key real-world precedent)

**Illinois River / Peoria Lake** is the best available real-world match for "a lake that straddles the river": Peoria Lake is a natural widening of the Illinois River itself (not an off-channel lake connected by a short link) — the river visibly narrows into a defined channel both upstream and downstream of it, flows in at one end and continues out the other, and in between it fans out to nearly a mile wide. This is exactly the geometry the design brief wants: the *same* watercourse, just locally wide, with clear inlet/outlet necking.

**Mill ponds / small reservoir lakes** (the more common small-scale version of the same idea — a dam, natural sill, or just a broad low spot causing the river to pool) give concrete length:width ratios:

| Lake | Length | Width | L:W ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Palgrave (Mill) Pond | 420 m | 200 m | ~2.1:1 |
| Powder Mill Pond | 2.6 mi (4.2 km) | 0.5 mi (0.8 km) | ~5.2:1 |
| Arlington Mill Reservoir | 1.7 mi (2.7 km) | 0.4 mi (0.64 km) | ~4.3:1 |

**Pattern:** real river-widening lakes cluster around **L:W ≈ 2:1 to 5:1** — elongated along the river's line of travel, not round or wide-relative-to-length. They're visibly "a fat spot in the river," not a separate round lake the river happens to pass near.

**How the river visibly enters/exits:** at both the inlet and outlet, the shoreline pinches back down toward channel width over a fairly short distance (tens to a couple hundred meters), so the transition from "river" to "lake" and back reads clearly on a map — there's a recognizable narrows at each end, not a gradual, ambiguous blend.

**Depth/shoreline pattern:** these widened-river lakes are characteristically **shallow and irregular-shored** compared to a glacial or tectonic lake — depth is set by the original valley floor plus whatever impoundment raised the water level, shorelines tend to have embayments where former floodplain lowlands got flooded, and there's often a old streambed / deepest-channel trace still visible as a subtly deeper line running down the long axis (the "ghost" of the original river channel through the lake).

## Ponds fed by tributary creeks

Small ponds dotting a river's floodplain fall into two conceptual categories that a generator can blend:

1. **Oxbow / cutoff-remnant ponds** — crescent-shaped, sited directly adjacent to the main channel (often nearly touching it), a direct leftover of a former meander loop. These don't need a "tributary" per se — they're relics of the river itself, may have a very short, marshy connecting channel, or none at all if fully cut off.
2. **Tributary-fed ponds** — round-to-oval ponds sited back in the floodplain or on a side drainage, connected to the main river by a genuine small creek. This matches the brief's requirement of "ponds that connect via tributaries."

**Small tributary creek dimensions** (concrete real examples, Pennsylvania headwater creeks connecting ponds to a larger system): typically **~6–20 ft (1.8–6 m) wide**, with depth commonly under 2 ft in the shallow marshy sections and increasing somewhat near a pond outlet. These are genuinely small — visually more like a ditch/brook than a river.

**Pond size relative to drainage area:** the rule-of-thumb from farm-pond design is that **10–15 acres of contributing drainage area are needed to reliably fill and sustain 1 acre of pond surface**. This ratio is a useful sanity check for placement (ponds should visually sit in a locally low point with an implied larger catchment, not perched with no plausible water source) even though a game map won't literally simulate the watershed.

**Density of ponds/oxbows along a floodplain:** precise per-kilometer density figures were not found in a clean citable form (this varies hugely by river age, floodplain width, and how recently the channel has been actively migrating — e.g. the Mississippi Delta and Amazon floodplains are described qualitatively as having oxbows/scroll ponds covering a notable fraction, cited informally as up to roughly a quarter of floodplain area in some studied reaches, but that's an area fraction, not a count-per-km). Treat any specific "N ponds per km" figure as a design choice rather than a cited fact — see the design-notes file for the recommended number for this station.
