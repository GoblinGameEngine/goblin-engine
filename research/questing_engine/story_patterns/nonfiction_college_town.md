# Nonfiction Story Patterns — College Town

Archetype recap (`_demographic_archetypes.md` §5): population and economy dominated by a resident
college/university; needs at least small-town scale (6,000+) to support a real campus. Population
tracks enrollment rather than local economy (-2% to +10%). Income varies widely ($50k-$87k) because
faculty/staff households average against low-income student households, so median income
under-describes the town. Defining signal: youngest median age of any archetype (24-34). Low
homeownership (39-58%, renter-heavy). Poverty artificially elevated (16-29%) as a statistical
artifact of counting low/no-income students as separate poverty-line households — **not** economic
distress; do not treat this as a "declining town" signal. Educational Services dominant employer
(19-29% of workforce), Health Care typically #2. Bachelor's+ high (37%+). Civic-anchor pattern: a
college green functioning as shared town/campus square (Oberlin's Tappan Square) is a genuine
third downtown-organization preset. Examples: Mankato MN, Cape Girardeau MO, Bowling Green OH,
Oberlin OH, Holland MI (hybrid with archetype 6).

---

## 1. Town-gown conflict over noise, housing, and tax-exempt land
**Sources:** "Town and gown" is an established, centuries-old term of art for this conflict
(traceable to incidents like Oxford's 1355 St. Scholastica Day riot as the historical extreme
case); the modern recurring version documents off-campus-party noise complaints, "studentification"
converting single-family housing stock to student rentals and displacing longtime residents while
raising prices, disputes over university property-tax exemption straining municipal budgets even
as the university claims economic-impact benefits, parking/traffic friction from campus expansion,
and recurring jurisdictional disputes between campus police and municipal police.
**Why it recurs here:** this conflict is close to definitional for the archetype — a town whose
population and economy are "dominated by" an institution with its own separate governance,
calendar, and priorities will structurally generate friction over whose interests the town serves.
**Locations:** `bar_tavern`, residential (`duplex`, `apartment_small`, `rowhouse_townhouse`).

## 2. Small-college financial distress and closure
**Sources:** Iowa Wesleyan University (Mount Pleasant, Iowa — note: Mount Pleasant is also a named
example in the Stable Ag/Manufacturing and Stable County-Seat archetypes, an explicit cross-
archetype case worth flagging) closed at the end of the 2022-23 academic year after 181 years of
operation, when Iowa's governor determined a proposed $12 million state loan "would not be
feasible" — a terminal outcome even after surviving an earlier 2018 closure threat via alumni
donations and a $26 million USDA loan; the Mount Pleasant school district subsequently bought the
campus's central grounds and practice fields for ~$1.2 million. This is one instance of a broader,
well-documented national wave of small private-college closures (covered continuously by *The
Chronicle of Higher Education* and *The Hechinger Report*) driven by demographic "enrollment
cliff" pressure and unsustainable tuition-discount models.
**Why it recurs here:** because Educational Services is this archetype's dominant employer at
19-29% of the workforce (the highest single-sector concentration of any archetype besides severe
company-towns), a college's closure is not just an institutional story — it is functionally
equivalent to the single-factory-closure story in Rust-Belt or ag/manufacturing towns, but hitting
a much larger share of the local economy at once.
**Locations:** `school` (nearest catalog analog), `library`.

## 3. Student-housing development fight and rent-driven displacement
**Sources:** Documented as part of the broader town-gown literature (§1) under the specific term
"studentification" — conversion of formerly owner-occupied or family-rental housing stock into
student rentals, typically raising per-unit rents (multiple students splitting one lease can
outbid a single family) and displacing longtime lower-income or elderly residents who are not
students.
**Why it recurs here:** the archetype's structurally low homeownership (39-58%) reflects a rental
market shaped by student demand; new student-housing development (or conversion of existing
housing) is a recurring zoning-and-city-council flashpoint distinct from the exurban subdivision
fight because the buyer/renter pressure here is calendar-driven (academic year) rather than
permanent household formation.
**Locations:** residential (`apartment_small`, `apartment_large`, `duplex`).

## 4. Athletics-driven seasonal local economy
**Sources:** A well-documented pattern in sports-business and regional-economics journalism —
football/basketball game-day tourism creates a seasonal revenue spike for local bars, restaurants,
and hotels in college towns, sharply different week-to-week from the steady-state local economy,
generating its own recurring "how local businesses plan around the football schedule" feature-
story genre.
**Why it recurs here:** the archetype's population is explicitly tied to "enrollment rather than
the local economy," and athletics season is the most visible periodic amplifier of that population
swing onto the local commercial economy.
**Locations:** `bar_tavern`, `hotel_inn_small`, `diner_cafe`, `restaurant`.

## 5. Campus free-speech/political controversy spilling into town politics
**Sources:** A recurring national-news genre (campus speaker disinvitations, protest
controversies, faculty free-speech disputes) that periodically pulls the host town's local
government and residents into a dispute that originated entirely within university governance —
well documented across dozens of individual campus incidents covered by national higher-education
press (*Inside Higher Ed*, *Chronicle of Higher Education*) with local-town ripple coverage.
**Why it recurs here:** because the college dominates both the town's identity and its economy,
national attention on campus controversies inevitably becomes local news, and town officials are
frequently asked to weigh in on matters technically outside municipal jurisdiction.
**Locations:** `town_hall`, `library`.

## 6. Faculty/student economic divide obscured by a misleading median
**Sources:** `_demographic_archetypes.md` itself flags this directly: median household income
($50k-$87k) "under-describes the town" because it averages high-earning faculty/staff households
against near-zero-income student households — a statistical-methodology point echoed in general
Census-methodology critiques of college-town data (a recurring caveat in any serious local
economic-development or housing-affordability report about a college town).
**Why it recurs here:** this is the archetype's own documented false-positive risk (alongside the
"artificially elevated poverty rate" warning) — a generator or a researcher treating this
archetype's income number at face value will misread the town's actual economic texture.
**Locations:** residential contrast (`victorian_queen_anne`/`colonial_revival` faculty housing vs.
`apartment_small`/`duplex` student housing).

## 7. International/exchange-student-driven small ethnic commercial scene
**Sources:** A minor but recurring trend-piece genre in college-town local press — small ethnic
grocery stores or restaurants opening to serve a graduate/international-student population,
occasionally covered as a "surprising diversity in a small Midwest town" human-interest angle.
**Why it recurs here:** the archetype's high Bachelor's+/graduate-education concentration and
university-driven demographic mix (distinct from every other archetype) is the only one in this
set that reliably produces this kind of small international-population-driven commercial niche.
**Locations:** `small_grocery`, `restaurant`.

## 8. Academic-calendar seasonal population swing (mini-resort-town effect)
**Sources:** Not a single iconic source, but a structurally documented pattern in college-town
economic-development literature — summer/break population crashes (when students leave) followed
by fall/spring surges, producing a boom-bust local-business cycle structurally similar to a lake-
resort town's tourist season (see `nonfiction_lake_resort.md` §6) but driven by the academic
calendar rather than weather/season.
**Why it recurs here:** population tracking "enrollment rather than the local economy" (the
archetype's own defining trend description) means the town literally empties out and refills on a
predictable annual cycle, unlike any other archetype except the resort towns.
**Locations:** `diner_cafe`, `bar_tavern`, `small_grocery`.
