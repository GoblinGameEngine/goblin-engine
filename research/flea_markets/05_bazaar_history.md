# The Bazaar and the Periodic Market: A Research History for Game Design

**Method and reliability.** I verified key facts with web searches and page reads on 2026-10-15. Bracketed numbers point to the source list at the end. Where sources disagree, both versions are given. Points that come only from general scholarly background, and that I did not re-verify this session, are marked "(background)". Treat those as leads to check, not settled fact. 

---

## 1. Words: "bazaar", "souk" and their relatives

- **Bazaar.**
  - The usual derivation chain is New Persian *bāzār*, from Middle Persian *wāzār*, from Old Persian *vāčar*, from a reconstructed Proto-Indo-Iranian *wahā-čarana* [1].
  - Other reconstructions are offered, such as Old Iranian *vāčā-zāra-*, "place of prices". The deeper root is disputed [1].
  - In Persian the word meant a town's public market district, not one event [2].
  - It spread widely. Ottoman Turkish has *pazar*, also the word for "Sunday" (market day). Hindi-Urdu has *bāzār*. Malay and Indonesian have *pasar*, as in *pasar malam*, "night market" [40].
- **Souk (sūq).**
  - Arabic *sūq* is a loan from Aramaic *šūqā*, which was itself borrowed from Akkadian *sūqu*, meaning "street" or "market" [2].
  - So the Arabic word goes back to the language of the Old Assyrian merchants described in section 2. The core sense is the street as market, the shop-lined thoroughfare.
  - Hebrew *shuk* is a cognate [2].
- **Related terms used below.**
  - *Khan / han*: inn or caravanserai, from Middle Persian *xānag*, "house" [18].
  - *Funduq*: Maghreb inn-warehouse, ultimately from Greek *pandocheion* (background) [18].
  - *Wikala*: Egyptian urban caravanserai, literally "agency" [18].
  - *Qaysariyya / qeysarieh / kissaria*: a lockable luxury market hall. The name comes from "Caesar's" market (background) [2].
  - *Bedesten*: Ottoman domed strong-hall for valuable goods [5].
  - *Timcheh*: Persian domed hall devoted to a single trade [8].
  - *Sarai / sara*: courtyard building inside the bazaar [8].
  - *Arasta*: a row of shops of one trade, often attached to a mosque complex (background).

## 2. The Ancient Near East

### 2.1 Mesopotamia and the *kārum*

- In Akkadian, *kārum* first meant "quay" or "harbour". It came to mean the merchants' quarter and the merchants' corporate body in a city [3].
- Mesopotamian cities had both palace and temple economies and private trade. The *kārum* was where merchants clustered, settled disputes, and dealt with the ruler as a body (background, see Larsen [63]).

### 2.2 Kanesh (Kültepe): the first well-documented merchant colony

- **Place.** Kültepe, ancient Kanesh, lies near modern Kayseri in central Anatolia.
- **Who and what.** In roughly 1950–1840 BCE, merchants from the city-state of Ashur on the Tigris ran a network of trading settlements across Anatolia. Kanesh was the hub [3].
  - The commercial settlement in the plain below the native Anatolian citadel is called "the kārum".
  - The Assyrians lived there as a semi-autonomous quarter beside the local population [3].
- **Archives.** More than 23,000 clay tablets from about seventy household archives were excavated, mostly from the kārum [3]. They survive because a fire destroyed the lower town about 1836 BCE [3].
- **Trade pattern.** Donkey caravans brought tin and woollen textiles from Ashur into Anatolia. The merchants exchanged them for silver and gold, which went back to Ashur. They also took part in the copper trade inside Anatolia [3][4].
- **Record-keeping.** The merchants carried Mesopotamian tools with them: simplified cuneiform, clay tablets in sealed clay envelopes, and cylinder seals. They used these to track loans, partnerships, lawsuits and letters home [4].
  - One Met Museum "caravan account" tablet lists silver invested in tin and textiles, and in lapis lazuli from Afghanistan. It also lists caravan expenses such as clothing [4].
- **Institutional features (background, from Larsen [63] and Veenhof).**
  - The kārum Kanesh had its own assembly ("the kārum, small and great") that acted as a court.
  - The colony system was hierarchical. Larger *kārum* stations sat above smaller trading posts called *wabartum*.
  - Long-term partnerships pooled capital in a joint fund (*naruqqum*, "sack"). Investors financed a travelling merchant for years.
  - Treaties with local Anatolian rulers set tolls, a right of pre-emption on goods, and protection on the roads.
  - Families split between Ashur and Anatolia. Wives in Ashur often produced the textiles and managed affairs at home.
- **Design relevance.** Kanesh is a template for a *merchant enclave inside a host city*. It has its own court, a written credit culture, a treaty-based toll regime, and a fixed long-distance caravan rhythm.

### 2.3 Persian and pre-Islamic Arabian markets

- Market overseers existed in Parthian and Sasanian Iran, before Islam [20].
- In pre-Islamic Arabia, the great market was a seasonal fair, not a permanent street.
  - **Sūq ʿUkāẓ**, between Mecca and Ṭāʾif, met yearly in the sacred month of Dhū al-Qaʿda. It was famous for its poetry contests as well as its trade [2].
  - **Suq Hajar** in Bahrain was known for dates, and **Suq ʿAdan** for spices and perfumes [2].
  - Neutrality from tribal feuds was declared while these seasonal markets ran, so exchange could happen safely [2]. This is a "market truce", directly usable in a game.

## 3. The great urban bazaars

### 3.1 The Grand Bazaar of Istanbul (Kapalıçarşı, "covered market")

- **Founding.**
  - Construction of the core began in winter 1455/56 under Mehmed II, shortly after the 1453 conquest [5].
  - The first bedesten was finished in winter 1460/61. Its rents were endowed to the waqf (pious endowment) of the Hagia Sophia, by then a mosque [5].
  - The bazaar was a revenue machine for a religious foundation from the start.
- **The two bedestens.**
  - The **Cevahir** ("jewels"), also called the İç ("inner") or Old Bedesten, measures about 43.3 × 29.5 m. It has four gates and 44 cellar vaults, and held the most precious goods [5].
  - The **Sandal Bedesten** measures about 40.2 × 42.2 m with 20 bays. It was the centre of the silk trade and later an auction house, mainly for carpets [5].
  - A bedesten worked as a strongroom. Merchants and private people stored valuables there under guard. In the Ottoman period it functioned as something like a safe-deposit bank (background).
- **Growth and disasters.**
  - The bazaar expanded greatly under Süleyman in the 16th century.
  - Fires struck from 1515 on. A severe fire in 1701 led to rebuilding in 1730–31.
  - Earthquakes hit in 1766 and 1894. The 1894 quake forced a major restoration.
  - Further fires came in 1943 and 1954, with restoration finished in 1959 [5].
- **Scale.** Shop counts vary by source and date.
  - Evliya Çelebi counted about 3,000 shops around 1638.
  - An 1890 survey counted 4,399 active shops.
  - Modern estimates run from about 3,000 to 4,000, with no official statistics [5]. Travel sources claim up to 5,000–6,000 [5]. The "about 4,000" figure is reasonable.
  - In the 17th century there were 67 streets, each named for a trade. There are 61 covered streets today [5].
  - An 1890 survey counted 24 hans. After the 1894 repairs, nine remained inside the roofed structure [5].
  - About 26,000 people work there, and it sees 250,000 to 400,000 visitors a day [5].
- **Gates and night.**
  - Eighteen gates were opened in the morning and closed in the evening.
  - Entry at night required an imperial edict. The only official night opening was in 1867, for Sultan Abdülaziz's return from Egypt [5].
  - Today it opens 9:00–19:00 and closes on Sundays [5].
  - The closed-at-night, gated "city inside the city" is a strong design hook.
- **Guild organisation.**
  - Each trade formed a guild that controlled entry by co-optation: a member inherited a place or bought a retiring member's place.
  - Each guild had a **kethüda** (chief, appointed by the kadı) and a **yiğitbaşı** (deputy), plus an assembly of elders [5][59].
  - Guilds were abolished in 1913 and replaced by a merchants' association [5].

### 3.2 Tabriz Historic Bazaar Complex (UNESCO, 2010)

- **What it is.** A set of interconnected covered brick structures with commercial, social, educational and religious functions. It sits on one of the busiest east-west trade routes [6].
- **Status.** Protected nationally from 1975 and inscribed as a World Heritage Site in 2010 under criterion (iv). UNESCO calls it an outstanding example of an "integrated multi-functional urban complex" shaped by commerce [6].
- **Size.** A university source gives about 27 ha of complex and more than 5.5 km of covered bazaars. The inscribed area plus buffer zone is about 104 ha [6].
- **Unverified claim.** Some secondary sources call it the largest covered bazaar in the world [6].
- **Dating.** UNESCO's summary text says Tabriz became "the Safavid capital in the 13th century". That is internally confused: the Safavids ruled from 1501. Tabriz was an Ilkhanid capital in the 13th century and the first Safavid capital in the 16th [6].
- **Building types (background).** Tabriz's bazaar is the standard reference for the full Persian set:
  - *rasteh*: linear vaulted shop-street.
  - *rasteh-bazaar*: main spine.
  - *timcheh*: domed single-trade hall. The Muzaffariyeh timcheh, for carpets, is the best known.
  - *sara / caravanserai*: courtyard building for wholesale and storage.
  - *dalan*: covered passage.
  - Mosques, madrasas and bathhouses woven into the fabric.

### 3.3 Isfahan: the Grand Bazaar and the Qeysarieh

- The bazaar is a vaulted street of about 2 km. It links the old Seljuk core around the Friday (Jāmeh) Mosque to Shah Abbas I's new Naqsh-e Jahān square [7].
- Its oldest parts, near the mosque, date to the 11th-century Seljuk era [7].
- The main Safavid entrance is the **Qeysarieh gate** on the north side of Naqsh-e Jahān. Dates for the gate run from 1611–29 to 1620–38 in different sources [7][8].
- The square is about 500–520 m long and about 165 m wide. It was itself a market, polo ground and parade ground, ringed by shops [7].
- Inside the bazaar are lanes, madrasas, caravanserais and **timchehs**. Lonely Planet defines a timcheh as a domed hall or arcaded centre devoted to one trade, such as carpets or copper [8].
- This is a clear historical case of a ruler *building a new planned market district* and stitching it onto an organically grown old one.

### 3.4 Kashan

- Kashan's bazaar is a vaulted rasteh lit by domes with circular skylights [10].
- Parts were damaged by an earthquake and rebuilt under the Qajar Fath Ali Shah [10].
- Its showpiece is the **Timcheh-ye Amin od-Dowleh**, a 19th-century Qajar-period domed hall of brick with muqarnas vaulting [9].
  - A comparative study notes that the Kashan timchehs use three domes with one prominent central opening for light and ventilation [9].
  - A widely repeated 1868 date could not be confirmed.
- Kashan is the best visual reference for the *skylight dome* (section 9).

### 3.5 Aleppo, Souk al-Madina

- Aleppo's covered souk is described as the largest historic covered market in the world, roughly 13 km of lanes, within the UNESCO-listed Ancient City of Aleppo [2]. The 13 km figure is widely quoted but I found no primary source for it.
- **Khan al-Gumruk** (the customs khan) was built in 1574. It was the largest khan in Aleppo, a textile centre with more than 50 stores [11].
- In September 2012 fighting set the souk on fire. One observer dates the burning to 29 September 2012 [12].
- UNESCO estimated about 60% of the Old City severely damaged and 30% destroyed [11]. Reconstruction began with Khan al-Gumruk, the copper market and Souq al-Saqatiyah [11].
- The pattern of *one great customs khan* where foreign cloth was taxed and stored is a ready-made game institution.

### 3.6 Khan el-Khalili, Cairo

- The name first meant one building. Between 1382 and 1389, under the Mamluk sultan Barquq, his master of the stables **Jaharkas al-Khalili** built a caravanserai there [13][14].
- He built it on the site of the Fatimid caliphs' mausoleum, the "Saffron Tomb" (Turbat al-Zaʿfarān), which he demolished [14].
- Sultan **al-Ghuri** (r. 1501–1516) tore down al-Khalili's khan and rebuilt the area about 1511 [14]. His separate **Wikala al-Ghuri** dates to about 1504–05 [14][18].
- Cairo's *wikālas* combined storage and trading on the ground floor with *rabʿ* rental apartments above. That gave two income streams, often for a waqf [18].
- Today "Khan el-Khalili" names the whole tourist-market district along al-Muʿizz Street [13].

### 3.7 Fez medina

- The medina of Fez el-Bali (UNESCO 1981 [2]) is the classic case of *trade-by-street*. Each craft historically had its own souk or lane, and largely still does [17]. Examples:
  - **ʿAṭṭārīn**: perfumers and spices.
  - **Seffarine**: coppersmiths' square.
  - **Nejjarine**: carpenters. The 13th-century *Funduq al-Najjārīn* ("inn of the carpenters") is now a woodcraft museum.
  - **Sekkatine**: horse, mule and donkey tack.
  - **Dabbaghin and Chouara**: tanneries and leather.
  - **Achebine**: poultry.
  - **Kissariat al-Kifah**: the old covered luxury cloth market by the shrine of Moulay Idris II [17].
- The *qaysariyya*, next to the city's holiest shrine and closed with gates or chains at night, is the general pattern for the valuable-goods core of a Maghreb souk [2].

### 3.8 Damascus, Souq al-Hamidiyya

- About 600 m long and 15 m wide, under a metal barrel roof about 10 m high [15].
- It runs from near the Citadel to the plaza of the Umayyad Mosque [15][16].
- Begun about 1780 under Abdülhamid I, extended under Abdülhamid II, and re-roofed in iron after a fire (1884 per one source) [15][16].
- Bullet holes in the roof are said to date from French fire in the 1925 Syrian revolt [16].
- The pierced iron roof throws spots of daylight on the floor, a famous visual.
- A frequently quoted 1863 date is not supported by the sources found.

### 3.9 Chandni Chowk, Delhi

- Laid out about 1650 in Shah Jahan's new capital, Shahjahanabad. Tradition credits the design to his daughter **Jahanara Begum** [46].
- Originally about 1,520 yards long and 40 yards wide, with about 1,560 shops [46].
- A canal down the centre and a pool reflected the moonlight, hence the name "Moonlight Square" [46].
- The canal was filled in under British rule after 1857 [46].
- These details rest on well-established tradition rather than cited Mughal documents [46].

## 4. Caravanserais, khans, hans and funduqs: the Silk Road infrastructure

- **Layout.**
  - Square or rectangular, with a *single* gate wide enough for loaded camels, so the building could be shut and defended.
  - Inside is a central courtyard open to the sky, ringed by rooms, bays and stalls [18].
  - Safavid caravanserais have vaulted iwans and rooms on one or two levels. Some later ones are octagonal, and rural ones often have corner towers [18].
  - They held fodder and had a well or fountain [18].
  - The common two-storey urban pattern, with stables and storage below and lodgings and counting rooms above, is the wikala/rabʿ model of Cairo [18]. Ottoman urban hans, including the Istanbul hans, were similar (background).
- **Spacing.**
  - Rural caravanserais were placed about "a day's journey" apart.
  - In Iran that meant about **30–40 km** on open plains or desert, and about **10 km or less** in mountains [18].
  - This gives a natural rule for spacing waypoints.
- **Builders and funding.**
  - **Seljuk hans** of the 12th–13th centuries include Sultan Han near Aksaray, Ribat-i Sharaf in Khorasan and Ribat-i Malik in Uzbekistan [18].
  - **Shah Abbas I** (r. 1587–1629) built networks of caravanserais to improve trade routes [18]. The popular "999 caravanserais" figure is legend; Wikipedia gives no number.
  - Urban caravanserais were often waqf property whose rents supported mosques and schools. Some were built specifically to fund a nearby complex, as with the Wikala al-Ghuri [18].
- **Free lodging (background, not verified this session).** Many Seljuk and Ottoman road hans offered travellers free food and shelter, commonly said to be for three days, paid by the endowment. This is plausible and widely repeated but needs a source.
- **The caravan as a mobile market (background).**
  - A caravan (Persian *kārvān*) travelled under a leader, the *kārvān-bāshī* (Turkish *kervanbaşı*).
  - The leader negotiated tolls and protection, chose campsites and route, kept order, and often judged disputes on the road.
  - Merchants bought and sold at each stop, so the arrival of a caravan briefly turned a town into a market. Caravans timed their movement to seasonal fairs and pilgrimages, for example the Hajj caravans from Damascus and Cairo.
- **Network of written credit.** Caravan trade ran on bills and partnership contracts (section 7.5), so merchants did not have to carry all their coin.

## 5. South Asia: haats, mandis and melas

- **Haat (hāṭ).**
  - Open-air periodic markets in villages and towns of India, Nepal, Bhutan and Bangladesh.
  - They usually meet once, twice or three times a week, sometimes once a fortnight [42].
  - They are social gathering places as well as markets. Settlements tend to grow up around them, and successful haats become towns [42].
  - India's government counts **22,941 rural haats**. About 11,811 are under panchayats and 1,274 under Agricultural Produce Market Committees (APMCs) [42].
  - A 2019 parliamentary committee wanted "Gramin haats" upgraded because small farmers live far from the wholesale mandis. It also found that market fees meant for traders were often charged to farmers [44].
- **Mandi.**
  - Hindi for "market place". In modern use it means the regulated wholesale market for farm produce run by a state APMC [43].
  - Licensed commission agents (*arhatiyas*) and traders buy from farmers by open auction, in a system intended to curb exploitation by middlemen [43].
  - Historically the *ganj* or *mandi* was the grain market of a town, often founded by a noble or official who took fees (background).
  - The haat/mandi pair is a clean two-tier model: village retail/periodic market feeding a town wholesale market.
- **Melas (fairs).**
  - The **Pushkar Fair** in Rajasthan begins in the month of Kartik and ends on Kartik Purnima, the full moon of October or November.
  - It combines a pilgrimage, with bathing in the sacred lake and the cult of Brahma, and a camel and cattle fair. Semi-nomadic herders traditionally brought animals for about five days of trading around the full moon [45].
  - It draws over 200,000 visitors [45]. Its exact age is disputed: locals say more than a century, some sources say 19th century [45].
  - Religious calendar + livestock market + entertainment (races, contests) is the general mela formula.
- **Other markets.** Chandni Chowk is covered in section 3.9. Mughal and Rajput towns also had a *katra*, an enclosed market-cum-caravanserai [18], and *ganj*es, market towns founded by grant (background).

## 6. East and Southeast Asia

### 6.1 G. William Skinner's theory of Chinese marketing systems

- **Source.** Skinner, "Marketing and Social Structure in Rural China", *Journal of Asian Studies* 24 (1964–65), in three parts [33]. It is based on his fieldwork in Sichuan in 1949–50.
- **Core idea.** Skinner applied Christaller and Lösch's central-place theory to rural China [34][35].
  - Markets form a hierarchy. **Standard market towns** are at the base, then **intermediate** and **central** market towns, up to cities [34][35].
  - The **standard marketing area** was the real unit of peasant social life, not the village. Marriages, credit, secret societies, temple fairs and gossip all followed it [33].
- **Geometry.**
  - In the ideal case, each market area is a hexagon, and the areas tile into a honeycomb [35].
  - The model standard unit has one market town at the centre and **18 villages** around it: 6 on an inner ring and 12 on an outer ring [35].
- **Periodicity.**
  - Markets were periodic, not daily. They met on fixed days of the lunar month, counted in **xún**, the ten-day thirds of the month.
  - Frequency ran from once per xún in remote areas up to every other day in the busiest places [34].
  - As trade grew, a market could add meetings, for example from two to four per xún [34]. A German county study found many markets still on the two-per-ten-days rhythm in the 1990s [34].
  - **Background, not verified this session.** Neighbouring markets were scheduled on non-clashing day sets so a pedlar could circuit them. A three-per-xún market would meet on days 1-4-7, 2-5-8 or 3-6-9. A two-per-xún market would meet on 1-6, 2-7, 3-8 and so on. A higher-level town's schedule was set to interlock with its dependent standard markets.
  - This interlocking schedule lets itinerant traders and artisans run a fixed loop through the market towns.
- **Pedlars.** Skinner's logic is that periodicity exists because demand in each area is too small to support daily shops. Mobile sellers concentrate demand in time instead (background).
- **Critiques.**
  - Field studies found towns often strung *linearly* along rivers, canals and roads rather than in hexagons: Lo (1986) for the North China Plain, Chiu and Leung (1983) for Guangdong [36].
  - Hodder argued Skinner's Sichuan maps were distorted toward hexagons [36].
  - Potter and Potter found the standard market community less socially central than claimed [36].
  - For a game: use the hexagon as a default and let terrain bend it.

### 6.2 Chinese night markets: Song Kaifeng and the end of the curfew

- **The Tang system.** Tang Chang'an was a walled grid of *fang* (wards) with gates shut at night. Trade was confined to the official East and West Markets under a curfew (background; [38]).
- **The Song break.** Under the Northern Song in Kaifeng, the ward walls and curfew broke down. Commercial streets spread through the city, and shops opened onto the street [37][38].
  - Dates are disputed. Popular accounts cite a 965 edict beginning to relax the night curfew. Wikipedia, following secondary scholarship, gives **1063** for the curfew's abolition [37].
  - Some Japanese scholars think enforcement had already lapsed in the Five Dynasties period or under Emperor Renzong [37].
- **The memoir.** Meng Yuanlao's *Dongjing Meng Hua Lu* (1147), written after the fall of Kaifeng, records:
  - night markets open until midnight or later;
  - reopening before dawn;
  - pleasure quarters (*wazi*) with storytellers and theatre;
  - food delivered to homes [37].
- **The image.** Zhang Zeduan's scroll *Along the River During the Qingming Festival* is the standard picture of this open commercial city (background).

### 6.3 Taiwan night markets

- **Shilin** (Taipei) grew out of a daytime market formally set up in 1909. The night market is dated to about 1913. Produce came by boat to a nearby wharf on the Keelung River [39].
- The old market building was demolished in 2002, and a permanent new site opened in 2011 [39].
- **Raohe Street** night market (1987) was Taipei's first with government-set traffic control. Cars are banned from 17:00 to midnight [39].
- Early Taipei night markets let rural migrants make a living selling the snack foods of their home districts [39]. This gives a regional-cuisine stall pattern.

### 6.4 Pasar malam and the Javanese rotating market

- *Pasar malam* is Malay for "night market". It is held in the evening in residential neighbourhoods across Indonesia, Malaysia, Brunei and Singapore [40].
- **Rotation.** Wikipedia ties it to the older Javanese market cycle, in which market days rotated between villages (background: the five-day *pasaran* week). The night market is seen as continuing this non-permanent market culture [40].
- **Origin claims.**
  - One account dates the modern form to colonial Batavia in the late 1820s, started by Tan Eng Goan, Majoor of the Chinese [40].
  - Electric lighting spread the format in the early 20th century [40].
  - One historian calls it "a Dutch transplant" rather than native [41]. A Malaysian food source credits China [40].

### 6.5 Floating markets

- Thailand's canal markets, such as **Damnoen Saduak** in Ratchaburi, are sold from boats on irrigation and transport canals (*khlong*). Vendors paddle sampans laden with produce.
- **Background, not verified this session.** The Damnoen Saduak canal is usually dated to 1866–68 under King Mongkut (Rama IV).
- Similar boat markets exist in the Mekong Delta (Cái Răng) and Banjarmasin. They are a direct model for markets on water or on any mobile platform.

## 7. Medieval and early modern Europe

### 7.1 The Champagne fairs

- **The cycle.** The counts of Champagne ran six fairs a year in four towns. Together they formed a near-continuous international market that "rotated" around the county [21][23]:
  1. **Lagny-sur-Marne**: from 2 January.
  2. **Bar-sur-Aube**: from the Tuesday before mid-Lent.
  3. **Provins (May fair)**: from the Tuesday before Ascension.
  4. **Troyes, "hot fair" (St John)**: late June.
  5. **Provins (St Ayoul)**: from 14 September.
  6. **Troyes, "cold fair" (St Rémi)**: from 2 November.
- **Phases of each fair** [21]:
  - an opening period of about eight days for arrival and setting up;
  - the **cloth fair**;
  - the **leather fair**;
  - goods sold by weight (*avoir de poids*: spices, dyes, wax and so on);
  - a final **settlement period of about four days** for accounts and payments.
- **Length.** Sources disagree, from two to three weeks up to six or more weeks per fair [21][23].
- **Peak and decline.** The fairs peaked in the late 12th and 13th centuries [21]. Total fair tax yields spiked in 1296, at about 8,383 livres tournois from five fairs, then collapsed [21].
- **Institutions.**
  - **Gardes des foires** (Guards of the Fair) were count-appointed officers. They heard complaints, enforced contracts and kept order [21][23]. Notaries and sergeants (police) were added under them [23].
  - The ultimate sanction was **exclusion**. A merchant who defaulted, and in time his whole town, could be barred from future fairs [21].
  - Weights and measures were strictly regulated. The "Troyes" weight, as in troy ounce, is a lasting legacy (background) [21].
  - **Safe conduct.** The counts protected merchants on the roads. In 1209 Philip Augustus granted safe conduct across France to merchants going to the fairs [21].
  - **Credit clearing.** After the cloth sales, Italian money-changers at their tables (*banche*):
    - made compensatory payments;
    - set up future credit;
    - lent to nobles;
    - settled bills of exchange that were often drawn to fall due *at a fair*.
  - The fairs became an international clearing house for paper debt [21].
- **"Letters of fair" (background).** In historical literature, *lettres de foire* were obligations sealed under the fair's seal. The guards could enforce them anywhere by writing to the debtor's home jurisdiction, and threatened to ban that whole town if it did not pay. The Wikipedia page does not use the term, so verify before citing.
- **Who enforced what?**
  - Milgrom, North and Weingast (1990) modelled the fairs as run by private "law merchant" judges who kept reputation records [65].
  - Edwards and Ogilvie (2012) argue the opposite. Enforcement was by *public* authorities: the counts' courts and guards. The fairs' rise and fall followed princely policy, and no collective merchant reprisals are recorded in 80 years of peak activity [22].
  - Edwards attributes the decline to Philip IV's policies after Champagne joined the French crown in 1285: taxing Italian merchants and restricting Flemish trade [21][22].
  - Other factors: Italian sea routes to Flanders, war, and later the Black Death [21][64].

### 7.2 English markets, fairs and charters

- **Chartered rights.**
  - In England, holding a market or fair was a royal **franchise**, granted by charter.
  - The holder collected tolls and stallage and ran a court.
  - An unlicensed or too-close rival could be suppressed as a nuisance.
- **Bracton's distance rule** (13th century).
  - A reasonable day's journey is 20 miles. Split it into thirds: travel to market, trade, travel home.
  - So a new market within **6⅔ miles** of an existing one harms it and can be suppressed [24][25].
  - English common law still uses the 6⅔-mile radius for rival-market protection. Norwich City Council's policy is one example, and *Birmingham City Council v Anvil Fairs* is a case on it [26][25].
  - Historians doubt the rule was ever consistently enforced. One quo warranto case alleged a five-mile rule, and maps of medieval markets show many closer pairs [27].
- **Piepowder courts.**
  - The "court of piepowders" (from *pieds poudrés*, "dusty feet", meaning travelling merchants and vagrants) was a special court that a borough or fair-owner ran during a fair or market [28].
  - Its civil jurisdiction covered contracts made within the fair. It judged within the day, between tides or within the fair's span.
  - Blackstone called it "the lowest, and at the same time the most expeditious, court of justice known to the law of England" [28].
  - Coke's "justice as quick as dust falls from the foot" explanation is folk etymology [28].
  - The courts declined from the 17th century. The last sitting was at Hemel Hempstead in 1898 [28].
- **Stourbridge Fair, Cambridge.**
  - King John is said to have licensed a three-day fair in 1199 for the leper chapel at Stourbridge. Other sources give a charter or first fair of 1211 [29][30].
  - At its peak it was one of the largest fairs in Europe, held each autumn on Stourbridge Common [29][30].
  - Defoe (visit about 1722, *Tour*, 1724–26) called it "not only the greatest in the whole nation, but in the world" [30]. He described streets of booths named for trades, a central wholesale square, and hops and wool trading (background, see [30]).
  - A fair laid out as a temporary town of trade-named streets, which vanishes after the season, is a perfect game set-piece.

### 7.3 German fairs: Frankfurt and Leipzig

- **Frankfurt.**
  - On 11 July 1240 Emperor Frederick II granted an imperial privilege putting merchants travelling to the Frankfurt fair under imperial protection, with escort. This is the founding charter of Messe Frankfurt [32].
  - Frankfurt held autumn and later Lenten fairs. It became the centre of the early book trade (background).
- **Leipzig.**
  - A fair at Leipzig is first mentioned about 1165 [31].
  - In 1497 Maximilian I raised it to an **imperial fair** [31].
  - It later received **staple rights** (1507): merchants within a radius had to offer their goods in Leipzig first. These were used against rival fairs at Erfurt, Halle and Magdeburg [31].
  - It became Europe's leading sample fair (*Mustermesse*) by the 19th century (background) [31].

### 7.4 Periodic market rotation in Europe

- The same day-of-week logic as Skinner's China applied in Europe.
  - Neighbouring English market towns usually had different charter days, for example Monday here and Thursday there. A dealer or carrier could work a weekly circuit.
  - The fair cycle (Champagne, the Flemish fairs, later Geneva, Lyon and Besançon/Piacenza exchange fairs) worked as a regional rotating clearing system (background) [64].

## 8. Institutions of the bazaar

### 8.1 Guilds: asnāf, esnaf, lonca

- **Islamic world.**
  - Trades were organised as *ṣinf* (pl. *aṣnāf*). Each had a headman (*shaykh*, *ʿarīf*, or *amīn*, the trustee or warden).
  - Guilds regulated members, mediated with government, collected taxes, and could protect both merchants and customers [2].
  - Pre-Ottoman Anatolian **Ahi** brotherhoods, based on *futuwwa* ethics, are seen as forerunners of the Ottoman guilds [59].
- **Ottoman esnaf.**
  - Each trade's council was headed by a **kethüda** (steward) with a **yiğitbaşı** (deputy) who handled internal disputes and passed matters upward [59]. In the Grand Bazaar the kadı appointed the kethüda [5].
  - The kadı fixed the number of shops in a trade, a quota system called *gedik* in later centuries (background).
  - Experts (*ehl-i hibre*) helped set the **narh** (official price ceilings) and check weighing instruments [59].
  - The kadı and the muhtesib supervised all guild production [59].
  - *Lonca* meant both a guild's meeting place and, in later usage, the guild itself [59].

### 8.2 Waqf: the endowed bazaar

- A *waqf* (vakıf) is an inalienable endowment under Islamic law that locks buildings and revenues into perpetual use [18].
- Bazaars, bedestens, hans and caravanserais were routinely built as waqf property. Their rents paid for a mosque, madrasa, hospital, soup kitchen or fountain.
  - The Istanbul Cevahir Bedesten funded Hagia Sophia [5].
  - Cairo's wikalas funded religious complexes [18].
- Commerce and piety were therefore physically and financially fused. The mosque often stood at the bazaar's heart, and shop rents kept it running.

### 8.3 Hisba and the muhtasib (market inspector)

- **Office.**
  - The *muḥtasib* held the office of *ḥisba* ("enjoining good and forbidding wrong") [19][20].
  - In practice he was the "inspector of markets and morals". He was also called *ṣāḥib al-sūq* ("master of the market") or *ʿāmil al-sūq* [20].
- **Origins.**
  - Some link the office to the Byzantine *agoranomos*. Others, such as Benjamin Foster, deny direct descent. Market overseers existed under the Parthians and Sasanians [20].
  - Tradition says Muhammad and ʿUmar appointed market inspectors. The first clearly documented muhtasibs are early Abbasid [20].
- **Duties.** In Mamluk Cairo the muhtasib regulated:
  - weights and measures;
  - prices;
  - public morals;
  - hygiene, baths and traffic [20].
  - He also investigated cheating and poor goods, controlled noise and cleanliness, and kept order in the lanes [2].
- **Limits of power.**
  - He could punish cheats publicly, for example by parading them [20].
  - He had no jurisdiction to hear contested cases. He settled matters only where facts were admitted or guilt was confessed. Contested cases went to the *qāḍī* [19].
- **Manuals.**
  - Hisba manuals catalogued traders' tricks for him to detect. Examples are al-Shayzari's *Nihāyat al-rutba* and Ibn al-Ukhuwwa's *Maʿālim al-qurba* [20].
  - From al-Andalus there is Ibn ʿAbdūn's Seville treatise (background). The Spanish *almotacén*, the municipal weights-and-measures officer, inherited the title (background).
- **Corruption.** The office was sold for a large monthly sum as early as 961, and muhtasibs were notorious for bribes [20]. This is a built-in source of game tension.
- **End.** The office was abolished in Cairo and Alexandria in 1837 and ended in Iran between the 1850s and 1926 [20]. In Pakistan, "Mohtasib" now means ombudsman [20].
- **Local titles.** *Kedkhoda* in Istanbul and *amir-i bazariyan* in Delhi [2].

### 8.4 The bazaar–mosque alliance in Iran (the *bāzārī*)

- The Iranian merchant class (*bāzārī*) and the Shiʿi clergy (*ʿulamā*) were tied by:
  - religious taxes (*khums*) paid by merchants;
  - waqf administration;
  - intermarriage;
  - shared space, since the bazaar wraps the Friday mosque.
- **Political moments.**
  - **Tobacco Protest (1891–92).** Bazaar strikes and a clerical fatwa against tobacco use forced cancellation of the British tobacco concession [57].
  - **Constitutional Revolution (1905–11).** Merchants took sanctuary (*bast*) in shrines and the British legation (background).
  - **1979 revolution.** Bazaar strikes, and bazaar money funding strikers, brought down the Shah [57].
- **Keshavarzian (2007).** Arasta Keshavarzian argues that the Pahlavi-era bazaar's power came from dense *horizontal* networks of trust. Under the Islamic Republic these were reshaped into *vertical* patronage tied to the state [58][57].

### 8.5 Credit and trust: suftaja, hawala and bills of exchange

- **Suftaja.**
  - A medieval Islamic written order to pay money in another place, a three-party instrument [55].
  - Abbasid fiscal agents used it to move tax revenue to the central treasury. Merchants used it to avoid carrying coin [55].
  - Jewish merchants used it by the 8th century, and it is abundant in the Cairo Geniza for the 11th–13th centuries (background) [54].
  - Some law schools disapproved because it could hide interest [55].
  - Benjamin Geva argues these instruments foreshadowed the European bill of exchange, both as a means of transmitting funds and as a negotiable paper [54].
- **Hawala.**
  - In Islamic law, *ḥawāla* is the transfer of a debt. Today it names the informal broker-to-broker money transfer system that runs on trust and offsetting balances.
  - Its "Islamic origin" is contested [56].
- **European bill of exchange.** It developed among Italian merchants by the 13th century. Bills were often made payable at fairs, so fairs served as clearing dates [21].

### 8.6 Reputation coalitions: Greif's Maghribi traders and the critiques

- **Greif (1989, 1993) [50].**
  - Uses the Cairo Geniza letters of the 11th-century "Maghribi traders", Jewish merchants of North African origin.
  - Argues they formed a **coalition**. Merchants hired only fellow members as overseas agents and shared information through letters.
  - An agent caught cheating one member would be shunned by all (*multilateral punishment*). So honesty was enforced by reputation, without courts.
- **Edwards and Ogilvie (2012) [51].**
  - Find no empirical example showing that a "coalition" existed.
  - Maghribis used formal Jewish and Islamic courts and did business with non-Maghribis.
- **Jessica Goldberg** (*Trade and Institutions in the Medieval Mediterranean*, 2012; "Making Reputation Work" [52]).
  - Agrees that reputation mattered, but through quite different mechanisms from Greif's model.
  - Merchants worked to manage and display reputation within business relationships, alongside legal tools.
- **Greif (2008) [51]** replied with fuller quantitative analysis of the letters.
- **Lisa Bernstein (2017) [53]** proposes a middle path. The Maghribis were a "bridge-and-cluster" network with relational contracting, and the critics' facts do not overturn the core idea.
- **Design takeaway.** Model reputation as *networked* and *partly visible*, with courts as a parallel option, not as a pure blacklist.

### 8.7 Geertz and the "bazaar economy"

- **The work.** Clifford Geertz studied the market of Sefrou, Morocco. It was a market town of about 15,000–30,000 people and a former caravan stop [47].
  - Main study: "Suq: The Bazaar Economy in Sefrou", in Geertz, Geertz and Rosen, *Meaning and Order in Moroccan Society* (1979) [48].
  - Short economics version: "The Bazaar Economy: Information and Search in Peasant Marketing", *American Economic Review* 68(2), 1978, pp. 28–32 [47].
- **Core claims [47].**
  - In the bazaar, **information is poor, scarce, maldistributed, inefficiently communicated and intensely valued**. The search for information, and its protection from others, is the central fact of bazaar life.
  - Prices are not posted. Quality is not standardised. Weights and measures vary. The cost of search is high.
  - Two institutions make search efficient:
    1. **Clientelization.** Buyers and sellers form repeated, durable relationships with particular partners. That cuts search, trading wide but shallow price comparison for deep knowledge of one partner.
    2. **Bargaining.** Haggling is multidimensional, over quantity, quality, credit terms and extras, not only price.
  - **Search** is mainly **intensive** (probing one offer deeply) rather than **extensive** (sampling many sellers), because clientelization favours depth.
  - The bazaar is a **structure of ties** of competition and interdependence, not a chaotic crowd [47].
- **Fanselow's critique (1990) [49].**
  - In "The Bazaar Economy or How Bizarre is the Bazaar Really?" (*Man* n.s. 25(2): 250–265), Fanselow argues that "bazaar" lumps two kinds of market together. One deals in **standardised, interchangeable goods**, the other in **unstandardised, variable goods**.
  - Geertz's features (hidden information, haggling, clientelization) belong to the unstandardised sector only. Grain or sugar of known grade trades very differently from carpets or used goods.
  - He shows this with south Indian evidence, linking it to price-setting, credit and labour.
- **Design takeaway.** Haggling and relationship mechanics should scale with how unstandardised a good is.

## 9. Architecture of the bazaar

- **Covered streets.**
  - The *rasteh* (Persian) or *arasta* (Ottoman) is a straight street of shops of one trade. It is barrel-vaulted or roofed with a chain of domes [8]; the domes-and-skylights pattern is background.
  - Shops are small cells raised on a platform (*dukkān* / *sakū*). The shopkeeper often sits inside with goods within reach. Heavy shutters lock at night [2].
- **Light.**
  - Each dome or vault bay has a skylight or oculus: a shaft of light that marks bay rhythm and lets out heat and smoke [10][9].
  - Kashan's timcheh uses a big central opening [9]. Damascus's iron roof is pierced with holes [15][16].
  - Light pools on the floor while the shopfronts stay shaded. This is a recognisable look.
- **Junctions.** Where main streets cross, a larger domed space (*chahārsū*, "four directions") forms a node, often with a fountain (background).
- **Timcheh / qaysariyya / bedesten.** A domed, often gated hall for one high-value trade. It is the most secure part of the market, closed at night [2][5][8].
- **Sarai / caravanserai / han / wikala.** A courtyard building opening off the bazaar. Wholesale, storage and lodging for visiting merchants. Animals and goods are on the ground floor, offices and rooms above [18].
- **Zoning.**
  - Shops are grouped by trade on named streets [2][5][17].
  - Clean, high-status trades (books, perfume, gold, cloth) stand near the mosque and the core.
  - Noisy, dirty or smelly trades (smiths, tanners, dyers, potters) stand toward the edges or gates (background; e.g. Fez tanneries at the margin [17]).
  - Grouping also helps price comparison and makes the muhtasib's job easier [2].
- **Gates and night.**
  - Market quarters had gates locked at night, with watchmen [5].
  - Ottoman Istanbul's bazaar needed an imperial edict to open at night [5].
  - Persian and Ottoman bazaars were lit by oil lamps in niches and lanterns at the gates (background). Night trade belonged to the less regulated street, coffeehouse and festival, as in Ramadan night markets.
  - The Chinese and Malay night-market traditions show the opposite pattern: markets that exist *only* after dark, lit by lanterns, then carbide and electric lamps [37][40].

## 10. Itinerant trader peoples: models for a mobile merchant culture

- **Old Assyrians of Kanesh.** A family firm split between home city and colony. Long-term joint funds, a colony court, and treaty-based tolls [3][63].
- **Sogdians** (from Samarkand and Bukhara, 4th–8th centuries).
  - The **Sogdian Ancient Letters**, five nearly complete letters, were found by Aurel Stein in 1907 in a watchtower west of Dunhuang. They are dated about 313 CE [61].
  - They show Sogdian merchant communities in the Gansu corridor and inner China, organised in hierarchical networks that shared information [61].
  - One letter, from a woman named Miwnay, records her and her daughter abandoned and destitute at Dunhuang [61]. The diaspora was not uniformly rich.
  - Sogdians later supplied caravan leaders (*sabao*) to Tang China (background).
- **Radhanites** (9th century).
  - Jewish merchants described by the Abbasid postmaster **Ibn Khordadbeh** in his *Book of Roads and Kingdoms* (about 870) [62].
  - They spoke Arabic, Persian, Greek ("Roman"), Frankish, Spanish and Slavonic. They ran four routes from the Rhône valley to China, by land and sea [62].
  - They carried goods of small bulk and high value: spices, perfumes, jewels and silk [62].
  - Whether "Radhanite" means a guild, a clan or Jewish long-distance traders in general is unclear [62].
- **Maghribi / Geniza traders** (11th century). Agency through fellow merchants and letters. See section 8.6 [50][52].
- **Armenian merchants of New Julfa.**
  - Shah Abbas I resettled Armenians from Julfa on the Aras river at New Julfa, a suburb of Isfahan, in 1604–05 (background).
  - From there they built a network stretching from London and Amsterdam to Manila and Acapulco [60].
  - Sebouh Aslanian (2011) describes a hub-and-spoke *circulation society* (hub New Julfa, nodes abroad) rather than a loose diaspora [60]. Its tools were:
    - the **commenda** contract: a stay-at-home financier (*khwāja*) funds a travelling agent for a share of profit;
    - family firms;
    - extensive commercial correspondence and couriers;
    - the Armenian Church as an information channel.
  - Julfans trusted mainly fellow Julfans. Travelling agents' families at home acted as a kind of collateral [60].
  - Their four circuits were the Indian Ocean, Russia, the Mediterranean, and north-western Europe [60].
- **Caravaneers and pedlars generally.**
  - The *kārvān-bāshī* led and judged the caravan.
  - Chinese pedlars circuited Skinner's market weeks [33].
  - Javanese traders followed the five-day *pasaran* rotation [40].
  - Pushkar's semi-nomadic herders came to the fair at the full moon [45].

## 11. Design takeaways

The following institutions and mechanics transfer well into a game. Each lists the historical basis and a suggested mechanic.

1. **Market warden (muhtasib, gardes des foires, clerk of the market).**
   - *Basis:* a public officer who checks weights, measures and prices, punishes cheats publicly, and keeps lanes clean and orderly. He can only settle cases where guilt is admitted; contested cases go to a judge [19][20][21].
   - *Mechanic:*
     - The warden NPC runs patrols. Rigged scales or watered goods carry a detection chance.
     - Penalties are public: pillory, parade, or a reputation hit visible to all.
     - Players can bribe the warden; the office was historically sold and corrupt [20]. Bribery lowers detection but raises scandal risk.
     - Disputes escalate from warden to judge (qāḍī / piepowder court).
2. **Guild rules (esnaf / asnāf).**
   - *Basis:* trade-specific guilds under a kethüda and deputy. Fixed shop quotas, entry by inheritance or by buying a retiring member's place, official price ceilings (narh), and expert price-setting [5][59].
   - *Mechanic:*
     - To open a shop in a trade street, the player needs guild membership: buy a *slot* (gedik), inherit it, or apprentice.
     - Guilds cap prices and the number of shops, and fine undercutting.
     - The guild head is a quest-giver and dispute-settler.
3. **Trade-by-street layout.**
   - *Basis:* named streets per trade [5][17]; clean and valuable trades at the mosque/core and in a locked bedesten/timcheh; noisy and dirty trades at the edges; khans for wholesale off the main spine [2][18].
   - *Mechanic:*
     - Procedural bazaar generator: spine street, with crossing nodes (*chahārsū*) at junctions.
     - Assign trades by "cleanliness × value" rings.
     - Place a strongroom hall at the core that closes at night, and caravanserais at the gates.
     - Use skylit dome bays as the repeat unit, with pools of light on the floor.
     - Players learn "copper is on Seffarine Street", which gives navigation by trade.
4. **Rotating fair cycle (Champagne) and periodic market days (Skinner).**
   - *Basis:*
     - The six Champagne fairs rotate through four towns over the year, each with set phases (setup, cloth, leather, goods by weight, settlement) [21].
     - Chinese standard markets meet on interlocking day patterns within a 10-day xún, so pedlars can circuit them [33][34].
     - The Javanese five-day market rotation [40].
   - *Mechanic:*
     - A calendar of market days per settlement, scheduled so neighbouring markets never clash.
     - Itinerant NPC merchants (and the player) follow a loop.
     - Big fairs have phases that change what is on sale.
     - A final "settlement days" phase clears debts; defaulters are banned from the next fair.
     - Market areas default to hexagons of about 18 villages around a market town, distorted by roads and rivers [35][36].
5. **Market rights and the distance rule.**
   - *Basis:* chartered franchise; the 6⅔-mile rule (one third of a 20-mile day) [24][25]; staple rights forcing goods through a fair city [31].
   - *Mechanic:*
     - Founding a market needs a charter from the ruler.
     - A rival market within a day-third radius can be suppressed or sued.
     - Staple towns force passing caravans to offer goods first.
6. **Fast fair justice (piepowder).**
   - *Basis:* a court for disputes arising inside the fair, decided within the day [28].
   - *Mechanic:* a quick dispute mini-game that only exists during fairs. Outcomes feed reputation.
7. **Market truce and safe conduct.**
   - *Basis:* the ʿUkāẓ truce [2], Champagne safe conduct [21], the Frankfurt escort privilege [32].
   - *Mechanic:* during fair periods, roads to the fair are protected, and violence inside the market is penalised by every faction. Violating safe conduct brings ruler-level consequences.
8. **Letters of credit (suftaja, bill of exchange, letters of fair).**
   - *Basis:* paper orders to pay in another city [54][55]; bills payable *at the next fair*, cleared by money-changers [21]; hawala broker networks [56].
   - *Mechanic:*
     - The player deposits money with a changer in town A and receives a letter payable in town B, for a fee, avoiding robbery.
     - Letters can be drawn "payable at the next X fair".
     - Money-changer NPCs settle net balances at fair-end.
     - Forgery and failed brokers are risks.
9. **Reputation coalitions.**
   - *Basis:* Greif's Maghribi coalition with multilateral punishment [50]; the critiques that courts and wider networks also mattered [51][52][53]; New Julfa's hub network with families as collateral [60].
   - *Mechanic:*
     - Merchant networks (guild, clan, diaspora) share reputation information *with a delay*, carried by letters and caravans.
     - Cheating one member hurts standing with all once the news arrives.
     - Courts remain a slower alternative.
     - Joining a network gives access to trusted agents (commenda partners) who carry your goods for a profit share.
10. **Haggling customs (Geertz / Fanselow).**
    - *Basis:* information is scarce. Search is mainly intensive. Clientelization (regular partners) and multidimensional bargaining are the core institutions [47]. They apply mainly to unstandardised goods [49].
    - *Mechanic:*
      - For staples (grain, salt), use posted prices with small spreads.
      - For unique goods (carpets, used gear, art), use hidden reservation prices and a haggling game over price, quantity, credit term and extras.
      - Repeat-customer relationships unlock better first offers and information ("the price is good today because a caravan came in").
      - Make extensive search costly in time, so loyalty pays.
11. **Waqf-funded markets.**
    - *Basis:* shop rents endow a mosque, school or inn [5][18].
    - *Mechanic:*
      - Buildings can be endowed. Their income permanently funds a public service, such as free inn nights (background claim) or a school.
      - Endowments cannot be sold, which is a political anchor.
      - Faction alliances like the Iranian bazaar–mosque bloc can call strikes and bazaar closures [57][58].
12. **Caravanserai network and caravan leader.**
    - *Basis:* waystations a day apart: about 30–40 km on plains, about 10 km in mountains [18]. Single-gate courtyard fortresses. A kārvān-bāshī negotiates tolls and judges disputes.
    - *Mechanic:*
      - Space caravanserais by travel-day.
      - Caravans are mobile markets that turn each stop into a pop-up bazaar.
      - The leader is elected or hired. The player can join, hire guards, or lead.
13. **Night economy.**
    - *Basis:* curfew-locked bazaars versus Song Kaifeng's lifted curfew and night markets [37]; Taiwan and pasar malam night markets with traffic closures [39][40].
    - *Mechanic:*
      - Day bazaars lock and empty at dusk.
      - Separate night markets of food, entertainment and grey-market goods open in streets or squares under lanterns.
      - A "curfew lifted" policy can be a city-level unlock.
14. **Merchant enclave (kārum, funduq, khan for foreigners).**
    - *Basis:* Kanesh kārum [3]; customs khans [11].
    - *Mechanic:* foreign merchant quarters with their own court and tolls set by treaty with the local ruler. A customs khan where all imported cloth must be registered and taxed.
