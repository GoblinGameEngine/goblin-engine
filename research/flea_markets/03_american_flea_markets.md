# American Flea Markets and Swap Meets: Reference Profiles for Game Design

Research done October 15, 2026. Numbers in square brackets are source numbers from the list at the end. "approx." marks numbers I am unsure of, and the text notes where sources disagree. Many flea-market facts online come from promotional listings or SEO travel blogs. I mark those as weaker sources and lean on news outlets, official sites, and Wikipedia wherever I can.

**What I could not find.** The sources I found say almost nothing about signage at most markets. I could not confirm a giant wheel at Wagon Wheel or deliberate "frontier/Western" theming at Trader's World. Most dimensions of pylons, arches and neon are also unconfirmed. I say so in each profile, so you can tell documented detail from design inference.

---

## 1. Trader's World Market (Lebanon / Monroe, Ohio)

**Location.** 601 Union Rd, off I-75 in Warren County. Addresses vary between sources. WCPO and travel sites say "Lebanon, OH 45036" [1]. The market's own site says "Monroe, Ohio 45050" [2].

**History.**
- Opened in 1984 [1].
- A family-memoir essay names founders Jay and Helen Frick, from Depression-era farming families. It says the land was bought in 1963, when that stretch of I-75 opened [3]. This is a single, informal source.
- In 2012 the official site said the market was "now in its 28th year" (i.e., 1984/85) [2].

**Size.**
- 16 buildings, 850 indoor vendor spaces and 400 outdoor spaces, on 11 acres "combined," with "over two miles of storefronts" [1][2].
- A scooter-retailer listing says "1,000+ booths" [4].
- The memoir says 120 acres. That probably means the whole landholding, not the market footprint [3].
- Ratio: about 1,250 spaces on 11 acres, roughly 110 spaces per acre. That is very dense, because most space sits in long indoor corridors.

**Indoor vs outdoor.** About 68% indoor and 32% outdoor by space count [1][2]. The corridors are long and air-conditioned [3].

**Hours.** Saturday and Sunday 9–5, plus the Friday after Thanksgiving [2].

**Fees.** No vendor rates are published online.

**Admission and parking.**
- The official site promises "plenty of paved parking" [2].
- One weak listing says admission and parking are free [4].

**Layout.** Rows of buildings joined by indoor "storefront" corridors. Outdoor spaces sit around them. Tenants include permanent shops such as a bridal shop, a barbershop/wig shop and produce sellers [2][3].

**Signage and branding.**
- Slogan: "The Midwest's Largest & Most Colorful Market." Also "It's a shopping safari…Enjoy the hunt!" [2].
- The entrance gate is topped by a **red rearing horse statue** and **several colored palm trees**, with a parade of plastic wide-eyed giraffes [3].
- Inside are murals, agrarian tools, antique cars and painted "wisdom" sayings on the walls, in a "museum-like" look [2].
- A food-court statue of an old maid is named "Matilda" [3].
- Sources disagree on the buildings: red-and-white barn style vs. white buildings in rows (travel blogs, weak).
- **I found no source that calls the theme "Western" or "frontier."** The documented style is a mix of farm Americana, safari and roadside kitsch.

**Culture.**
- Fudge stand, food court, soft-serve, giant cookies [3].
- Vendors sell Case knives, Zippos, boots and Bengals clocks [3].
- A recent visit found "more empty booths than occupied," a sign of decline [3].
- Neighbor landmark: the "Touchdown Jesus" statue across I-75, which burned in 2010 [3].

## 2. The Red Barn (several markets share the name)

**Main candidate: Red Barn Flea Market, Bradenton, Florida** (1707 1st St E).
- **History:** run by one family since 1981 [83][84]. No founder name found.
- **Size:**
  - 145,000 sq ft of flea market, plaza shops, food courts and open-air farmers markets [83].
  - Marketspread: about 80,000 sq ft air-conditioned and 600+ booths [84].
  - We3Travel: 55 acres and 700+ merchants [82].
- **Split:** three zones: air-conditioned indoor, non-air-conditioned indoor, and open-air [82].
- **Hours:** Friday–Sunday 9–4 [82]. Other listings add winter Wednesdays (Nov–Apr, 8–4).
- **Admission and parking:** free entry and free parking per listings. It gets congested in snowbird season [82].
- **Food:** Mexican stand, Polish sausage, sweets, a small bar, and a pet shop with exotic birds [82].
- **Signage:** none documented. The name implies a barn-red building and sign, but no source describes it.

**Second candidate: "Big Red Barn," Aromas, California.**
- A 52-ft barn built in 1945 by Kaiser Aluminum as a hay barn for Harry Ellingwood [85].
- In 1969 Frances Ellingwood and her husband began selling antiques and Disney memorabilia inside, and a market of several hundred vendors grew around it [85].
- The barn itself was closed in 2009 over county code issues. After Frances died in 2018, an inheritance fight followed [85].
- This is the best example of a "painted barn as landmark."

**Others:** Red Barn Flea Market Antiques & More (Waxahachie, TX) and the Red Barn Marketplace (Lincolnville, ME, about 30 vendors). Both are small.

**For the game:** "Red Barn" is a generic name. A barn-red gable building is the usual visual cue.

## 3. Wagon Wheel Flea Market (Pinellas Park, Florida) — CLOSED 2020

**History.**
- Founded in 1966 by Hardy and Janet Huntley [5][6].
- Hardy was a used-car dealer. Janet scouted yard sales for things he fixed and resold. Their stand on Park Boulevard grew out of his used-car lot [5][6].
- A 1986 fire destroyed it. It reopened the next day, and nearly 2,000 booths were rebuilt [5].
- Janet died in 2009 and Hardy in 2013 [6][7].
- The site also took in the former **Mustang Drive-In Flea Market**, and both closed together ("Wagon Wheel and Mustang Flea Markets") [6][9].

**Size.**
- 125 acres [5].
- Vendor counts disagree: "nearly 2,000 booths" [5], "2,500-plus vendors" [6], and "more than 1,500 vendors" displaced [9].
- Ratio: about 12–20 vendors per acre.
- Billed as one of Florida's oldest and busiest flea and produce markets [6][8].

**Indoor vs outdoor.** "Outdoors and under cover" [6]. Permanent buildings and food trucks were added over time [5].

**Hours.** Saturday and Sunday, 8–4, "rain or shine" [5][8].

**Fees, admission and parking.** Not found.

**Culture.**
- Beer garden and live music all day [8].
- Fresh produce and grilled food [8].
- Three generations of the same family ran it [8].
- Vendors had sold there "for decades" [7].

**Closure.**
- It shut in March 2020 (COVID). On June 10, 2020 the family announced it would not reopen ("the most difficult decision we have ever made as a family"). Vendors had to clear out by June 30 [5][6].
- As of January 2026 the land is still vacant and owned by the Huntley family [7].
- Displaced vendors moved to Shawn Yang's Pinellas Farmers & Flea Market (Largo, 2021–2025). The county bought that 21-acre site for $33.3M, and in 2024 it was used as a hurricane-debris yard [7].
- In November 2025 they moved again, to the "Farmers and Flea Bazaar" in Derby Lane greyhound-track parking lots: about 150 vendors, 500–1,000 visitors a day, free, 7am–1pm [7].
- This chain is a good template for a "diaspora of a dead market" story.

**Signage.** **I found no source documenting a giant wagon wheel.** The name implies one, but treat it as design inference.

## 4. Flea World (Sanford, Florida) — CLOSED 2015

**History.**
- Opened by Orlando businessman Syd Levy, on US 17-92 [10].
- Founding year disagrees: 1981 per Wikipedia [10], 1982 per WKMG ("staple since 1982") [11].

**Size.**
- More than 1,700 vendors [10].
- Acreage disagrees: 104 acres [10], 118 acres [12], about 110 gross acres [12].
- Levy claimed more than 3 million visitors a year at its peak [10].

**Indoor vs outdoor.** Billed as "the nation's largest flea market under one roof," so mostly covered [10]. The buildings had iron roll-up doors, sold off during demolition [12].

**Hours.** Friday, Saturday and Sunday [10].

**Attractions.** **Fun World**, an amusement park beside the market, opened in 1990 for $5M: go-karts, bumper cars, carnival rides [10].

**Culture.** The Orlando Sentinel called it "a poor man's mall." Vendors sold tools, toys, legal advice, fortune-telling and eyebrow waxing [10].

**Closure.**
- Announced November 2014 [10].
- Closed August 30, 2015 (GrowthSpotter says the 31st), after "a dealer exodus" [10][11][12].
- Demolition began May 12, 2016 for the "Reagan Center" mixed-use project and the US 17-92 widening [10].

**Signage.** Not documented.

## 5. Gibraltar Trade Center (Michigan) — CLOSED 2014 / 2017

**History.**
- 1970: opened as an outdoor market and auction on Woodruff Rd, in Gibraltar, Michigan (hence the name) [13].
- 1980–2014: Taylor, in a former Joshua Doore furniture warehouse on Eureka Rd. It filled the building by 1982 and doubled in size in 1993 [13].
- 1990–2017: Mount Clemens, on the former **Mount Clemens Race Track** site. A vendor confirms "it was once Mount Clemens Raceway" [13][15].
- CEO/owner: Bob Koester [13][16].

**Size.**
- Sources disagree a lot: 300,000 sq ft (Wikipedia infobox), "over 600,000 sq ft" (Wikipedia text), and about 250,000 sq ft per later press on the building's reuse [13][17].
- Called one of the nation's largest indoor flea markets [13].

**Indoor vs outdoor.** Mainly one big indoor building, with some merchants along the outside walls [13].

**Hours.** Friday, Saturday and Sunday [13].

**Other events.** Gibraltar Gun & Knife Shows, plus Gibraltar Rug & Furniture outlets [13][16].

**Fees, admission and parking.** Not found online.

**Closures.**
- Taylor closed November 16, 2014; the land was sold and demolished in 2015 [13][14].
- Mount Clemens closure was announced July 2, 2017, with the last day August 27, 2017. Online shopping was blamed, and the owner said the property was "sold," not that the company was in trouble [14][16].
- Vendors called it "a family" and said shoppers came from Canada and Germany; "it's dark in there now" [15].
- The building later became a Pleasantrees cannabis dispensary and event space [13][17].
- Wikipedia mentions an unrelated "Gibraltar Trade Center 2" at the Mall of Monroe in "September 2026." It is unverified and possibly an error [13].

## 6. Rose Bowl Flea Market (Pasadena, California)

**History.**
- Founded by R.G. Canning and Bill Thunell. Spellings vary: Tunnell, Thunell [18][20].
- Canning had promoted car shows, and the partners' early Orange County markets struggled. The Rose Bowl wanted year-round income [18].
- First held in 1968. One secondary source gives November 10, 1968, with about 200 vendors and 12,000 visitors (unverified) [20].
- Run by R.G. Canning Enterprises [18].

**Size.**
- "Over 2,500 vendors" (a sell-out) selling "more than 2 million items" [18][19].
- About 20,000 buyers per event [18][19].
- Ratio: about 8 buyers per vendor.

**Outdoor.** Entirely outdoor, in the stadium parking lots and the ring around the stadium.

**Schedule.** Second Sunday of every month, rain or shine [19].

**Admission.**
- General $15 (online only, from 9am).
- VIP/early $22 (5:00–8:45am, "limited lighting… vehicles moving").
- Under 12 free.
- Cash and card sales on the day only at Gate "C" [19].
- An earlier 2026 article cited a $20 early-bird ticket [20].

**Parking.** Free, plus "preferred parking" for $20 [19].

**Vendor rules.**
- One vehicle per space, under 20 ft; a trailer needs a second space [19].
- Move-in times on the page disagree: 3–6am vs 4–6:30am. Early-bird check-in starts at 2am, and stand-by vendors move in about 7am [19].
- In-event speed limit 3 mph. No vendor food sales. Counterfeit goods are banned (LV, Dior, Marc Jacobs named) [19].
- Permanent vendors pay a $10 vendor ID and must stay "three admissions ahead" [19].
- Space rates are not published. A weak price-guide site estimates $60–120.

**Color-coded zones** (a good design hook) [19]:
- **Blue/Gold:** new merchandise and crafts (resale permit required).
- **Orange:** antiques and collectibles only.
- **Pearl/White:** vintage clothing and general goods.

**Branding.** "Flea Market of the Stars." The Travel Channel listed it among the world's 10 best [20]. The stadium itself is the visual anchor.

## 7. Brimfield Antique Flea Markets (Brimfield, Massachusetts)

**History.**
- Started by auctioneer Gordon Reid on his field "Auction Acres," which was a cow pasture [21][22].
- First-show year disagrees: 1959 (Boston Globe, 67 dealers) [21], May 1960 per the Journal of Antiques (about 300 attendees, dealers selling from station-wagon tailgates) [22].
- Billed as "Gordon Reid's Famous Flea Market" and "Greatest Antique Show Under the Sun" [21][22].
- A September show was added in about 1961 and a July week in the 1970s. By 1974 there were about 700 dealers and nearly 10,000 buyers (approx.) [22][23].
- After Reid died (1974), his daughters ran J&J Promotions. About 20 other landowners opened their own fields on the same week [21][23].

**Size.**
- 20–21 independent shows ("fields") along about one mile of Route 20 [22][23].
- 3,000–5,000+ dealers per week [22][23].
- Attendance "up to 40,000" [22]. The Boston Globe says about 50,000 for July in the 1990s, down to about 10,000 in 2014 [21].

**Outdoor.** Almost all outdoor tents in fields, plus some barns and motel grounds.

**Schedule.** Three weeks a year: May, July and September, Tuesday–Sunday. 2026 dates were May 12–17, July 14–19 and September 8–13 [24].

**Fees.**
- Each field sets its own entry: about $5–10 on opening day, often free later [24].
- A 2014 booth rental example: $600 for the week [21].
- Parking is usually paid private lots or lawns. Amounts were not confirmed.

**Layout and signage.** A main road with fields on both sides. Each field has its own entrance sign, opening "gate time," and rush at the opening ("field opening" crowds). J&J screens dealers to be "bona fide antiques dealers" [21].

**Culture and decline.**
- eBay and *Antiques Roadshow* made dealers more price-savvy. "A ghost of what it was" [21].
- Buyers shifted from collectors to decorators and "upcycling" [21].

## 8. Renninger's (Adamstown PA, Kutztown PA, Mount Dora FL, Melbourne FL)

**Operator.** Renninger's Promotions / Renninger's Marketplaces. Locations [28]:
- Kutztown: 740 Noble St.
- Adamstown: 2500 N. Reading Rd, Denver PA.
- Mount Dora: 20651 US-441.
- Melbourne: Eau Gallie Blvd.

**Adamstown.**
- Renninger's began as a farmer's market and switched to antiques [25].
- The town's "Antiques Capital USA" reputation dates to 1962, when Charles Weik started flea markets at Shupp's Grove [25]. One travel piece gives 1955 for an Adamstown farmer's market.
- Kutztown's antique market reportedly started in 1974 (weak) [26].
- Sundays only, 7:30–4 [26].
- 375+ indoor dealers in one hall, plus up to 300 outdoor (weather permitting). Outdoor rows open earlier, about 5am [26].
- "Extravaganza" Sundays three times a year (April, June, September), $10 admission [26].
- Outdoor rows run along Route 272 (N. Reading Rd) [26].

**Mount Dora.**
- 117 acres of rolling land with live oaks over the main paths [27].
- Two units on separate schedules [27]:
  - Flea Market (open-air pavilions and rows, Saturday–Sunday 8–4, "1,000+ dealers" per listing).
  - Antique Center (Friday 10–4, Saturday–Sunday 9–5). One guide describes it as about 40,000 sq ft and 180+ booths (approx.).
- Antiques & Collectors Extravaganzas in November, January and February, about 800 extra dealers. 2026 was set for November 20–22. A listing shows $4–15 admission [27][28].
- Founding date not confirmed. 1985 and early-1990s claims are unsourced.

## 9. First Monday Trade Days (Canton, Texas)

**History.**
- Dated to the 1850s court days. Circuit judges held court on the first Monday of each month, and stray horses were auctioned [29][32].
- Some call the origin legend [29]. The logo says "1850," and the 150th anniversary was held in 2023 [30].
- 1940s: hogs. Later dogs ("Dog Monday") [29].
- About 5,000 people came by 1950. Residents rented out yards and bathrooms [29].
- 1965: a designated 6.5-acre trading ground north of the courthouse square. The city later bought more land [29].
- Henry Lewis built the "Original Pavilions" in 1988–90 [29].
- Until 2006, Canton levied no city property tax, thanks to market revenue [29].

**Size.**
- City-owned grounds of 100+ acres, plus private markets next door [29].
- Secret Dallas says about 450 acres in all [31].
- Vendors: 3,000+ vs 6,000 [29]; 5,000+ [31].
- Attendance: up to 100,000 per weekend [29]; 620,000+ a year [31].
- Ratio: about 30–60 vendors per acre on the city grounds.

**Schedule.** Thursday–Sunday before the first Monday of every month [29].

**Layout.** Named zones: Original Pavilions I–III, The Porch, Walkway by the Creek, Civic Center, Trade Centers I–IV, Canton Marketplace (climate-controlled, 300+ vendors), Old Mill Marketplace, The Village, The Mountain, The Arbors, Dog Alley, Row 46 Building, and a Log Cabin [29][30]. Pavilion spaces are reserved year-round, and unreserved areas are first come, first served [29].

**Fees.**
- About $200 per weekend for an indoor booth (2014) [29].
- Parking about $5 in private lots. Canton residents park free with a water bill [29][33].

**Culture.** Free trolley, scooter rentals, an RV park. Tornadoes hit just before market days in 2008, 2017 and 2019 [29].

## 10. Shipshewana Flea Market (Indiana)

**History** [34]:
- 1922: George Curtis held livestock sales at his home.
- 1947: Fred Lambright built a new sale barn. People sold from car trunks, and a roof went over the flea area that year.
- 1961: Walter Schrock bought it. 1968: grounds expanded from 100 to 400 vendors.
- 1978: antique auction building (ten auctioneers at once). 1979: barn fire, rebuilt by volunteers in about three months.
- 1981: Lambright family; 1,000 vendor spaces.
- Later: Farmstead Inn (1997), RV park (2008, 165 sites), Event Pavilion (2012), Expo Barn (2017).

**Size.**
- "600+ vendor spaces" / "nearly 700 vendors" [35][36].
- 40+ acres [36]. A third-party listing says 100 acres (unverified).
- 10,000–12,000 visitors a week in season [36].
- Ratio: about 15–17 vendors per acre.

**Mostly outdoor.** Some vendors rent season-long buildings or "big-top tents," and some booths sit in the Antique Auction Building [35][36].

**Schedule.** Tuesday and Wednesday, May–September, 8–4, plus holiday weekends. The Wednesday auction runs year-round [34][35].

**Fees.**
- $45 a day or $65 a week for a 25×20 ft space with one 20A outlet.
- $207–242 a month plus a $90 deposit.
- An older rate sheet said $35 a day [35].

**Admission and parking.** Free entry; parking $5 cash [36].

**Layout.** Themed vendor "trails" (Flea Bag Trail scavenger hunt), a gem-mining sluice, the Auction Restaurant at the SR 5 gate, and pavilion food courts [36].

**Culture.** Amish and Mennonite sellers and buggies. Food: Port-A-Pit chicken, Amish peanut butter, pretzels [36].

## 11. Englishtown Auction (Manalapan, New Jersey)

**History.**
- Opened in 1929 as a farmers' trading ground for equipment, produce and livestock [37][39].
- Sobechko family ownership, third generation (per a weak real-estate source; a Patch post names owner "Jimmy Sobecko") [40].

**Size.** Over 40 acres; several hundred vendors, about 300 indoors in five large buildings [37]. "Over 300 vendors" in the directory [39].

**Outdoor.** Includes sunny dirt lots [39].

**Hours.** Saturday and Sunday 8–4, rain or shine, including Easter Sunday and Black Friday [38].

**Rules.** Designer goods (new or used) and CBD/THC are banned [38].

**Fees and admission.** Not found.

**Parking.** Across from the market floors [39].

**Culture.** Described as like a "barrio flea market." Small weekend bar, bakery, produce; tailoring and vacuum repair [37][39].

## 12. Swap-O-Rama (Chicago area; also Florida)

**History.**
- The company says it was established in 1969 [41]. Others say 1969 as a response to the Union Stock Yards closing.
- WTTW, citing the company, says the Midwest start was 1973 and the Ashland site came in 1992 [44].
- A 1973 Sun-Times photo shows it at the International Amphitheatre [45].

**Location.** Ashland Ave at 41st/42nd, in Back of the Yards on former stockyards land [41][43].

**Other sites.** Alsip (Tri-State), Melrose Park, and Webster and Homosassa in Florida [41].

**Fees (2026)** [42]:
- Ashland: outdoor $45 Saturday/Sunday, $30 Thursday, $20 Tuesday (April–November); indoor $35/$38. A City of Chicago business license is required.
- Alsip: $27–37, plus $3 village license.
- Melrose Park: $29–32, plus $1.

**Admission (per South Side Weekly).** $1 weekdays, $2 weekends; half price for children and seniors [43].

**Hours.** Tuesday (April–November) and Thursday 7–3; Saturday–Sunday 7–4 [43].

**Layout.** Outdoor stalls along narrow tent avenues. On weekends indoor halls open on the same grid ("feels like a maze") and the market roughly doubles in size [43].

**Culture.**
- Mostly Spanish-speaking and Latino, with Asian and Black vendors too [43][44].
- Boots are the most common item. Vendors sell storage-locker finds, overstock and haircuts [43].
- After the New Maxwell Street Market relocations, Maxwell-style taco stands made it "the new hot taco spot" [46].
- Attendance has not recovered from the pandemic [46].

## 13. Columbus, Ohio: Westland Flea Market and South Drive-In

**Westland Flea Market** (4170 W. Broad St, near I-270).
- Opened in 1992 inside the former Lincoln Lanes bowling alley [47].
- About 50,000 sq ft indoor plus outdoor space, about 300 vendors, Friday–Sunday [47].
- Sells cowboy hats, Jordans and electronics.
- The neighboring Westland Mall (1969) was demolished from 2023. I found no source on whether the flea market is still open, so treat its status as unverified.
- Good "repurposed bowling alley" archetype.

**South Drive-In** (3050 S. High St).
- Opened May 11, 1950 by the Yassenoff family. Driveinmovie.com says 1957 instead, which disagrees [48][49].
- Car capacity grew from 500 to 1,100. Two screens from 1979; the 1972 steel screen fell in a 1973 tornado [48].
- A weekend flea market has run on the lot since the mid-1970s; one listing says Wednesday, Saturday and Sunday from March to November [47][49].
- Classic "drive-in by day = swap meet" setup: vendors park in the speaker-post rows.

## 14. Hartville MarketPlace & Flea Market (Ohio)

**History.** Began as a livestock auction in 1939 and moved down the street to its current site in 2002 [50][51]. Founders not named; the Coblentz family attribution is not confirmed.

**Size.**
- 12+ acres, including 3 acres of indoor shopping [50].
- The indoor MarketPlace is about 100,000 sq ft (2007) [51].
- Vendor counts disagree: 60–110+ permanent indoor shops; two covered pavilions with up to 200 vendors (weak sources).

**Layout.**
- Indoor permanent shops are "mall-like," decorated by tenants [51].
- Outdoor flea and covered pavilions run May–September. Outdoor vendors arrive about 7am to claim spots [51].
- Hartville Kitchen restaurant (440 seats, famous coleslaw and pies) is across the parking lot [50][51].

**Hours (2007).** Monday, Thursday, Friday (to 8pm) and Saturday [51].

**Admission and parking.** Not documented.

**Events.** Coin, toy and card shows; fall craft festival [50].

## 15. Rogers Community Auction & Flea Market (Rogers, Ohio)

**History.**
- Founded July 1, 1955 (some say July 4) by auctioneer Emmett (Emmet) and Lucille Baer [52][53].
- Started with one building on 12 acres, with 15 vendors selling eggs, produce and small animals [52].
- Vendors set up around the auction barn to catch auction crowds [53].
- Son Jim added pavilions, indoor buildings and a poultry barn [53]. The third and fourth generations run it now [52][53].

**Size.**
- About 250 acres and 16 buildings [52].
- Vendor spaces disagree: 1,200+ [53], 1,400+ [52], 1,600+ with 350 covered [54].
- Five miles of aisles [53].
- About 30,000 visitors on a "slow Friday" [53].
- Ratio: about 5–6 vendors per acre over the whole site; much denser on the selling ground itself.

**Parking.** 70 acres free. A photo caption says "1,400+ spaces," which seems low for 70 acres, so treat it as approx. [52][53][54].

**Schedule.** Friday flea market and auctions (household, antiques, produce, livestock). Wednesday hay and grain auction. Second Tuesday equipment consignment [54].

**Admission.** Free, except $6 for the "Backyard LIVE!" entertainment area [52].

**Fees (weak, undated).** Outdoor $23–24; 12×12 pavilion $26; building space $30 [55].

**Incidents.** 2014 ICE counterfeit raid ($500k seized); 2019 pavilion hay fire [54].

## 17. Fort Lauderdale Swap Shop (Florida)

**History.**
- Betty and Preston Henn opened the Thunderbird Drive-In on November 22, 1963, with one screen [57].
- Henn added a flea market after a 1966 trip west. Jalopnik says the idea came from a swap meet at a Compton, CA drive-in [57][58].
- Early on, a fence divided the lot by race [57].
- 11 screens by 1980; 14 screens by 2005 [57].
- The drive-in closed in 2023 after a suspicious fire burned screen 5. The flea market continues [57].
- Henn died in 2017 [60].
- Sister sites: Margate (closed 2007), Tampa Fun-Lan (closed 2021), Lake Worth (closing September 2025) [57].

**Size.**
- Acreage disagrees: 80 [57], 88 [drive-ins listings], 9 acres in 1963 growing to 30+ by 1985 [58].
- About 2,000 vendors (Miami New Times / Culture Trip) [59].
- Wikipedia's "12 million visitors a year" estimate is very likely inflated (approx.) [57].
- Ratio: about 25 vendors per acre.

**Indoor vs outdoor.** Open daily. There is an air-conditioned indoor hall and food court; the outdoor flea spills into the parking lot [57][59].

**Admission.** Free (weak source).

**Branding and attractions.**
- Henn wanted "the Disney World of flea markets" [58].
- Food court opened in 1979, enclosed with a stage about 1989. Acts included Willie Nelson, Loretta Lynn and KC & the Sunshine Band [57].
- The **Hanneford Family Circus** played daily in the food court from 1989 to 2005/06, with elephants, tigers, acrobats and clowns [57][58].
- The circus was replaced by a display of Henn's **exotic and race-car collection** (valued at over $100M), including a "Swap Shops"-decaled Ferrari FXX [58].
- Henn painted entrance signs **fluorescent orange** to annoy local aesthetics boards [58]. He wore ads on his necktie, and his race Porsche won the 1983 Daytona 24 Hours [58][60].

## 18. Raleigh Flea Market / The Raleigh Market (NC State Fairgrounds)

**History.** Started in 1971 with six tables [61]. A weak source names the Stewart family. The market now calls itself "North Carolina's largest flea market" [62].

**Size.**
- 20,000 sq ft indoor/outdoor (this figure seems low) [61].
- 500–600+ vendors [61][62].
- Up to 2.4 million shoppers a year (approx., a promotional claim) [61].
- In the 1980s it reportedly had about 1,000 vendors across six buildings (weak).

**Hours.** Saturday–Sunday 9–5, rain or shine. Historically closed in October for the State Fair [61][62].

**Admission and parking.** Both free; Gate 9, 4285 Trinity Rd [62].

**Culture.** Food trucks, fair food, farm stands. Goods range from velvet Elvis to fishing lures, leather and turquoise [61][62].

## 19. Nashville Flea Market (The Fairgrounds Nashville)

**History.** Founded 1969 [63].

**Size.**
- Today: over 300 vendors from 30 states and about 500,000 visitors a year [63].
- 1996 figures: about 2,300 booths on 6.5 acres in **nine buildings and six sheds**; up to 250,000 per weekend and 1.3M a year ("outdraws the Opry") [64].
- The fairgrounds made about $1M a year from booths [64].
- WKRN (2025): 204 vendors in 500+ booths for one month [65].

**Schedule.** Disagrees: "fourth weekend" historically [64] vs "third weekend, excluding December" (2025) [65].

**Fees (1996).** $45–72.50 per weekend [64].

**Admission and parking.** Free admission; neighbors rented overflow parking for $3 (1996) [64].

**Layout.** Moved from the old hilltop buildings into new fairgrounds facilities [66]. Wrestling at the arena was cancelled on market weekends to free booth space. About a third antiques, a third crafts, a third miscellaneous [64].

## 21. San Jose: Berryessa Flea Market ("La Pulga") and the Drive-In Swap Meets

**Berryessa Flea Market / San Jose Flea Market.**
- Opened March 1960 on the site of a former meat-packing plant [69][70].
- Founders: George Bumb Sr. (with a waste and landfill background, he wanted to resell discards), Lawrence Headrick and Joe Kokes [69][70].
- Produce stands came first, and Latino farmers gave it its "La Pulga" identity [70].
- **Size:**
  - Total site about 120 acres (a 57-acre north parking area plus a 63-acre south market) [69].
  - Market itself about 60 acres [70].
  - Vendors: 2,400 vendors and 4M visitors a year in 1987; "over 2,000" later; about 400 a week (KQED 2021); the vendor association represents 450+ [69][70][71].
- **Layout:**
  - 8 miles of aisles; a **quarter-mile Produce Row** (24 stands burned in 2006) [69].
  - Two stages, one for mariachi [69].
  - Vintage carousel, arcade, three playgrounds, carnival rides, Mexican/American restaurants [69].
- **Closure fight:**
  - 2007 rezoning for housing [69].
  - The 2021 Berryessa BART Urban Village plan (3,450 homes, a 5-acre indoor "urban market") was later withdrawn and replaced by a 940-home builder's-remedy plan [70][71].
  - The market can stay open until at least January 1, 2026. There is a $5M Bumb plus $2M city transition fund [71].
  - **Current status after January 2026 not confirmed.**

**Capitol Drive-In / Capitol Flea Market.**
- Opened May 25, 1971 as "the world's largest drive-in complex," with 4 screens, later 6. Architect Vincent G. Raney. Operated by Syufy/Century, then West Wind (2010–) [72][73].
- Daytime flea market since about 1978 (single source) and about 1.3M visitors a year (approx.) [86].
- Now "Capitol Drive-In & Public Market" [72].

**Spartan Drive-In** (First & Alma).
- Opened as the Shamrock in 1950 and reopened as the Spartan in 1955 [74].
- Closed in 1975 or 1976 (sources disagree). The site is now offices [74].
- **I found no evidence the Spartan hosted a swap meet.** The San Jose drive-in swap meet is the Capitol.

## 23. Cross-cutting summary for game design

### Origins (ranked by how often they appear)
1. **Livestock or produce auctions** that drew trunk sellers: Shipshewana (1922/1947), Hartville (1939), Rogers (1955), Englishtown (1929), Renninger's (farmers' market), Gibraltar (auction house). Typical sign: an auction barn at the center, with the restaurant beside it.
2. **Court or market days:** First Monday (1850s). Usually municipal, with named pavilions added over time.
3. **Drive-in theatres:** Swap Shop (1963/66), South Drive-In (1970s), Capitol (1978), Mustang (Pinellas). Daytime vendors set up between speaker posts, the screens loom overhead, and the movie marquee doubles as the market sign.
4. **Reused large sites:** racetrack (Gibraltar Mount Clemens), stadium lot (Rose Bowl), naval runway (Alameda), stockyards (Swap-O-Rama), bowling alley (Westland), furniture warehouse (Gibraltar Taylor), meat plant (Berryessa).
5. **Fairgrounds rentals:** Nashville, Raleigh, Kane County. These are monthly or weekend markets in exhibit halls plus sheds.
6. **Founder entrepreneurs on cheap highway land:** Trader's World (by I-75), Flea World (US 17-92), Wagon Wheel (a used-car lot), Traders Village.

### Physical layout patterns
- **A core of permanent indoor shops** (mall-like, air-conditioned, tenant-decorated) **surrounded by a daily outdoor ring**: Trader's World (68/32), Englishtown, Hartville, Red Barn, Canton Marketplace.
- **Open-sided pavilion sheds** in long parallel rows. Rogers has 350 of its approx. 1,600 spaces covered (approx. 22%). Nashville had nine buildings and six sheds.
- **Grid aisles** of tents. Swap-O-Rama's indoor halls copy the outdoor grid. La Pulga has 8 miles of aisles; Rogers has 5 miles of aisles on about 250 acres.
- **Named zones and districts:** Canton's pavilions, Rose Bowl's color zones, Shipshewana's "trails," Berryessa's Produce Row.
- **Typical space sizes:**
  - Outdoor: 20×25 ft (Shipshewana, with your car on the space) or about 12×12 ft (Rogers pavilions).
  - Rose Bowl: one vehicle under 20 ft per space.
- **Anchors** placed at the gate: a restaurant (Hartville Kitchen, Shipshewana Auction Restaurant), a food court with a stage, a beer garden, amusement rides (Fun World, Traders Village, La Pulga carousel), and once a circus (Swap Shop).

### Typical ratios (approx., derived from the cited numbers)

| Ratio | Range | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Vendors per gross acre, sprawling outdoor sites | 5–30 (typically 12–25) | Rogers 5–6, Traders Village about 12, Red Barn about 12, Wagon Wheel 12–20, Flea World about 16, Shipshewana about 15, Swap Shop about 25, La Pulga about 33 on its 60 market acres |
| Spaces per acre, dense indoor/outdoor markets | 100–350 | Trader's World about 110; Nashville in 1996 about 350 (buildings only) |
| Visitors per vendor per market day | about 8–25 | Rose Bowl about 8; Rogers about 20–25 on a slow Friday |
| Parking land share | about 25–50% of the site | Rogers 70 of 250 acres (28%); Berryessa about 57 of 120 acres (about 48%) |

- A design rule of thumb: plan **1 vendor vehicle per space plus 3–10 customer car spaces per vendor**. At a general surface-lot density of about 100–140 cars per acre (an engineering norm, not from these sources), that is about 1 acre of parking per 15–40 vendors.

### Money (typical ranges)
- **Daily outdoor vendor rent:** about $20–45 (Swap-O-Rama $20–45; Rogers $23–30; Shipshewana $45).
- **Weekend indoor booth:** about $35–200 (Canton about $200 in 2014; Nashville $45–72.50 in 1996).
- **Monthly permanent space:** about $200–250 (Shipshewana).
- **Admission:**
  - Usually **free** at weekly working-class markets: Rogers, Raleigh, Nashville, Traders Village, Wagon Wheel, Red Barn.
  - **$1–2** at urban swap meets (Swap-O-Rama).
  - **$5–22** at curated antiques events: Kane $7, Renninger's Extravaganza $10, Rose Bowl $15/$22, Brimfield fields $5–10.
- **Parking:** free or **about $5** (Canton, Shipshewana, Traders Village). Preferred parking about $20 (Rose Bowl). Neighbors rent lawns when lots fill (Canton, Nashville).

### Days and hours
- **Weekly weekend markets:** Saturday–Sunday about 8/9am–4/5pm.
- **Rural markets often use one weekday tied to the old auction day:** Rogers Friday; Shipshewana Tuesday–Wednesday.
- **Antiques events are monthly or seasonal with very early starts:** Rose Bowl VIP 5am; Renninger's outdoor 5am; Alameda 6am; Brimfield field openings.
- "**Rain or shine**" is a near-universal slogan.

### Signage and branding (only partly documented)
- **Superlative slogans everywhere:** "Midwest's Largest & Most Colorful," "Nation's largest under one roof," "America's #1 Marketplace," "Greatest Antique Show Under the Sun," "First Name in Flea Markets," "Flea Market of the Stars," "Disney World of flea markets."
- **Roadside giant objects and kitsch:** Trader's World's red rearing horse, colored palm trees, fiberglass giraffes and the Matilda statue; the Swap Shop's fluorescent-orange signs, circus and supercar display; drive-in screens and marquees.
- **Painted barns:** the Big Red Barn in Aromas, Red Barn Bradenton.
- **Venue landmarks used as identity:** the Rose Bowl stadium, Alameda's skyline views, Canton's Log Cabin.
- Wagon Wheel's giant wheel and specific neon or arch designs: **not confirmed by any source.**

### Naming patterns
- **Trader/Trade:** Trader's World, Traders Village, Gibraltar Trade Center, First Monday Trade Days, Shipshewana Trading Place. Echoes barter and frontier commerce.
- **World/Village/Center/Marketplace/Place:** scale and civic sound (Flea World, Traders Village, Hartville MarketPlace, Canton Marketplace).
- **Rural object:** Barn (Red Barn), Wheel (Wagon Wheel), Mustang (a drive-in name), Thunderbird (a drive-in name). Western and car-culture words came over from drive-ins.
- **Swap:** Swap Shop, Swap-O-Rama ("-O-Rama" is 1950s–70s showbiz style), "swap meet" (the West Coast term, especially at drive-ins).
- **Place plus event type:** Kane County Flea Market, Englishtown Auction, Rogers Community Auction, Nashville Flea Market, Alameda Point Antiques Faire ("Faire" is antique-styled spelling).
- **Calendar names:** First Monday, Trade Days, Extravaganza, "Second Sunday."
- **Founder or family names:** Renninger's, (Gordon) Reid's, Shupp's Grove.
- **Spanish nicknames** from immigrant vendors: La Pulga (San Jose); "Little TJ" for a Los Angeles-area drive-in swap meet.

### Recurring culture and story beats
- Multi-generation family ownership (Baers, Huntleys, Bumbs, Lambrights, Sobechkos).
- Immigrant and Latino vendor communities (La Pulga, Swap-O-Rama, Englishtown).
- Auctions as the heart of rural markets.
- Food courts with stages; beer gardens.
- Counterfeit raids (Rogers 2014; Rose Bowl and Englishtown bans).
- Fires and next-day reopenings (Wagon Wheel 1986, Shipshewana 1979, Berryessa 2006, Rogers 2019).
- **Decline drivers:** eBay and online sales (Brimfield, Gibraltar); land value and redevelopment (Flea World → Reagan Center, Berryessa → BART village, Gibraltar Taylor); COVID (Wagon Wheel); vendor exodus (Flea World).
- Successor "diaspora" markets in parking lots of other venues (Wagon Wheel → Largo → Derby Lane).
