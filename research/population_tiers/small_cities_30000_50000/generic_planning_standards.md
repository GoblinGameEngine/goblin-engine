# Generic American small-city planning/zoning standards (cross-city reference)

These are not tied to one city but are broadly-applicable planning-literature figures used
to fill gaps where a specific city's ordinance wasn't fully extractable, and to
cross-check the per-city numbers found. Used as the backbone of `_tier_summary.md`.

## City block size
- "Standard" square block widely cited by urban planners: roughly **300-330 ft per side**
  (Mobile AL, Tulsa OK, and Anchorage AK are all cited as sharing a 300 ft x 300 ft
  internal block despite very different settlement eras). Chicago downtown blocks average
  about 330 ft. Portland's short blocks (~200-260 ft) and Salt Lake City's oversized blocks
  (~660 ft, designed for wagon turning radius) are the two outlier extremes — small
  Midwestern county-seat towns platted in the 19th century typically land close to the
  300-330 ft standard rather than either extreme.
- Typical city block length range overall: **300-660 ft**, with 311 ft (~1/17 mile) cited
  as a common "standard square block" reference figure.

## Street widths
- **Local residential street, curb-to-curb: 28 ft is the widely-cited minimum** for
  two-way traffic plus one side of on-street parking (20 ft for two-way travel + 8 ft for
  parking). Many communities require wider (32-40 ft); "traffic-calming" advocates argue
  22-26 ft is sufficient in low-density residential settings without sacrificing emergency
  access.
- **Local street right-of-way (property-line to property-line, including sidewalks/
  verges): typically 50-65 ft**, wider in commercial/industrial areas for truck traffic.
- **Collector street right-of-way: ~66 ft** typical; where a collector meets an arterial,
  an 80 ft right-of-way is sometimes required within 300 ft of the intersection.
- **Arterial street right-of-way: 60-100+ ft**; principal arterials in some jurisdictions
  reserve up to 200 ft.
- **Travel lane width (not full street width): arterial ~12 ft/lane; collector ~11
  ft/lane** typical (AASHTO-influenced rural/small-city standards).

## Parking ratios
- ITE-referenced standard retail parking ratio: **3.0-5.0 spaces per 1,000 sq ft** of
  retail floor area (some sources cite 4-5 spaces/1,000 sq ft specifically for shopping
  centers). "Investment grade" commercial parking ratios broadly cited at 5-10
  spaces/1,000 sq ft including circulation/overflow.

## Arterial-vs-core commercial siting logic
- Early 20th-century (1920s-30s) zoning ordinances specifically created linear commercial
  strip zoning along arterial roads/highways, deliberately separated from both residential
  zones and the pedestrian-oriented downtown core — the origin of the now-familiar
  Midwest small-city pattern of big-box/auto-oriented retail lining the highway/arterial
  bypass while pedestrian storefront retail stays downtown.
- Rationale given in planning literature: highway-commercial districts are meant for
  businesses that function independently of pedestrian foot traffic and don't need
  proximity to other storefronts — they need vehicle visibility/access instead, hence
  large parcels (2+ acres cited as a minimum in one example ordinance) directly fronting a
  thoroughfare.
- Documented downside/tradeoff: each big-box store located adjacent to an arterial is
  associated with a measurable increase in crash incidence (one source cites 6.6% increase
  in total crashes per adjacent big-box store) due to multiple curb cuts/driveways
  increasing conflict points — a real-world constraint that keeps this format off local
  streets and concentrated on arterials specifically.

## Riverfront/waterfront property value pattern
- Investment in riverfront parks measurably raises adjacent property values: Pittsburgh's
  13-mile riverfront park investment (multi-decade, multi-million-dollar) is associated
  with a 60% property value increase near the riverfront 2001-2015, vs. 32% citywide over
  the same period — roughly double the appreciation rate.
- A cited private-to-public investment ratio for riverfront redevelopment: **$20 of
  private investment for every $1 of public riverfront/open-space investment** (large multi-
  city dataset, not small-city-specific, but indicates the general leverage effect once a
  waterfront amenity project is credible).
- General Rust Belt/Midwest small-city pattern: **declining industrial use of a river or
  lake almost always precedes a park/promenade/mixed-use redevelopment of the same
  frontage** — no example found in this tier of a downtown river/lake edge that skipped
  the industrial phase and went straight to park use; the industrial-to-amenity
  transition appears to be the dominant, near-universal sequence for this city-size tier
  in the Midwest.
