# Danville, Illinois (pop. 29,204, 2020 census — just under this tier's floor, included
for regional variety and because it functions at this scale)

Vermilion County seat, eastern Illinois near the Indiana border, at the junction of the
forks of the Vermilion River (Salt Fork, Middle Fork, North Fork). Elevation 637 ft.

## Downtown / commercial core layout
- "Danville River District" (per Wikipedia) is the recognized name for the
  downtown-adjacent riverfront area — several historic landmark buildings cluster on North
  Vermilion Street downtown, including the 1911 United States Post Office and Court House
  (federal Eastern District of Illinois courthouse) — i.e. downtown includes a federal
  civic anchor building in addition to commercial storefronts, typical of county seats that
  also host federal courts.
- Rezoning activity noted near downtown: conversion of North Vermilion St. properties (just
  north of Fairchild) from residential to professional/office use as converted homes were
  increasingly used for businesses — a common downtown-fringe transition pattern (houses
  adjacent to the CBD gradually convert to offices as commercial pressure radiates outward
  from downtown).

## Water proximity / property value pattern
- Downtown sits near the confluence of three forks of the Vermilion River — a multi-fork
  confluence town, similar in structural logic to Mankato's two-river confluence, though
  the river here is smaller/rockier (the Vermilion is a state-designated Scenic River with
  exposed shale/limestone banks in places) rather than a major shipping river.
- "Danville River District" branding suggests active civic effort to reposition river
  frontage as a downtown amenity/identity asset, consistent with the broader
  industrial-to-amenity riverfront trend seen across this tier.

## Topography / drainage
- Surrounding Vermilion County terrain is mostly flat glacial till/silt-loam plain, but the
  county contains over 15,000 acres of parkland across six county/state parks — notably
  more preserved natural land than the flattest farm towns in this tier (Bowling Green),
  likely because the multi-fork river confluence and its associated bluffs/ravines created
  terrain unsuitable for row-crop farming and thus preserved as parkland instead. This is a
  useful generator rule: **where rivers cut ravines/bluffs through otherwise flat farm
  country, expect clustered parkland rather than continuous farmland**, right up to the
  edge of the built-up area.
