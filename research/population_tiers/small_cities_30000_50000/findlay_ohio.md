# Findlay, Ohio (pop. 40,313, 2020 census)

Hancock County seat, northwest Ohio, on the Blanchard River (of "Down by the Old Mill
Stream" fame — written here by Tell Taylor). An inland, non-Great-Lakes river town with
active recent flood-mitigation work.

## Downtown / commercial core layout
- Central Business District designation (B-3) exists distinct from general commercial
  (B-2) and light industrial (I-1) zoning; example rezonings found: land north of Main
  Cross St./south of the Blanchard River moved from I-1 (Light Industrial) to B-1 (Local
  Business) in 1997, and downtown-adjacent lots moved from B-2 (General Business) to B-3
  (Central Business District) in 2006 — i.e. **the CBD designation is treated as its own
  more-intense/more-permissive zone even within "commercial," separate from general
  arterial commercial strip zoning** (B-2), consistent with the broader pattern of a
  distinct downtown-core zoning tier used across this dataset.
- Industrial-zoned land sits immediately adjacent to the river north of downtown —
  consistent with the historical pattern of siting industry directly on river frontage
  (water for process/cooling/transport, floodplain land being cheaper), with commercial/
  retail uses layered in as those industrial parcels are rezoned over time.

## Water proximity / property value pattern
- The Blanchard River runs through/adjacent to downtown and has a history of serious
  flooding; the city has an active FEMA Letter of Map Revision (LOMR) process tied to
  Phase I "benching" (channel modification) of the river — i.e. **downtown flood risk from
  the river is an ongoing, actively-engineered concern**, not a solved historical problem,
  distinguishing Findlay from towns with a completed static floodwall (Cape Girardeau).

## Topography / drainage
- Findlay sits in the historic footprint of the Great Black Swamp region of northwest
  Ohio (see also Bowling Green, OH in this tier) — originally poorly-drained, flat glacial
  lake-plain terrain, artificially drained in the 19th century via ditches feeding rivers
  like the Blanchard. This produces a flood-prone, engineered-drainage flat-plains
  character distinct from the natural-relief bluff towns elsewhere in this dataset.
