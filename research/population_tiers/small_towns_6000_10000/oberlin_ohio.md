# Oberlin, Ohio (Lorain County)

Population: 8,555 (2020 census), up 3.2% from 8,286 (2010) — modest growth, with a 2021 estimate (8,249) suggesting a brief pullback before recovering to ~8,587 by 2024. Land area 4.96 sq mi. Located in Lorain County, about 31 miles southwest of Cleveland, roughly 10 miles south of the Lake Erie shore in the Erie watershed — inland from the lake itself but within its snowbelt/climate influence ("tempered by the Great Lakes"). Founded 1833 by Presbyterian ministers John Jay Shipherd and Philo P. Stewart, who secured 500 donated acres plus 5,000 additional acres at $1.50/acre for the combined town-and-college venture. Home to **Oberlin College**, a liberal arts college and conservatory of roughly 3,000 students — meaning the town's *de facto* daytime/resident population is materially larger than its census figure once students are counted. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oberlin,_Ohio)

## Core layout / Downtown-Main Street layout
Oberlin is a genuine **college-town founding**: unlike every other settlement in this research set, the town and the institution were planned together from 1833 as a single venture, not a college added later to an existing settlement. The historic center of both town and campus is **Tappan Square**, a 13-acre former-campus-turned-shared-public-park (est. 1885, funded by a bequest from Alcoa founder Charles Martin Hall) — this functions as Oberlin's equivalent of a courthouse square or village green, except it is owned/anchored by the college rather than the county, and it hosts both town events (Juneteenth) and college events (commencement) with no fence or gate separating the two populations. Downtown retail (the Gibson's Bakery block and similar storefronts) sits directly adjacent to Tappan Square, meaning the commercial core and the college green are effectively the same public space rather than being separated by campus walls — a distinctive "no town/gown boundary" layout not present in any of the existing 36 towns. Notable buildings ringing or near the square include Peters Hall (administration), Finney Chapel, and the Allen Memorial Art Museum (the first college art museum west of the Alleghenies). ## Residential pattern

Given the college's ~3,000 students are housed substantially in campus dormitories/college-owned housing rather than the general rental market, expect the town's off-campus residential fabric to more closely resemble a tier-typical small-town grid (single-family homes, modest lots) than a dense student-apartment district, though a ring of rental housing immediately around campus for upperclassmen/staff is plausible. Homeownership is comparatively low at 58.2% of occupied units (2024, DataUSA) — lower than any of the other three new Great Lakes towns except Benton Harbor, consistent with a renter-heavy population mix of students, junior faculty, and staff. Functionally expect three zones: the shared college-campus/Tappan-Square/downtown core; surrounding in-town residential blocks; and outlying rural/agricultural land typical of Lorain County exurbs. Oberlin's role as the birthplace of the Anti-Saloon League and a historic Underground Railroad station (site of the 1858 Oberlin-Wellington Rescue) reflects a long civic-institutional identity layered onto the town plan, though this is historical rather than a current physical-layout driver.

## Water — founding/water relationship
Oberlin is **not** a water-founded town in the mill-dam or harbor sense seen elsewhere in this research set — it was sited for land availability and planned-community purposes, not for water power or shipping access. It sits inland in the Lake Erie watershed (about 10 miles south of the shore), tempered climatically by the Great Lakes but with no harbor, navigable river, or mill-founding history identified. The one specific water event found is a 1924 flood severe enough that "the water was so deep that children swam in Tappan Square," indicating a local drainage/flood-prone low point at the town's historic center, but this is incidental rather than founding-related. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oberlin,_Ohio)

## Topography/drainage
General till-plain topography typical of inland Lorain County; the 1924 Tappan Square flood (above) suggests the historic town center sits at a local low point with at least episodic drainage/flooding issues, but no systematic floodplain or creek data was retrieved in this pass (flagged gap).

## Distinctive buildings
- Tappan Square (13 acres) — shared town/college green, with the Clark Bandstand and Memorial Arch (1903, commemorating Boxer Rebellion missionaries) as its only remaining structures
- Peters Hall — Oberlin College administration building
- Finney Chapel
- Allen Memorial Art Museum — first college art museum west of the Alleghenies (building by Cass Gilbert)
- Oberlin Lake Shore and Michigan Southern Railroad Station — converted historic rail depot, now in a park setting
- Gibson's Bakery block — downtown storefront directly adjacent to Tappan Square

## Demographics
- **Population trend**: growing modestly, +3.2% 2010→2020 (8,286 → 8,555); a 2021 estimate (8,249) suggested a brief dip, with 2024 estimates (~8,587) showing recovery to roughly flat/slow growth overall. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oberlin,_Ohio) / [City-Data](http://www.city-data.com/city/Oberlin-Ohio.html)
- **Median household income**: $81,308-$87,476 (2024 estimate, City-Data/DataUSA) — a strong jump from prior years (~10% YoY growth cited), likely reflecting both regional income growth and the college's institutional economic weight.
- **Median age**: 28.0 (2020 census), ~27.3-27.4 (2024 estimate) — by far the youngest of the four new Great Lakes towns, and markedly younger than any town in the existing 36-sample, driven directly by the ~3,000-student college population within a total population of ~8,555-8,587.
- **% under 18**: 12.3% (2020 census) — notably low for the age skew, since the college-age population (18-22) dominates rather than families with children.
- **% 65+**: 22.4% (2020 census).
- **Homeownership rate**: 58.2% of occupied housing units (2024, DataUSA) — the lowest of the three Michigan/Ohio villages/small towns except Benton Harbor, consistent with a substantial renter population of students, junior faculty, and staff.
- **Average household size**: not found in this pass (flagged gap); likely lower than a typical small town given the student/renter population mix.
- **Top employment sectors**: educational services (28.9%, ~1,097 employees) — by far the dominant sector, reflecting the college's role as the town's primary employer — followed by health care & social assistance (12.5%, ~475 employees) and accommodation & food services (10.8%, ~410 employees). This educational-services dominance is a defining signature of the college-town archetype, absent from every other town profiled in this research set.
- **Poverty rate**: 16.1% (2024) — elevated above the national average (12.5%); plausibly inflated as a statistical artifact of college towns generally (many low-income-on-paper students counted as town residents), a pattern worth flagging distinctly from genuine economic-decline poverty (see Benton Harbor, below).
- **% bachelor's+**: not found in numeric form in this pass (flagged gap), but given Oberlin College's presence and the educational-services employment dominance, the town's overall educational attainment is very likely well above the Midwest small-town norm.
