## Numeric parameters for the generator

### Downtown / Main Street
- **Main Street / primary commercial-spine width: ~90-100 ft** right-of-way (curb-to-curb + walk), vs. **~60-66 ft for ordinary grid streets**. Confirmed hard figure: Bryan, OH plats Main & High St at exactly 100 ft, all other streets at 66 ft (66 ft = 1 chain, the standard 19th-c. US platting unit). *Why:* wide commercial streets needed room for two-way traffic plus angled parking on both sides plus wide sidewalks; 66 ft residential streets follow the near-universal chain-based rural/town platting convention. **Rule of thumb: primary commercial street = ~1.5x ordinary grid street width.**
- **Block length: ~300-400 ft** (standard for Midwest courthouse-square and railroad-town grids; also the widely-cited "300 ft x 300 ft" American block norm, though 300-600 ft range appears elsewhere in the literature). *Why:* walkable block length balancing land-parcel efficiency with pedestrian cross-street frequency; consistent with 100-address-numbers-per-block convention observed in McCook.
- **Individual commercial lot width: ~25 ft** (confirmed as a standard historic platting unit in multiple sources, e.g., Park City UT ordinance citing 25 ft as the traditional commercial lot width; storefront facade "bays" are typically divided into 25-30 ft segments even when buildings combine multiple lots). Lot depth: **~50-100+ ft** (Park City ordinance cites 50 ft minimum commercial lot depth as a comparable figure).
- **Building height: overwhelmingly 1-2 stories**, occasional 3-story anchor buildings (opera houses, hotels, courthouses) — every deep-dive town's downtown fabric is described as "1-2 story brick/masonry."
- **Setback: zero** (buildings built to the sidewalk edge/property line) is the norm for every downtown examined; this is the single most consistent finding across all 12 towns.
- **Civic anchor pattern — two archetypes observed, roughly evenly split:**
  1. **Courthouse square**: courthouse/civic building at the center of a full block, commercial storefronts facing inward on all 4 sides (Fairfield IA, Bryan OH — both explicit "courthouse square" layouts).
  2. **Park/green square**: a public park or green serves the same anchor role without a courthouse (Charles City IA's Central Park).
  A third pattern, **rail-corridor spine** (commercial street runs parallel/adjacent to the tracks with no central square at all), appears in the pure railroad towns (McCook NE, Carroll IA, Litchfield MN).
  *Why:* generator should offer these 3 downtown-organization presets rather than assuming courthouse-square is universal — railroad-founded towns (a plurality of this tier: McCook, Carroll, Litchfield, Kendallville partially) often lack a formal square entirely.

### Parking
- Minimal formal off-street parking is the norm at this population scale; the dominant pattern is **angled on-street parking directly on Main Street** (both sides). Angled stall dimensions: **~18-20 ft long x 8.5-10 ft wide**; one-way 45° angled parking can compress to 12-14 ft aisle width where space is tight.
- Streets carrying angled parking on both sides typically run **~60-70 ft** total curb-to-curb width to fit two parking lanes + two travel lanes — consistent with the ~66-100 ft Main Street figures above.
- Beyond on-street parking, small towns at this population scale generally have at most **one or two small municipal surface lots** (often a converted single block or half-block near the courthouse/depot), not large parking structures or multiple big-box-scale lots. None of the 12 towns researched showed evidence of parking garages.

### Residential zoning
- Standard small-town zoning splits residential into **2 tiers**: a "suburban" single-family district (larger lots, e.g. Wapakoneta's R-1) and an "in-town" single/two-family district closer to downtown (smaller lots, e.g. Wapakoneta's R-2), sometimes with a separate mobile-home district (R-5) at the edge of town. This 2-3 tier residential structure recurred in every zoning-code reference found.
- Generic (non-town-specific but well-corroborated) residential lot norms for this scale of town: **lot width ~60 ft, lot area ~7,000-9,000 sq ft (~0.16-0.2 acre)** is the median American single-family lot size range and matches typical small-city R-1/R-60-style zoning minimums found in comparable ordinances. *Confidence: moderate — this is a generic US suburban norm corroborated by zoning-atlas/lot-size literature, not confirmed against any of the 12 towns' actual ordinance text directly, except structurally (the tier system itself).*
- Confirmed specific data point: **Fairfield, IA requires a 35 ft rear-yard setback on through-lots deeper than 95 ft**, and treats through-lots as having a front setback on *both* street frontages (no "cheat" shallow backyard on a corner/through lot). *Why for generator:* any lot touching two streets should get front-yard-style setback treatment on both sides, not a reduced rear setback.
- Residential streets: **~22-24 ft wide without curbs** is the typical rural/small-town subdivision standard (cited range 16-24 ft depending on formality); many small-town residential streets, especially older/platted sections, lack curbs and sidewalks entirely, especially toward the town's outer edge. *Why:* lower traffic volumes and tighter municipal budgets at this population scale don't justify full urban curb-and-gutter treatment everywhere.
- Grid pattern: **prevalent in the historic core**, fraying into curvilinear/cul-de-sac subdivision patterns at the newest (outermost) edges of town where late-20th-century development occurred — implied by every town's historic-district-vs-modern-growth contrast, though not numerically quantified in sources.

### Distinctive building types (with typical footprint/scale)
- **Grain elevators**: small-town wooden/concrete elevators typically **~25-90 ft footprint** (examples found: 26x26 ft, 38x44 ft, 87x24 ft) and **70-125 ft tall** — a very tall, narrow, visually dominant structure relative to its small footprint. Cluster along the rail corridor, physically and functionally separate from the Main Street retail core.
- **Opera houses / halls** (built ~1870-1930 across the Midwest): typically **~50-120 ft long x 40-50 ft wide**, 1-3 stories externally (occasionally taller), seating 100-1,300; usually located directly on or one block off Main Street, often multi-use (ground-floor retail + upper auditorium).
- **Carnegie libraries**: small, symmetrical, classical-revival stone/brick buildings, roughly **60-80 ft per side**, 1-1.5 stories, usually sited near but not directly on the main commercial block (found in Fairfield IA and Rice Lake WI).
- **County courthouses**: the largest single building mass in a courthouse-square town, occupying a full block, typically 2-4 stories with a dome/tower, built 1870s-1900s in most of the towns researched.
- **Gas stations (1920s-1930s)**: small "domestic style" cottage-form stations with a gabled canopy over the pump island — no reliable square-footage figure found in this pass, but qualitatively a small (roughly single-car-bay to 2-bay), low, single-story structure, usually sited at a Main Street corner or along the highway/bypass edge rather than mid-block.
- **Rail depots**: 1-story, long/narrow rectangular footprint, sited directly on the tracks at the edge of the commercial district in every railroad-founded town researched.

## River/creek/property-value/commercial-intensity correlation (this tier)

At this population scale, the relationship between water and downtown is best described as **founding-era causation that persists as a spatial fact even when the economic rationale has changed**:
- **River-mill towns** (Charles City IA, Napoleon OH) have their historic downtown built *directly against* the water — Charles City's Main Street literally crosses the river on a bridge integral to downtown; Napoleon's downtown fronts the Maumee. In these towns, the highest-intensity commercial fabric (oldest, densest, tallest buildings) is the fabric closest to the water, because that's where the town started and grew outward from.
- **Inland county-center towns** (Fairfield IA, Mount Pleasant IA) were sited for centrality/elevation, not water, and their downtowns show no particular water proximity — commercial intensity there correlates with distance from the courthouse square, not from any creek.
- **Railroad towns** (McCook NE, Carroll IA, Litchfield MN) show commercial intensity correlating with proximity to the **rail depot/corridor**, not water — water (where present, e.g. McCook's Republican River, Litchfield's Lake Ripley) is a secondary, often recreational amenity rather than the commercial spine's organizing feature.
- **Lake-resort towns** (Rice Lake WI) invert the usual pattern: the *highest-value* land is lakefront **residential/recreational** property, not downtown — downtown itself sits a short distance from the shoreline. This is the one case in the tier where water proximity drives *residential* value more than commercial value.
- **Net rule for the generator:** if the town's founding type is "water" (mill/river), put downtown adjacent to the water body and make near-water blocks the oldest/densest. If founding type is "rail" or "inland/trail," put water (if present at all) at a middle distance from downtown, functioning as a recreational amenity that boosts *residential* rather than commercial land value. Only make water the primary downtown-value driver when the town's founding type is explicitly river/mill-based.

## Topography / drainage patterns

- The Midwest examples in this tier (Ohio, Iowa, Nebraska, Wisconsin, Minnesota) are overwhelmingly **flat-to-gently-rolling former prairie or glacial till plain**, with the historic grid laid out with no topographic accommodation (streets run true north-south/east-west regardless of minor grade).
- **Creeks/drainage** at this scale typically run through town in one of two forms: (a) a named river bisecting the town (Charles City's Cedar River, Napoleon's Maumee, McCook's Republican, Wapakoneta's Auglaize) which the historic grid was platted up against, or (b) minor unnamed drainage ditches/swales at the town's outer edge, transitioning into farm drainage tile systems just past the platted boundary — this matches the general Midwest county-seat pattern of "town grid stops abruptly at a section-line road, farmland (with its own drainage-tile grid) begins immediately beyond."
- **Floodplain**: rivers running directly through/against downtown (Charles City, Napoleon, Wapakoneta) imply the historic core sits partly within the modern FEMA floodplain — a real risk/constraint worth modeling (e.g., ground-floor flood-proofing, periodic flood event as a map/story hook) for any town using the river-mill founding archetype. Riparian setback ordinances found in comparable Midwest jurisdictions during this research (e.g., Green, OH) commonly exempt engineered drainage ditches/tile systems from setback rules while still restricting building near natural stream banks — a useful generator rule: treat "natural creek" and "engineered drainage ditch" as different setback categories.
- **Non-Midwest contrast (Abingdon VA)**: sits in Appalachian ridge-and-valley terrain, Main Street following a level valley/ridge-top wagon-road alignment rather than a flat grid — offered as an optional "hilly terrain" variant, not the tier default.

## Assumptions and confidence notes
- Population figures are 2020 census (or latest official estimate) as of research date; three towns (Kendallville IN, Carroll IA, and borderline Mount Airy-type towns considered but not used) sit slightly above the nominal 10,000 ceiling — included anyway because their overall built-form scale is indistinguishable from firmly-in-range towns, and small towns' populations fluctuate across this threshold within any given decade.
- Where a specific town's zoning ordinance text could not be retrieved in full (most towns — public zoning-code hosting varies widely and several sites blocked automated fetches), figures in this summary fall back on well-corroborated generic small-town/US suburban planning norms (lot width, street width ranges) rather than town-verified numbers. Town-specific confirmed figures (Bryan OH street widths, Fairfield IA through-lot setback) are flagged as such in both the per-town files and here.
- No satellite/GIS imagery was visually inspected in this research pass (text/web research only, per task scope); all spatial claims are derived from written sources (Wikipedia, city planning documents, historic-register nominations, local chamber/tourism sites) rather than direct map measurement. A future pass with GIS/satellite tools could tighten block-length and lot-size figures considerably.
