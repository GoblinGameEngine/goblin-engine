## Core layout — numeric patterns

- **Downtown building count**: at this tier there is usually no "downtown" in the city sense — typically **a single commercial street 1-3 blocks long**, or in county-seat/highway-junction cases, a small square/crossroads. Occupied storefront counts scale roughly with population: expect on the order of **5-15 active commercial buildings** for villages under ~1,000 (Buda, Elma, Wyanet), rising to **15-30** for the 1,000-2,000 range (Toulon, Dysart, Wyoming, Clarksville, Roanoke), and **30-60+** for the 3,000-5,000 range (New Hampton, Hoopeston), where a genuine multi-block contiguous storefront district becomes viable (sourced indirectly: New Hampton's Main Street supports a walkable parade route and continuous storefront window displays for its "Live Windows" event — [homes.com](https://www.homes.com/local-guide/new-hampton-ia/)).
- **Building contiguity**: commercial-core buildings are typically **zero-lot-line/contiguous** (shared walls, built to the sidewalk) in the historic 1-2 block core, consistent with the New Hampton storefront-window-parade evidence. Outside that core (churches, schools, elevators, depots), buildings are **freestanding with gaps**, usually one per block-face.
- **Original plat size**: Roanoke, IL's original 1874 plat was documented at exactly **15 blocks** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roanoke,_Illinois)) for a town that reached ~1,960 people over the following 150 years — a good anchor: **~10-20 original blocks** is a reasonable platted-core size for a village that will eventually reach 1,000-2,000 population, with later growth mostly infilling rather than re-platting.
- **Street width**: no village-specific figures were found, but general Midwest platting convention research turned up **66 ft (20 m) as the standard street right-of-way width** (the Chicago/Illinois standard, widely reused across Midwest railroad-town plats) with **16 ft (4.9 m) alleys**. Use 66 ft as the default Main Street ROW, narrower (~50 ft / 15 m) for secondary residential streets.
- **Sidewalks/curbs**: present in the 1-3 block commercial core (needed for storefront access); typically absent or informal (gravel shoulder, no curb) on residential streets and at the village edge.

## Residential pattern — numeric patterns

- **Lot size**: no single village in this sample published an explicit residential lot dimension, but cross-referencing general Midwest small-town platting practice suggests historic in-town lots on the order of **50-66 ft wide x 120-140 ft deep (roughly 0.15-0.2 acre / 600-850 m²)** in the platted core, opening up to **0.25-1+ acre lots** at the built-up edge nearest farmland. This is consistent with back-calculated household density: e.g. Clarksville (966/sq mi ÷ ~2.3 people/household ≈ 420 households/sq mi ≈ **1.5 acres per household** including streets/downtown/public land — meaning actual private lots are well under that once ROW and non-residential land are subtracted, supporting the 0.15-0.5 acre residential-lot range).
- **Grid extent**: villages in this sample rarely exceed **2-3 residential streets** beyond the core in the sub-1,500 population range; by 3,000-5,000 (New Hampton, Hoopeston) expect **6-10 residential streets** forming a small multi-block grid, still usually a simple rectangular grid rather than curvilinear subdivision patterns.
- **Distance from core to open farmland**: directly bounded by each village's tiny total land area (0.85-3.69 sq mi in this sample). For a roughly circular/square built-up area of that size, **farmland is reached within approximately 300-600 m of the town center** for villages under ~1,500 population (land area ≈0.85-1.3 sq mi), extending to **roughly 800 m-1.1 km** for the 3,000-5,000 population villages (land area ≈3.4-3.7 sq mi). **Recommended generator default: farmland begins 400-800 m from the core, scaling with population within that band.**

## Traffic/zoning — numeric patterns

- **Non-residential building types actually present**, ranked by how consistently they appear across the 12 files: **church(es)** (near-universal), **grain elevator** (present in every village with active/historic rail service — 9 of 12 explicitly noted), **post office** (assumed universal, directly confirmed for Buda/Elma), **railroad depot** (historic in all 12; many adaptively reused as museums/trail HQ — Plainview, Wyoming, Toulon), **school** — but school presence is population-dependent (see below), **tavern/bar** (a recurring surviving business type in the smallest villages, e.g. Buda).
- **School consolidation threshold**: this sample suggests a rough threshold — **villages under ~600 population tend to have lost their local school building entirely to district consolidation** (Elma, school closed 2013, bused to Howard-Winneshiek CSD); **villages of ~1,200-2,000 may retain a partial/satellite building in a shared district** (Dysart, kept a "learning center" within a 1993-merged district; Roanoke has a named two-town consolidated high school, "Roanoke-Benson"); **county-seat or 3,000+ population villages can support a full local public school plus a separate parochial school** (Toulon has its own high school as county seat despite only 1,193 population; New Hampton at 3,494 has both a public district and St. Joseph's Catholic School). **Recommendation for generator: population < 700 → no school building; 700-2,000 → one shared/consolidated school building, possibly named for a paired neighboring town; 2,000-5,000 → one or two full school buildings, optionally plus a small parochial school.**
- **Non-residential placement relative to Main Street**: grain elevators consistently sit **on a side street near the rail corridor, not directly on Main Street** (Wyoming's elevator is explicitly on "East Williams Street," off the core); churches and the depot/museum-conversions are typically **within 1 block of Main Street** (Calmar's depot sits exactly one block north of the Public Square).

## Parking

No village in this sample published parking-lot data. Consistent with general knowledge of the type: assume **on-street parking along the 1-3 block commercial core is the primary parking supply**, with at most one small informal gravel lot (church, feed mill, or elevator scale-house) — **no structured or large paved lots** at this population tier.

## Water-proximity patterns (this tier)

- Of the 12 villages, **3 have a direct, sourced water-founding relationship** (Elma: original 1854 creek settlement predating the rail town; Clarksville: 1853 dam+sawmill, 1867 flour mill directly on the Shell Rock River; Wyoming: sited 0.5 mi from the Spoon River with tributary creeks through the south side of town).
- **7 of 12 have no mapped water area at all** within city limits (Buda, Wyanet, Toulon, Dysart, Plainview, New Hampton, Hoopeston) — i.e., **more than half of this sample are purely railroad/highway/administrative foundings with no on-site water feature.** This is an important corrective: at the village tier, water-siting is common but **not the majority pattern** once the railroad era (1850s-1880s) is the dominant founding driver — rail lines could be built anywhere, decoupling town siting from water availability in a way earlier (pre-rail, e.g. Wyoming IL 1836) or mill-dependent (Clarksville) towns were not.
- **Where water founding does occur, it is functional, not scenic**: a mill/dam site (Clarksville) or a creek suitable for early settlement before rail access (Elma) — not lakefront/riverfront property value in any modern real-estate sense. **No village in this sample showed evidence of a property-value premium tied to water proximity** — at this tier, land value signals (where noted at all) track proximity to the commercial core/highway junction (Calmar) or county-seat administrative function (Toulon, New Hampton), not water. **Recommendation: do not model a water-proximity value premium at the village tier** — reserve that pattern for larger town/city tiers where lakefront residential development becomes a distinct product type.
- **Recommended generator rule**: roll water-founding with roughly **1-in-3 to 1-in-4 probability** for a generated village (matching this sample's ~30% water-founded rate), and when it occurs, place the core directly adjacent to the water body (within ~1 block, mill-site style) rather than merely "nearby."

## Drainage/topography patterns

- No village in this sample published explicit drainage-ditch or floodplain data, but indirect evidence supports standard rural-Midwest drainage conventions: Clarksville's "Water Street" naming marks a historic river-frontage plat edge, implying the original plat was drawn up to, but the block layout otherwise respects, the floodplain edge.
- Terrain is uniformly described as **flat to gently rolling till-plain** in Illinois/Iowa/Nebraska examples, with one **notable exception**: Calmar, IA lies in Iowa's **Driftless Area** (unglaciated, higher local relief, more incised creek valleys) — a useful topographic variant for generator diversity even though Calmar's own core is not water-adjacent.
- **Elevation range across sample**: 653-1,686 ft, with the Nebraska example (Plainview, 1,686 ft) markedly higher than the Iowa/Illinois till-plain examples (653-1,247 ft) — reflects real west-to-east rise across the region; a generator spanning multiple states in this aesthetic should vary base elevation accordingly, though this has no direct gameplay implication beyond visual/skybox tuning.
- **Recommendation**: model roadside drainage ditches as a default alongside any rural road outside the built-up core (standard Midwest practice, not contradicted by any source found), and keep the built core's lowest streets/blocks set back from any adjacent creek/river by at least one block (Clarksville's "Water Street" pattern) to imply floodplain avoidance without needing explicit flood data per village.

## Distinctive tiny-scale building types — cross-sample notes

- **Grain elevator**: explicitly confirmed as **the tallest structure in town** for Wyoming, IL; general research found small wood-cribbed elevators historically ran **32-40 ft tall / roughly 26x26 ft footprint** at the smallest (e.g., the Gano Grain Elevator reference, ~15,000 bushels) up to **70-120 ft tall** for larger small-town elevators (26,000+ bushels, e.g. the Historic Ely Elevator, Iowa, 1900, 26,000 bushels). **Recommendation: village-tier elevators should be modeled at the smaller end, roughly 30-60 ft tall on a ~25-30 ft square footprint**, sited on a side street near the (often disused) rail corridor.
- **Railroad depot**: near-universal historic presence (all 12 villages had rail service at founding); many are now **adaptively reused** — museum (Plainview), state-park-trail HQ (Wyoming, and by extension Toulon's trail terminus), or simply preserved-but-vacant (Roanoke). A generator could model 1 depot per village, optionally flagged as "active," "museum/reuse," or "vacant/derelict" depending on whether the rail line is still active.
- **Carnegie library**: appeared twice in this sample (Plainview NE, Hoopeston IL) despite very different populations (1,282 and 4,915) — Carnegie libraries are a recognizable, recurring small-town building type independent of exact population, likely because their construction (early 1900s Carnegie grant program) predates modern population trends. Worth including as an optional distinctive building even in smaller villages.
- **Consolidated/shared school naming**: recurring paired-town district names (Roanoke-Benson, Howard-Winneshiek, Union [Dysart/La Porte City], Bureau Valley) confirm that at this tier, school district identity is frequently **not the same name as the village itself** — a naming detail a generator could use for flavor text/signage even without modeling the neighboring town.
- **Founder's mansion**: Hoopeston's Hoopes-Cunningham Mansion is an example of an atypically large "town founder's house" — recommend including **at most one per village**, and only for villages with a clearly named founder in their history (roughly half of this sample has one).
- **Church**: near-universal; the only architectural note found (Wyoming, IL) is a "distinctive pointed tower" on the First Congregational Church — small frame or brick single-tower churches are the expected default; no floor-plan-level NRHP source was found specific to a village in this sample (general search located examples like Bethel Church, Morning Sun, IA, 1855, sited on a 1.8-acre lot, and Loucks Grove Church, Greenfield, IA, on under 1 acre — useful as generic small-church lot-size reference: **under 2 acres, typically under 1 acre**, for a standalone rural/village church parcel).

## Assumptions flagged

- Parking, sidewalk/curb presence outside the core, and drainage-ditch specifics were **not directly sourced** for any individual village in this sample; the recommendations above rely on general knowledge of small rural Midwestern town conventions rather than a cited primary source, and should be treated as lower-confidence defaults.
- Lot-size figures are **derived/back-calculated** from Census population-density and land-area figures, not read off an actual plat map or assessor's parcel dataset; treat the 0.15-0.5 acre residential lot range as a reasonable working default, not a hard sourced fact.
- Water-founding rate (~30%) and school-consolidation population thresholds are **pattern-matched from this specific 12-village sample**, not a statistically representative survey of all US villages in this population range — reasonable as generator defaults but should be revisited if broader data becomes available.
