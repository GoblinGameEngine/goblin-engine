# C1. Europe's stacked warrens (research stream C, 2026-10-14)

The user, 2026-10-14: "The islands should have stacked warrens. Research European, South American and Canadian
examples. Avoid [sub-Saharan] Africa." North Africa is covered by stream A.

**How to read this**
- Figures come from the sources cited. Where no source gave a number, it is marked **(est.)**; these are working values
  from common building practice, for the generator to use.
- Each section ends with **"For the Aloft"**: what to take, and how it changes on a cold, light, commercial island

## 1. Edinburgh Old Town: the "lands" and closes
- **Plot and building.** Plots were *tenements*, and the buildings on them were *lands*. Multi-storey lands appear in
  the late 16th century. Most were 6–8 storeys on the High Street front, and sources say up to 12 at the low end of the
  slope, because the ridge falls away behind. The fall adds storeys at the back without adding any at the front: the
  same building counts 6 floors from the High Street and 11–12 from the close below. [canmore 52322; history.org.uk;
  cccc.org]
- **Gladstone's Land (1617–20)** is the reference example:
  - It is 6 storeys, or 5 plus attic and cellar.
  - Frontage is about 23 ft 6 in (7.2 m) and depth 63 ft (19.2 m), split by a transverse wall from cellar to attic.
  - The ground floor is an open arcade set back from the building line. The ashlar front replaced earlier **timber
    galleries** that projected over the street.
  - A curved stone **forestair** rises to the first-floor door.
  - The top two floors run up into the gables. [Gladstone's Land, Wikipedia; Canmore 52528; HES LB29233]
- **Access.**
  - The Royal Mile is the spine. **Closes** run off it at right angles, falling steeply. They are private and could be
    gated, and lead to the tenements and small courts behind.
  - **Wynds** are public through-routes linking streets at different levels.
  - **Pends** are covered passages with rooms built over them. [Pend, Wikipedia; edinburgh.org closes]
  - Close width is about 1.2–2.5 m (est.).
  - A **common stair** (turnpike, a spiral in a round tower) serves 2 flats a landing in better lands and up to 6 in poor
    ones. The smallest unit was the one-room **single end**. [sarahglynn.net; 1900s.org.uk; Tenements (Scotland) Act
    2004 s.29]
- **Materials and colour.**
  - Harled rubble and ashlar: grey-brown sandstone #6e6457, soot-dark #3d3a36.
  - Lime harl #d8cfbf, ochre limewash #c9a66b.
  - Slate roofs #3b4148; crow-stepped gables.
- **Fantasy echo.** Diagon Alley and Knockturn Alley; the Hogwarts-adjacent "Edinburgh Gothic" look.
- **For the Aloft:**
  - The **"land"**: a slab 7 m wide and 19 m deep, rising 8–12 storeys over a sloped deck. It is lower at the front
    on the promenade and taller at the back down the rolling relief.
  - Each land has a **turnpike stair tower** on the close side. **Closes** are 1.5 m slits running from the main path
    to back courts, and **pends** carry rooms over them.
  - An arcaded ground floor holds shops.
  - **Timber galleries** cantilever off the upper floors.
  - The best stacked-warren model in this set: a few tall slabs packed shoulder to shoulder with narrow cuts between.

## 2. Jettied timber towns: Rothenburg, Strasbourg, Chester, Prague, Old London Bridge
- **Jettying.** Each upper floor projects past the one below.
  - The **bressummer** beam rides on the ends of the jetty joists. **Dragon beams** run 45° to the corners when two
    sides jetty.
  - Overhanging corner posts get curved **brackets**.
  - It works over 3–4 storeys, each stepping out again.
  - Regional names: German *windbrett* (the decorated underside of the jetty), French *encorbellement*, Turkish *cumba*.
    [Jettying, Wikipedia; lookingatbuildings.org.uk]
  - Typical projection is 0.3–0.6 m per storey (est.), so a 4-storey house gains about 1.5 m at the top. Opposite houses
    across a 3 m lane come within about 0.5 m of each other at the eaves, as in "The Shambles".
- **Rothenburg (Plönlein).** A narrow, leaning, yellow half-timbered house where two lanes split at the Siebersturm and
  Kobolzeller towers; the model for Disney's *Pinocchio* village. [rothenburg.de; sygic Plönlein]
- **Roofs** are steep (55–60° (est.)) with stacked dormers 2–3 rows high and **hoist gables**: a door in the gable with a
  beam and pulley above it, for loading the attic granary.
- **Colour.**
  - Timber oxblood #6b2a22 or black-brown #2e231c.
  - Infill whites #efe7d6, yellows #e3c56a and pinks #d99a8a.
  - Red plain-tile roofs #9a4a33.
- **Old London Bridge (1209–1761).**
  - Houses up to 5 storeys (the tallest reportedly 7) on hammer beams overhanging the Thames by "several feet".
  - **Nonsuch House** (1579) spanned an arch: 4 storeys, onion-domed corner turrets, prefabricated in Holland.
  - [amusingplanet Nonsuch; the-past.com Gerhold; bryarsandbryars]
- **For the Aloft:**
  - The **jettied stack**: every storey corbels out 0.4–0.6 m, so a 5-storey timber house is 2.5 m wider at its eaves.
    Opposite stacks almost meet over 3 m lanes.
  - Hoist gables carry real hoists for winching goods to the attic store.
  - Fire bans killed jettying in London (1667) and Rouen (1520). On a hydrogen island, canon has the Aloft ban open
    flame entirely, so jetties are safe for them.

## 3. Bridges lined with houses: Ponte Vecchio (Florence), Krämerbrücke (Erfurt)
- **Ponte Vecchio (1345).**
  - Shops line both sides. Their *retrobotteghe* (back-shops) hang off the sides on wooden brackets (*sporti*).
  - The **Vasari Corridor** (1565) runs on top of the eastern shops.
  - Goldsmiths have held the bridge since 1593. [reidsitaly; visittuscany; travelmyth]
- **Krämerbrücke.**
  - 125 m long overall (the vaulted bridge itself 79 m).
  - 32 half-timbered houses survive of the 62 built after the 1472 fire.
  - 3 storeys, 13–15 m tall; the longest row of inhabited buildings on any bridge in Europe.
  - [Krämerbrücke, Wikipedia; atlasobscura]
- **For the Aloft (very strong):**
  - A **bridge street**: the gap between two lashed islands, or a permanent span between a big island's two lobes,
    built over with shops.
  - Back-shops hang off the sides on brackets over a 750 m drop, with a covered corridor along the roof.
  - The 13 m docking bridges stay plain (they must retract), but a big island's internal spans can be inhabited.

## 4. Matera's Sassi: roofs as streets
- Houses are dug into the calcarenite ("tufo"). **Streets run on the roofs of the houses below**: the foundation of one
  dwelling is the roof of the next, and walkers find themselves on roofs opening onto terraces.
- In places there are **ten levels** of dwellings one above another, and only about 30% of the fabric is visible from
  the street.
- Most residents were moved out in the 1950s; UNESCO listed it in 1993.
- [Sassi di Matera, Wikipedia; wol.jw.org; hpb.co.uk]
- **Section (est.):**
  - Each terrace level is 3–4.5 m.
  - A cave room runs 6–15 m into the rock behind a built vaulted façade 4–6 m wide.
  - The roof terrace in front of the level above is the path, 2–3 m deep.
  - Stairs zig-zag between levels.
- **Colour.** Honey tufo #c9b08a, shadowed #8f7a5c, whitewash patches #ece5d6.
- **For the Aloft:**
  - The **terrace stack** fits the rock *underside* and the rolling relief: houses step down a hill, each roof the
    next level's path.
  - On the rock rim, cave rooms are cut into the island's flank, with doors and windows *in the underside rock* looking
    out over the void. This is unique to floating islands: a cliff warren hanging below the deck edge, reached by stairs
    down from the promenade.

## 5. Genoa caruggi and Naples' Quartieri Spagnoli
- **Genoa.**
  - The largest contiguous medieval fabric in Europe is claimed: about 113 km of alleys in about 1 km².
  - Caruggi are 1–3 m wide between palazzi of 6+ storeys, so height to width often exceeds 10:1. They never see sun.
  - The piano nobile is on the 2nd or 3rd floor; floors above are poorer, smaller and lower.
  - Note: the dimensional figures come from blogs and are illustrative. [blog.indiecinema.co; visitgenoa.it;
    italyplanner]
- **Naples, Quartieri Spagnoli (1530s).**
  - A grid of about 18 × 12 lanes with about 14,000 people. Lanes are about 3 m wide and buildings up to 6 storeys.
  - **Bassi** are one-room ground-floor homes or workshops whose door opens straight onto the lane; the bed is visible
    from the street.
  - Laundry is strung across the lanes. Shrines (edicole votive) are lit at night on the walls.
  - [Quartieri Spagnoli, Wikipedia; napolisotterranea; eurovistatrips]
- **Colour.**
  - Ligurian and Neapolitan stucco: ochre #d9a441, terracotta #c46a3c, pompeian red #9e3b2b, salmon #e59a7a, faded
    green shutters #4f6b4a.
  - Genoese **trompe-l'œil** painted windows and quoins.
- **For the Aloft:**
  - The **canyon grid**: a regular grid of 2.5–3 m lanes between 6–8 storey blocks.
  - A **basso** at every ground door: a lit one-room shop or home with its door open.
  - Washing lines and sign strings cross every lane.
  - Painted trompe-l'œil (fake windows and balconies) on blank walls is cheap variety from one texture.

## 6. Ligurian and Amalfi cliff stacks: Manarola, Riomaggiore, Positano
- Tall, narrow, colourful stucco tower-houses (4–7 storeys on footprints of about 5 × 8 m (est.)) jammed in a jagged
  pattern down a ravine to the harbour.
- Ligurian *case-torri* have a door on the lane at the top and another at the bottom, two or three floors lower.
- [getnofilter Manarola; photo12]
- **Colour (Ligurian).**
  - Wall: ochre #e3a857, rose #d77d6b, raspberry #b8485a, lemon #e8cf6a, sea-green #6fa08d, sky #8fb5c9.
  - Shutters: green #3e6b47.
  - Roofs: slate grey #5d6066 (Liguria) or terracotta #b55a3c (Amalfi).
- **For the Aloft:**
  - The **ravine stack** runs down an island's rim slope or along a dock-face notch.
  - Upper doors open onto the promenade and lower doors onto stair lanes down the rock.
  - Every house a different saturated colour.

## 7. Lisbon's Alfama and Porto's Ribeira
- **Alfama.** The medieval (Moorish) lane plan survived the 1755 earthquake.
  - *Becos* (blind alleys) and stairs; tiled (azulejo) façades.
  - *Miradouros* are viewing terraces.
  - Tram 28E squeezes through.
  - Small taverns and grocers sit at the corners.
  - [viatravelers Alfama; Lonely Planet; portugal.com; MIT Dome photo]
- **Porto Ribeira.** Very narrow tiled houses (4–6 storeys, about 4–6 m wide (est.)) stack up the river cliff, with
  iron balconies at every window.
- **Colour.**
  - Azulejo blue #2f5d9e on white #f2f2ee.
  - Façade ochre #d8a34c, faded pink #d79a8e, green tile #3f7d6a.
  - Iron balconies #26292b.
- **For the Aloft:**
  - **Tile skins**: tile_panel façades in clan colours. One 0.15 m tile texture recoloured makes endless variety.
  - **Miradouro** platforms cantilever off the rim.
  - A tram-like cable car runs along the main road of the big islands.

## 8. Santorini (Oia) cave houses
- **Yposkafa**, "dug into the rock": a room bored horizontally into pumice and tuff, closed by a thin stone wall and a
  shallow **barrel vault** of ash-lime mortar, then lime-washed white.
- A cistern in every house.
- Captains built freestanding houses on the rim, and sailors lived in the caves below.
- [studiomatrx Santorini; holeinthedonut; showcaves]
- **Colour.** Lime white #f4f3ee, Aegean blue domes and doors #1e5aa8, ochre caves #d9a75e, bougainvillea #c2306a.
- **For the Aloft:** **vault-cells**. Barrel-vaulted white cells stack down a slope, with blue domes on chapels and the
  wealthy. One "vault cell" component (about 4 × 6 m) builds a whole slope.

## 9. Fortress hill towns: Mont-Saint-Michel, Rocamadour, Monaco, Mdina, Kotor
- **Mont-Saint-Michel.**
  - Houses tier up the south and east flanks inside the walls, along the single **Grande Rue** that climbs through three
    successive gates to the abbey.
  - Medieval shop signs survive.
  - The houses were timber-framed, now mostly stone. Half the mount is kept free of buildings, as gardens reached by
    suspended staircases.
  - The **Merveille** is six rooms on three levels.
  - [le-mont-saint-michel.com; ot-montsaintmichel]
- **Rocamadour** stacks a village, sanctuary and castle up a 120 m cliff (est.).
- **Mdina and Valletta.** The **gallarija** is an enclosed timber balcony on stone corbels, glazed in small panes.
  - The first known example is about 1675, on the Grand Master's Palace.
  - Often emerald green or in bold colours matching the door; possibly descended from the mashrabiya.
  - [Gallarija, Wikipedia; guidememalta]
- **Kotor.** A walled sea town at the foot of a cliff, with the wall zig-zagging up behind it.
- **For the Aloft:**
  - The **spiral high street**: one main street climbs round and up the island's central hill through gates, with houses
    tiered on both sides and the clan hall or temple at the summit.
  - **Gallarija** bays in clan colours line it.

## 10. Balkan and Ottoman towns: Gjirokastër, Berat, Mostar, Plovdiv
- **Gjirokastër kullë** (tower house).
  - A stone lower body (storage, cistern, kitchen) carries a timber gallery floor of family rooms and a reception room on
    top.
  - Roofs are heavy limestone slabs.
  - The Zekate House (1811–12) has 3 high storeys. [Tower houses in the Balkans, Wikipedia; gjirokastra.org]
- **Berat**, "city of a thousand windows". White 2-storey Ottoman houses stack up the slope (Mangalem) so their window
  rows read as one wall of windows. [albaniatravelguide; loudavymkrokem]
- **Plovdiv Revival houses.**
  - Upper floors and bays project on brackets over the street, often not at right angles: plans bend to fit odd lots.
  - Façades are painted with flowers, landscapes and geometric frescoes.
  - Gossip rooms (bays) sit upstairs.
  - [Old Town Plovdiv, Wikipedia; lostinplovdiv; gallivance "upside down wedding cakes"]
- **Colour.**
  - Ottoman white #f1ece2, bay timber #5a3b26.
  - Plovdiv: blue #3c6fa8, ochre #d39b3a, red #a8402f, Prussian #26496b.
  - Stone roofs #8b8a85.
- **For the Aloft:**
  - The **upside-down wedding cake**: each floor bigger than the one below, on brackets, ending in a wide eaved top. This
    is ideal over a small lot on a crowded deck.
  - Window walls (Berat) read beautifully lit at night.

## 11. Bergen's Bryggen and the Hanseatic wharf
- Long parallel rows of gabled timber buildings face the harbour.
  - Each **gård** (yard) is a pair of rows facing each other across a narrow plank passage.
  - The buildings are 2–3 storeys of rooms and **external galleries**.
  - Each has a **crane** or pulley at the gable, and an assembly hall (*schøtstue*).
  - Galleries and bridges link the facing rows so goods move floor to floor across the passage.
- About 61 buildings survive; rebuilt after the 1702 fire on the medieval pattern. [UNESCO 59; Lonely Planet; voicemap]
- **Colour.**
  - Ochre #d9a441, ox red #8e2f25, white #eee7da, cream #e9d9b0.
  - Tarred timber #3a2d22.
- **For the Aloft (very strong):**
  - The **gård row** sits perpendicular to a dock face: two timber rows with galleries, bridged across the passage at
    every floor, with a gable crane over the edge at the end.
  - It works like a wharf: each gård's hoist is the island's cargo hoist.

## 12. Amsterdam canal houses and Flemish gables
- **Plots.**
  - 17th-century lots started at 18 Amsterdam feet (5.1 m) and grew to 20–26 ft (5.6–7.3 m), about 5–7 m.
  - A single house has 3 bays; a double house has 5 bays (15–18 m).
  - Deep: a *voorhuis* (front house) and *achterhuis* (rear house) are separated by a court.
  - Warehouses run up to 40 m deep.
  - [UNESCO canal ring; Canal house, Wikipedia; Expatica; MIT OCW chapter 2]
- **Hoist beam (hijsbalk).** It projects from the top gable with a hook or pulley; goods and furniture go up the façade
  and in through tall windows because the stairs are too steep. Façades lean forward on purpose so loads clear the
  wall. [Expatica; Accor; ericroseman substack]
- **Gables, by period.**
  - Step (crow-step), 1600–65.
  - Neck, 1640–1840.
  - Bell.
  - Cornice (spout).
  - [buffaloah; UNESCO]
  - Brussels' Grand-Place guild houses are Baroque, gilded and ultra-narrow, so every guild got a frontage on the
    square. [List of houses of the Grand-Place, Wikipedia]
- **Storeys (est.).** Ground 3.5–4 m (the original *voorhuis* was double-height), upper floors 2.8–3.2 m, attic and loft
  2–3 levels.
- **Colour.**
  - Brick #6b3a2c and #8a4a33.
  - Black-green shutters #1f2a24.
  - White sash and cornice #f2efe6.
  - Gilt #c9a13b on the Brussels gables.
- **For the Aloft (essential):** every tall narrow house gets a **hoist beam** at its gable. This is how goods reach
  upper floors on a cold, crowded island with steep stairs, and it reads instantly as "traders". Gables in stepped,
  neck, bell and cornice forms are a cheap profile set that varies a skyline enormously.

## 13. Venice
- **Altana.** A plank roof deck on posts over the tiles, reached by a short stair from the attic. Most are open, some
  half-covered. [bonjourvenise altane; listings]
- Bridges over canals; *sotoporteghi* (pends); chimney pots (*camini*).
- **For the Aloft:** an **altana** on every pitched roof as its roof access. It fits the user's "all buildings have roof
  access" and gives lark pads and cloud nets a deck.

## 14. Hallstatt and Meteora: perches
- **Hallstatt.**
  - Houses stand on a steep slope between mountain and lake like "swallows' nests", gable ends to the lake.
  - Shingled roofs; boat houses on stilts over the water.
  - 16th-century houses have stone ground floors and timber uppers. [dachstein.salzkammergut.at; worldheritagesite;
    detail.de]
- **Meteora.** Monasteries perched on rock pillars. Until the 1920s they were reached only by **net and windlass**
  hauled up the cliff: a *real* precedent for sky trade hoists and baskets (common knowledge, not fetched).
- **For the Aloft:**
  - **Swallows' nests**: small timber boxes bracketed out from the rim rock, reached by ladders.
  - **Windlass towers** for the cargo hoists.

## Night light and signs in these streets
- **Edinburgh closes:** a single lamp at each close mouth over a painted name board, and lit windows high up.
- **Naples:** lit shrines (edicole) in wall niches; strings of bare bulbs at festivals (*luminarie*).
- **Southern Italy:** *luminarie* are huge lit arches and screens of bulbs over the street. They are the European
  Christmas-tree look the user wants.
- **Ponte Vecchio and Mont-Saint-Michel:** shop signs on wrought-iron brackets (blade signs) and lanterns at the gates.
- **Amsterdam:** strings of bulbs along the canal bridges' arches; gables floodlit; red-lit windows (avoid).
- **Bergen:** lanterns along the galleries.

## Fantasy echoes
- Diagon Alley: jettying and leaning stacks.
- Minas Tirith: tiered gates and the spiral high street.
- Ankh-Morpork: Shades warrens; bridges with houses.
- *Bioshock Infinite*'s Columbia: floating Americana blocks, docks, signage everywhere. It is a direct precedent for
  floating trader islands.
- Hogsmeade: snow on jettied roofs.
