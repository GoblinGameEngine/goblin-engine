# C2. South America's stacked warrens (research stream C, 2026-10-14)

Figures come from the sources cited; **(est.)** marks working values where none was found.

## 1. Valparaíso's cerros (Chile): the amphitheatre city
- **Setting.** About 42 *cerros* (hills) rise straight from a narrow port flat, forming an amphitheatre round the bay.
- **Houses.**
  - Timber frames clad in **corrugated iron**, reputedly ship ballast and container sheet.
  - Painted in **ship paint**, so each house is a different saturated colour: red, green, yellow, blue. Houses had no
    numbers, so colour was the address.
  - Typically 2–4 storeys on the uphill street side, dropping 2–3 more storeys down the slope behind (est.).
  - Bay windows and balconies are cantilevered over the drop.
- **Access.**
  - **Ascensores** (funiculars): 26 were built 1883–1932 and 15 still run. Concepción (1883) was first and originally
    steam-powered. Artillería is 175 m at 30°. Polanco is fully vertical, an elevator tower reached by a tunnel.
  - Steep stairways have painted risers and murals; there are alleys and a few real streets.
- **Art.** Almost every wall, stair riser and iron fence carries murals, mosaics or paste-ups.
- **Night.** The hills light up as a bowl of window lights over the bay. New Year fireworks over the bay draw crowds onto
  the cerros; 16,000 fireworks were set off in 2007.
- **Colour.**
  - Corrugated cladding: vermilion #d2402c, ochre #e6a532, ultramarine #2c5aa0, mint #5fb89a, lilac #9a7cc0, lemon
    #f0d24a.
  - Rust #8a4b2c and white trim #f2efe8.
- **For the Aloft (excellent):**
  - The **corrugated cerro**: a whole rolling-hill island of tin-clad houses in ship colours, stacked up its central
    rise.
  - Each house has an uphill door on a lane and a downhill balcony over the next roofs.
  - **Ascensor** cars (funicular cabins on rails up the hillside) climb the hill. The Polanco type is a vertical lift
    tower with a footbridge at its top.
  - Murals on every blank wall; light reads as a bowl of colour at night.

## 2. La Paz and El Alto (Bolivia): Freddy Mamani's *cholets*
- **What they are.** "New Andean architecture" since the 2000s: about 70 buildings by Freddy Mamani in El Alto, about
  5 storeys.
- **Stacking by use:**
  - Ground floor: **shops** and commercial space.
  - Floors 2–3: a **party hall** (ballroom), double height, with chandeliers and mirrored, multicoloured columns.
  - Floors 4–5: **apartments** or sports courts.
  - Roof: the owner's own house, a pitched-roof **chalet**, the *cholet* itself (*chola* + *chalet*).
  - The commercial floors are meant to pay for the building.
- **Look.**
  - Tiwanaku and Aymara geometric motifs (stepped crosses, condors) in mirror glass and bright paint on concrete-frame
    façades.
  - Interiors in neon colours: green, pink, gold.
- **Sources:** [Dezeen; Pin-Up; Fondation Cartier; Univision]
- **Colour.**
  - Façade: lime #9ccc3c, magenta #d1308a, turquoise #2bb3b1, gold #e2b23c, mirror-blue glass #5aa0c8.
  - Andean geometry in black #222 and white.
- **For the Aloft (perfect for commerce societies):**
  - The **cholet block**: shops below, a lit **ballroom** above (the island's party hall, visible at night through tall
    windows), flats above that, and the owner's **chalet** on the roof.
  - The façade is a bold geometric pattern in mirror glass that sparkles under the Sunline.
  - Each clan could build one as its showpiece.

## 3. Rio's favelas (Rocinha, Santa Marta): growth by the *laje*
- **Mechanism.** Houses grow upward by **construção de laje**: building on the roof slab.
  - A family pours a reinforced-concrete frame and slab, fills it with hollow clay brick, and later sells or gives the
    **roof rights** (*direito de laje*) for another storey.
  - Common heights are 3–6 storeys. The core of a new house can go up in under 24 hours; the structure is upgraded later.
  - Population figures vary: 100–150k in Rocinha.
- **Sources:** Martins & Farias 2019, *Sustainable Development*; ISDRS 2017 abstract; ArchDaily (Beneventi);
  TU Darmstadt (Reis 2022); arXiv "Favelas 4D" (laser scans of the morphology).
- **Form (est.):**
  - Lots 4–6 m wide; storeys 2.6–2.8 m.
  - Upper floors cantilever 0.5–1 m over the alley.
  - Stairs are on the outside (straight concrete flights up the side walls).
  - Roofs are flat slabs with blue **water tanks** (caixa d'água), rebar starter bars sticking up ready for the next
    floor, and satellite dishes.
  - Becos are 1–2 m wide; a tangle of wires (gatos) overhead.
- **Colour.**
  - Bare brick #b5643f, raw concrete #9b9a94.
  - Painted patches: Santa Marta's "Favela Painting" grid of primary colours, #e0332e, #f6c21c, #2a7fc4, #3aa457.
  - Blue tanks #2f6db5.
- **For the Aloft:**
  - The **laje stack**: concrete-frame boxes that visibly *grew*, each storey a slightly different size and colour.
  - Rebar bristles on the roof; a water tank on every roof; outside stairs.
  - Roof rights could be a trade good in their economy.
  - Keep it tidy enough not to read as squalor: lit, painted, proud.

## 4. Medellín's Comuna 13: escalators up the hill
- **Outdoor escalators** opened in 2011: 384 m long in **6 sections**, paired up and down, free. They replace a
  35-minute climb with about 6 minutes, roughly a 28-storey climb. The neighbourhood has about 12,000 people.
- Graffiti and murals now cover the route.
- [ABC; Fox; Elevator World; Iberia love2fly]
- **For the Aloft:** **covered escalators and moving walks** up a big island's hill or roof terraces, with orange
  canopies. Murals line every section, and the landings are lit at night.

## 5. Caracas: Torre David
- An unfinished **45-storey** office tower, abandoned in 1994 and occupied from 2007 by over 750 families (about 5,000
  people, by eviction reports).
- Residents built homes, shops, gyms, a church, tailors and salons inside the bare frame. With no lifts, **motorbike
  taxis** ran up the parking-garage ramps.
- ETH / Urban-Think Tank documented it (*Torre David: Informal Vertical Communities*). It has since been emptied.
- [Daily Beast; LensCulture; Urban-Think Tank]
- **For the Aloft:**
  - The **infilled frame**: a huge clan-built concrete skeleton, each bay filled in differently by its occupants with
    different walls, windows and colours.
  - A ramp tower for cargo carts and bikes.
  - A vertical-street lantern at night. Strongly a "stacked warren".

## 6. Colonial grids with balconies: Cartagena, Bogotá's La Candelaria, Cusco
- **Cartagena.**
  - Walled-city mansions with **carved wooden balconies** (often orange or brown) dripping with pink bougainvillea, over
    painted façades.
  - Getsemaní, outside the walls, is "a kaleidoscope of colours".
  - [Rough Guides; voyages-d-affaires]
- **La Candelaria.**
  - Two-storey 16th-century houses with inner courtyards, thick walls and wood-beamed ceilings.
  - Its oldest balconies date to 1602, carved in garlands and friezes.
  - The district is famous for graffiti. [bogota.gov.co; colombia.travel; Monocle]
- **Cusco.** Spanish colonial upper floors with blue-painted balconies on top of Inca fitted-stone bases.
- **Colour.**
  - Cartagena: saffron #e8a33a, coral #e2725b, sky #7fb7d8, lime #c3d65a.
  - Balcony wood #6b3e24.
  - Bougainvillea #c2306a.
  - Cusco: whitewash #f2efe6, balcony blue #2a5ea8.
- **For the Aloft:**
  - The **balcony street**: two- to three-storey courtyard houses with long timber balconies.
  - Flower boxes hold hardy alpines rather than bougainvillea.
  - The stone base / light upper split (Cusco) maps onto the dense skystone below with light timber above.

## 7. Guanajuato (Mexico): *callejones*
- A ravine silver town of colourful houses climbing the slopes, joined by *callejones* (alley-stairs).
- The **Callejón del Beso**: balconies of facing houses about 68–70 cm apart, close enough to kiss across.
- [Journey Mexico; Mexico News Daily; Lonely Planet]
- **For the Aloft:** **kissing balconies**. Facing upper balconies across a 1.5–2 m stair-lane, nearly touching. A
  romance landmark for an island.

## 8. Brazilian Baroque hill towns: Ouro Preto, Salvador's Pelourinho
- **Sobrados** are two-storey colonial houses on steep streets.
  - Because houses were built from the upper street level, a lower floor appears below the main one on the downhill side.
  - Commonly, though not confirmed for Ouro Preto, a shop or storage is below and the residence above.
  - Ouro Preto's historic palette is being researched stratigraphically (ArchDaily 2025).
- **Pelourinho** (Salvador). Sobrados in saturated pastel: pink, blue, yellow, green, along a steep plaza.
- **Colour.**
  - Pelourinho: #f2b5c4, #8fc6e6, #f3d35c, #7cc28e, #e98a5a.
  - Window frames white #f4f1ea or contrast colours.
  - Ouro Preto: white #f1eee6 with blue #2c5b9a or ox red #7a2c22 frames.
- **For the Aloft:** **sobrado slopes**, two-storey houses that gain a storey downhill. Simple and colourful; fill
  between the warrens.

## 9. Buenos Aires: La Boca and Caminito
- **Conventillos**: tenement houses round a central patio, rooms let to immigrant families (often a family a room).
  - They were knocked together from ship planks and **corrugated iron**, and painted with leftover **ship paint** in
    patchwork: each wall or board a different colour, because there wasn't enough of one colour.
  - Genoese immigrants brought the custom.
  - Benito Quinquela Martín revived it in the 1950s.
- Tango, balconies with painted figures.
- [amusingplanet La Boca; Buenos Tours; weblogtheworld]
- **Colour.**
  - Patchwork of #e63b2e, #f4c430, #2d6fb5, #3ea55f, #f08c3a, #8e44ad.
  - Black trim #1f1f1f.
- **For the Aloft:**
  - The **patchwork conventillo**: a patio tenement (rooms on two or three galleried floors round a court) clad in tin.
  - Every panel a different colour. The patchwork itself is the motif.
  - At night, coloured bulbs strung round the patio galleries.
