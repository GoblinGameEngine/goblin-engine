# C3. Canada's stacked and cold-climate towns (research stream C, 2026-10-14)

Canada matters most for this job because it is **cold**, like the Aloft's air. Its towns show how dense living handles
snow, ice and long dark evenings with light.

## 1. Québec City Old Town: Upper and Lower Town, the Breakneck Stairs
- Lower Town (**Petit-Champlain**, Place Royale) sits on a narrow shelf under the Cap Diamant cliff. Upper Town sits on
  top inside the walls.
- They are linked by stairs. The **Escalier Casse-Cou** (Breakneck Stairs) is the oldest: a path from 1635/1670, iron
  stairs by Charles Baillairgé in 1889–93. A **funicular** also runs up the cliff.
- The stairs are lined with shops and tenements on both landings. [quebec-cite.com; urbanguidequebec]
- **Houses.**
  - 17th- and 18th-century French stone houses (e.g. Maison Chevalier, 1752), with deep window sills and massive iron
    hinges.
  - **Steep roofs** (45–60° (est.)) in tin or slate, with **dormer rows**.
  - **Firewalls** with corbels rising above the roofline between houses. After 1720 rules the roofs carry fire ladders
    and walkways: a built-in roof access.
- **Winter.** Snow on the steep roofs. The Christmas market lights the streets, with string lights in the trees and on
  every shopfront in winter.
- **Colour.**
  - Grey fieldstone #8c8780, white-rendered stone #efece4.
  - Roofs: red tin #a33a2b, green tin #4a6e58, black slate #2f3336.
  - Doors and shutters: red #9e2b25, blue #2e4f7a, green #2f5b45.
- **For the Aloft (strong):**
  - The **cliff town**: a lower shelf of stone houses on a terrace under the island's central rise, with the upper town
    on the summit.
  - A **Breakneck stair** plus a **funicular** between them.
  - Firewall parapets with roof ladders give roof access. Steep tin roofs shed the snow.
  - **Winter lights** on everything: this is the closest real look to the "Christmas tree" the user wants.

## 2. Montréal's plexes: outside spiral stairs
- **Form.**
  - Duplexes and triplexes, 1870–1930: one flat per floor, stacked, on party walls, with flat roofs and ornate cornices.
  - A 3 m front setback rule pushed the stairs *outside* to keep floor area. The landlord saved heating, and families had
    separate doors.
  - The city banned outside stairs in 1945 and lifted the ban in the late 1970s.
- **Stairs.** Spiral on narrow lots; straight, L, S or T shapes where there is room.
  - Upper doors open off **galleries** (balconies) at each floor.
  - Iron or wood, painted.
- **Lots.** 14.3–15.2 m wide by 26.5–29 m deep, double or triple units (studies). Narrower lots got the spirals.
- **Sources:** [Atlas Obscura; The Main; Globe and Mail; Construction Canada; Érudit]
- **Colour.**
  - Brick: red #8b3a2a, buff #c9a46b, grey stone #9a958c.
  - Stairs and galleries in wrought iron: black #1d1d1d, forest green #2d4a3a, ox red #7a2a20, sky blue #6c9cc8.
  - Pressed-metal cornices #4b4b4b.
- **Ceiling tiles.** Montréal and old Canadian shops have **pressed-tin ceilings** (stream B covers ceiling tiles).
- **For the Aloft (essential, and cheap):**
  - The **plex stack**: one flat per floor, every upper flat reached by an **outside spiral stair** and a **gallery**.
  - This is the island's signature roof access: the stairs simply continue to the roof.
  - Rows of plexes along the roads make a strong, readable street.

## 3. Habitat 67 (Montréal, Moshe Safdie)
- 354 identical prefabricated concrete **boxes**, about 600 sq ft each (about 11.7 × 5.3 × 3 m; the casting cage was
  38 × 17 ft), 90 t each.
- Stacked in a stepped, ragged pyramid up to **12 storeys**, each box offset from the one below so every unit's roof is
  its neighbour's garden terrace. Steel cables tie them.
- About 146–158 units of 1–8 boxes; pedestrian streets run through the stack at intervals.
- **Sources:** [Arch2o; ArchDaily AD Classics; Canadian Encyclopedia; Designing Buildings]
- **For the Aloft (strong):**
  - The **box pile**: identical modules (one part reused hundreds of times, ideal for footprint) stacked in offset
    stepped piles.
  - Roof terraces on every box; walkways through the pile; balloon tethers anchored on the tops.

## 4. St. John's (Newfoundland): Jellybean Row
- Wooden row houses following the old lanes up the steep harbour slope.
  - The Globe and Mail calls them flat-roofed; mansard-like fronts also appear, and the "saltbox with a linney" is a
    local type.
  - They are clad in clapboard and painted vivid **jellybean** colours.
- Templeton's hardware has sold Matchless paints since 1902. Its Heritage Colours include a "Jelly Bean Palette".
- The fog story (colours so sailors find home) is romantic, not documented.
- **Sources:** [NL Tourism; Globe and Mail; Senate of Canada; Behr]
- **Colour.**
  - Jellybean: Point Amour Red #c23b33, Sugar Cube White #f4f2ec, purple #6d3a7e, teal #1f8a8a, sunflower #f2c230,
    lime #8cbf3f, sky #4aa3d8, tangerine #ef7d2d.
- **For the Aloft:** **jellybean rows**. Clapboard row houses stepping up a slope, each a different vivid colour with
  white trim. Bright against snow.

## 5. Lunenburg (Nova Scotia)
- A UNESCO British colonial grid on a steep slope down to the harbour. Wooden houses in strong colours.
- The **"Lunenburg bump"** is a 5-sided dormer projecting from the roof over the front door, often stacked over a bay
  window. (Common knowledge; the search did not cover it.)
- Waterfront fish sheds are painted ox red #8e2f25.
- **For the Aloft:** **bump dormers**, projecting multi-sided bays on the roof slope. Cheap and very readable, with a
  lamp in each at night.

## 6. Dawson City (Yukon) and Gastown (Vancouver)
- **Boomtown false fronts (Dawson, 1896–1900).**
  - A tall rectangular parapet front hides a plain gable roof, giving a one- or two-storey timber shop an "urban" look
    and room for a big **painted sign**.
  - The Yukon Hotel is 2½ storeys of massing behind a 3-storey false front.
  - Winaut's Store has a deep entablature on four columns on corbels.
- Sources: [historicplaces.ca; Parks Canada; NPS Skagway; kupi]
- **Gastown / Granville (Vancouver).**
  - Vancouver once had about **19,000 neon signs**, one per 18 people, rivalling Las Vegas. Granville's Theatre Row in
    the 1950s was one of the world's densest neon displays.
  - 1970s bylaws removed most of them; survivors such as the Smilin' Buddha sign are in the Museum of Vancouver.
  - Gastown has cobbles, brick warehouses and the steam clock.
  - [Burnaby Now; Destination Vancouver; Forbidden Vancouver; CBC]
- **Colour.**
  - False fronts: barn red #8a2e22, cream #ede1c4, mustard #c9a03a, slate #4c5a63.
  - Sign lettering in gilt and black.
- **For the Aloft (strong):**
  - **False fronts are signs**: each shop's front is a tall flat board above the roof, a giant sign surface, lit at night.
  - **Neon Granville**: a road of neon over the shops, the canonical North American neon street.
