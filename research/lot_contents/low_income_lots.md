# Low-Income Lots (cross-cutting income tier)

## 1. Overview

Income, not just house era, is the primary axis the brief asks this catalog to sample on — this file captures the item-pool *reductions and substitutions* that apply to a low-income household regardless of which era/setting file (`prewar_intown_lots.md`, `postwar_suburban_lots.md`, `rural_farm_adjacent_lots.md`) its house archetype normally belongs to. Use this file as a **modifier layer**: start from the relevant era/setting file's baseline percentages, then apply the adjustments below. Low income correlates most strongly with the **shotgun house, mobile/manufactured home, small starter home, and older/un-renovated duplex or apartment-adjacent stock**, but can appear on any archetype (a Foursquare or Victorian in genuine disrepair, a farmhouse on a struggling small farm).

## 2. General pattern

The defining material-culture signal of a low-income lot is not absence of "stuff" — often the opposite (older vehicles, more visible storage, more informal/improvised structures) — but **absence of discretionary, purchased, permitted amenities** (pool, deck, permitted shed, formal landscaping) combined with **higher visibility of maintenance deferral** (peeling paint, a tarped roof section, a yard that's mowed but not landscaped, a driveway that's cracked/unpaved rather than freshly sealed).

## 3. Garage / driveway typology

- **Detached garage present but often in visibly poor repair, or absent entirely**: garage presence rate drops 15-25 percentage points below the era/setting baseline; where present, sag/patch/mismatched-repair visual cues are appropriate.
- **No garage, on-street or driveway-only parking**: elevated relative to baseline — cars parked on a gravel/dirt strip or directly on cracked concrete/asphalt with no garage at all is the modal case for low-income in-town and mobile-home lots.
- **Driveway material**: gravel, dirt, or old/cracked/patched concrete or asphalt — new, freshly-sealed asphalt or stamped/decorative concrete essentially never appears on this tier; unpaved (dirt/gravel, even in town where paving would be the norm) is a reliable low-income visual signal.
- **Multiple vehicles, at least one older/higher-mileage-coded or partially disassembled**: elevated rate (~20-30%) of a "project car" or inoperable vehicle visible in the driveway or side yard — a real, well-documented material-culture signal of lower-income households, tied to the economics of vehicle repair-vs-replace decisions; sample tastefully and not as a caricature.

## 4. Outbuildings

- **Storage shed**: present but often informal/DIY-built or a low-cost prefab kit rather than a finished, painted-to-match structure; permit-exempt small sheds (under the common ~120 sq ft no-permit threshold) are the norm here precisely because a permit fee and formal construction are added costs this tier avoids.
- **Detached workshop / greenhouse**: rare to absent (~0-5%) — these are the most clearly discretionary/hobby outbuildings in the catalog and track income closely.

## 5. Gardens & landscaping

- **Vegetable garden**: actually *elevated* relative to some middle-income suburban baselines (~35-50%) — a food-budget-driven motivation (home food production as a genuine cost offset) that partially counteracts the general "amenities decline with income" pattern; garden here is more likely to be informal/unedged (a tilled patch, not a landscaped bed) than on higher-income tiers.
- **Foundation plantings**: present but minimal/overgrown or absent (~40-55%, below the 85%+ baseline of the era/setting files) — the clearest single "why" here is maintenance cost/time and the fact foundation planting is a discretionary/curb-appeal investment rather than a functional necessity.
- **Lawn condition**: functionally mowed but visually rougher — bare/dirt patches, minimal edging, dandelions/clover rather than a manicured monoculture lawn — this is a legitimate, well-documented low-cost-vs-high-cost lawn-care visual signal for the generator to use instead of overt "clutter."

## 6. Leisure/recreation

- **In-ground pool**: 0% — cost-prohibitive by a wide margin ($66,000 average vs. this tier's typical home value), never sample.
- **Above-ground pool**: still present at a modest rate (~5-8%) — the lowest-cost pool format specifically makes it one of the few "pool" options accessible to this tier, so don't zero it out, just keep it well below the middle/upper-middle rate.
- **Deck**: rare (~10-15%), usually small/basic or absent; **patio**: more likely than deck if either exists (a poured slab is cheaper than built carpentry).
- **Swing set/playset**: present at a moderate rate (~20-30%) but skews toward a simple/older/hand-me-down set rather than a large modern multi-module structure.
- **Firepit**: informal ring rather than built masonry, moderate rate (~20-30%).
- **Basketball hoop**: portable/movable pole-base hoop (cheaper, no permanent mount) more common than a garage-mounted backboard.

## 7. Vehicles/equipment stored outdoors

- Utility trailer, older boat, or camper stored on-site at a *higher* rate than the income level alone would predict, when the lot is rural/unincorporated (see `rural_farm_adjacent_lots.md`) — storing rather than paying for offsite storage is itself an income-driven choice, and lack of code enforcement in low-income unincorporated areas allows it; in-town, code enforcement (particularly RV/boat visibility ordinances) suppresses this signal more than income alone would.

## 8. Pets/animals

- **Doghouse/dog run**: present at a comparable or slightly elevated rate vs. baseline, typically a simple DIY doghouse and a stake/tie-out line rather than a built kennel run.
- **Backyard chickens**: present where locally legal, sometimes marginally over ordinance limits (a realistic, low-stakes "why" detail — food-cost motivation outweighing minor compliance risk) — sample sparingly and only as a rural-village or large-lot detail, not a caricature.

## 9. Fencing

- **No fence, or a single stretch of sagging/mismatched chain-link**: dominant pattern; chain-link (cheapest fence material) is disproportionately common on this tier wherever any fence exists at all, versus wood privacy or ornamental fencing on higher tiers.

## 10. Utility items

- **Trash/recycling bins**: often visible from the street more consistently than ordinance allows (a realistic small-scale "compliance gap" detail, e.g. bins left at the curb past the collection-day window that several municipal codes explicitly regulate against) rather than a hidden, screened storage area.
- **Mailbox**: older, sometimes dented/leaning post-mounted box rather than a decorative unit — a simple, low-cost, unrenovated USPS-standard box is period/income-appropriate.

## 11. Climate items

Clotheslines are meaningfully *more* common on this tier (~35-45%, above every other income tier) — the clearest, most literal income signal in the whole climate-items category: a clothesline eliminates a real, recurring utility cost (electric or gas dryer operation), and this tradeoff is well documented as a driver behind "wealthy suburban communities ban clotheslines" HOA culture versus lower-income/rural persistence of the practice. See `great_lakes_climate_items.md` for the full comparative table across tiers.

## 12. Building-to-lot relationship notes

- **`shotgun.md`**: The catalog's smallest, historically most economical archetype; this file's reductions (no garage, informal shed if any, chain-link-or-nothing fencing) apply almost by default rather than as a deviation from a wealthier baseline.
- **`mobile_home.md`**: The archetype most consistently paired with this income tier; combine this file's modifiers with `rural_farm_adjacent_lots.md`'s low-regulation pattern (gravel driveway, stored trailers/equipment, informal shed) for the fullest picture.
- **`starter_home_minimal_traditional.md`**: A postwar/`postwar_suburban_lots.md`-pattern archetype (built explicitly as affordable/entry-level housing) that should show this file's driveway-material and landscaping reductions layered onto the smaller attached-garage baseline of that file, rather than the alley-garage pattern.
- Older/un-renovated examples of **`foursquare.md`**, **`victorian_queen_anne.md`**, or **`duplex.md`** (a Victorian or Foursquare that has become a low-income rental rather than an owner-occupied restored home) are a legitimate low-income variant within `prewar_intown_lots.md` — apply this file's maintenance-deferral and fencing/driveway modifiers to that file's baseline rather than treating income and pre-war-in-town as mutually exclusive.
