# Rural / Farm-Adjacent Lots (rural tier — villages, pop. 500-5,000, and outlying farmsteads)

## 1. Overview

This file covers lots in the **rural tier**: farmhouses on working or former farmland, mobile/manufactured homes on rural parcels, and rural-village in-town lots that are still large/unplatted enough to carry farm-adjacent habits (large gardens, livestock-adjacent outbuildings, equipment storage). This is the least regulated lot type in the catalog — many rural townships have no zoning or only light agricultural zoning, so this is also the tier with the **widest variance and the most "everything on the lot" density** of any tier: outbuildings, vehicles, and equipment accumulate here in a way in-town and suburban lots don't, because there's no ordinance and no HOA stopping it and genuine functional need (farm equipment, firewood, larger family food production) driving it.

**Population tier**: rural (villages, pop. 500-5,000) per the population-tier research; also applies to unincorporated farmstead parcels outside any village limit that would feed the same zone.

## 2. Lot layout baseline

- Lot size: highly variable, from a large in-village lot (0.25-1 acre) to a full farmstead parcel (several acres of yard/dooryard space around the house, distinct from the cropland itself).
- No alley system (rural platting, not urban grid) — driveway runs directly from the road to the house/farmyard.
- The **farmyard/dooryard cluster**: house, garage, and 1-3 additional outbuildings (barn, machine shed, corn crib on older farmsteads, modern pole barn on active ones) grouped together near the road, with cropland/pasture beyond — this clustering is itself the defining "lot content" pattern of this tier.
- Long gravel or dirt driveway, often several hundred feet, is itself a defining visual/functional feature distinct from the short paved aprons of the other two tiers.

## 3. Garage / driveway typology

- **Detached garage**: 60-75% of rural-tier houses, sited near the house but as a separate small building (not a rear-alley pattern — there's no alley — but genuinely detached, often predating the automobile-era retrofit-a-garage habit seen in town).
- **No garage / vehicles parked in farmyard or under a pole-barn bay**: 20-30%, especially where a larger pole barn or machine shed already provides vehicle shelter and a dedicated garage was never built.
- **Driveway material**: gravel is the dominant material by far (~70-80%) — cheapest and most practical for a long rural driveway subject to farm-vehicle traffic and frost heave; asphalt (~10-15%) appears on longer-established, higher-income farmsteads or where the driveway is short enough to be affordable; concrete is rare except for a small pad directly at the garage/house.
  - *Why gravel dominates here specifically*: driveway length (often 300-800+ ft from road to house) makes paving cost-prohibitive relative to the short suburban apron, and gravel drains and handles frost-heave/snowplowing better over an unmaintained rural roadbed than a rigid paved surface would.
- **Carport**: uncommon; mobile/manufactured homes on this tier sometimes have a small attached carport (~15-20% of mobile home lots) as a low-cost alternative to a full garage.

## 4. Outbuildings — the dominant item pool on this tier

- **Detached garage**: 60-75% (above).
- **Storage shed**: 50-65%, larger than the suburban shed (often 10x12 to 12x16 ft), holding lawn/garden equipment separate from farm equipment proper.
- **Barn / machine shed / pole barn**: on any lot still functionally connected to farming (a true working farmstead as opposed to a rural residential parcel that merely has a big yard), 70-90% will have at least one large outbuilding of this kind — but note this is adjacent to, not inside, the residential lot-contents scope of this catalog; treat it as a visible neighbor structure rather than an item the house's own lot pool needs to sample per-house on smaller rural-village lots that aren't active farms.
- **Detached workshop**: 25-35%, more common here than any other tier — rural households skew toward self-sufficiency/repair culture (equipment maintenance, woodworking), and space is abundant and unregulated.
- **Greenhouse / hoop house**: 8-15%, more common than suburban/in-town rates given the larger garden culture of this tier; ranges from a small hobby greenhouse to a serious hoop house for a market-garden household.
- **Corn crib / grain bin (older/decorative rather than functional on residential-only parcels)**: legacy structure on some older farmsteads (pre-1960s), increasingly rare as working infrastructure but sometimes retained as a yard feature; sample at low probability (~5-10%) as a period/flavor detail rather than a functional item.

## 5. Gardens & landscaping

- **Vegetable garden**: 55-70% of rural-tier lots — the highest rate of any tier, consistent with the rural-vs-urban gardening research (36% of rural-county residents report a garden vs. 28.5% urban), and typically much larger than an in-town garden bed (500-2,000+ sq ft row-garden rather than a small edged bed), often fenced against deer/rabbit.
  - *Why*: direct food-production tradition, larger available land, generally lower grocery-delivery convenience justifying home production, and in many households genuine continuity with active farming.
- **Foundation plantings**: present but less manicured/formal than in-town or suburban examples (~50-65%) — practical/informal plantings rather than a designed foundation bed.
- **Orchard/fruit trees**: 15-25%, a handful of apple/pear/cherry trees near the house, a legacy homestead-orchard habit.
- **Hedge/windbreak row**: 20-30%, functional (windbreak against Great Lakes winter wind) rather than purely ornamental — see `great_lakes_climate_items.md`.

## 6. Leisure/recreation

- **Swing set/playset**: 25-35%, often homemade/wood rather than a purchased kit given the DIY culture and space of this tier.
- **In-ground pool**: 2-4%, rare — the cost of a farmstead-scale septic/well system plus a pool is a bigger stretch here than in a municipal-utility subdivision, and pools are a less established cultural norm in working-farm households.
- **Above-ground pool**: 8-12%, the practical/affordable option where a pool exists at all, same logic as the low/middle-income suburban tier.
- **Firepit/fire ring**: 35-50% — the single most common "leisure" item on this tier, informal and often large (burn-pile-adjacent), reflecting both genuine recreational use and the practical habit of burning yard/brush debris on unregulated rural land.
- **Basketball hoop**: 20-30%, pole-mounted at the edge of the farmyard/driveway turnaround rather than over a garage door.

## 7. Vehicles/equipment stored outdoors — the strongest tier-differentiator

- **Farm equipment (tractor, implements, wagons)**: on active farm parcels, near-universal; parked in the farmyard or under a machine shed, visible from the house lot even if not technically "residential" property.
- **Boat**: 15-25% of rural-tier lots in the Great Lakes region — meaningfully higher than suburban/in-town rates, reflecting both higher per-capita boat ownership in this region (e.g., Minnesota's ~14% ownership rate, among the highest in the US) and the lack of zoning restriction on where a boat/trailer can sit.
- **RV / camper / travel trailer**: 15-20%, parked beside the house or garage; rural/agricultural-zoned parcels are explicitly exempted from the acreage/commercial-zoning restrictions that govern RV storage in town (residential zoning "almost never permits" a storage *business*, but individual rural parcels storing their own RV face far fewer restrictions than in-town lots).
- **Utility trailer**: 30-45%, the single most common piece of stored equipment on this tier — general-purpose farm/yard-work trailer.
- **ATV / side-by-side**: 20-30%, functional (field/property access) as much as recreational.
- **Older/inoperable vehicles ("yard cars")**: 10-20% at the lower end of this tier's income range — a genuinely period/regionally-real detail (absence of code enforcement on unincorporated land) but should be sampled conservatively/tastefully as a rare flavor detail, not a default.

## 8. Pets/animals

- **Doghouse / dog run**: 40-55%, often a simple doghouse with a long run-line rather than a fenced kennel — working/farm dogs common on this tier.
- **Backyard chickens**: 30-45% on rural-tier lots — the *unregulated* end of the chicken-keeping spectrum (contrast with the "backyard chickens as a specific in-town exception" note in `_lot_contents_index.md`); coop is typically a simple wood structure near the garden or a repurposed shed, no setback rules apply outside incorporated village limits.
- **Small livestock (goats, a few sheep) on larger parcels**: 5-10%, sample sparingly as a large-parcel/hobby-farm flavor detail rather than a default.

## 9. Fencing tied to yard use

- **No fence around the house yard itself**: dominant (~60-70%) — open dooryard is the norm; fencing on this tier is functional (garden fence against deer/rabbit, livestock fence, dog-yard fence) rather than a boundary/privacy statement.
- **Garden fence**: 40-55% wherever a vegetable garden exists — short wire/mesh fence specifically to exclude deer and rabbits, a genuine functional necessity in this tier that in-town gardens rarely need.
- **Chain-link, dog yard**: 20-30%.

## 10. Utility items

- **Trash/recycling bin storage**: often not municipal-collection at all on true rural/unincorporated parcels (self-haul to a transfer station, or burn-barrel disposal, both period/regionally real); where curbside collection exists (rural-village in-town lots), bins are stored beside the garage/driveway with much less strict visibility ordinance enforcement than the other tiers.
- **Mailbox**: single post-mounted box at the end of the driveway on the road, OR (increasingly since the 1970s-80s and near-universal on new rural postal routes) a shared **cluster box unit (CBU)** at a road junction serving several farmsteads — sample single roadside box as the default for older/established farmsteads and a CBU as the default for anything platted or added to a route since the 1970s USPS centralized-delivery push.
- **Burn barrel**: 25-35%, a genuinely period/regional rural utility item (trash/brush burning) distinct from a recreational firepit.

## 11. Climate items

See `great_lakes_climate_items.md` for the full pool. Rural-specific notes: **woodpile** prevalence is highest on this tier of any in the catalog (~35-50% keep some woodpile, vs. much lower rates in-town/suburban) — wood heat (primary or supplemental) is disproportionately a rural practice nationally, and this tier has the space, source material (own woodlot/windbreak trimmings), and often genuine heating-cost motivation to maintain one; **storm-prep culture** (root cellar/storm cellar) is also strongest here — the true root-cellar-as-storm-shelter building type (distinct from a finished suburban basement) is a rural-farmstead-specific structure with real Midwest/Tornado-Alley historical roots, appropriate as an occasional (~10-15%) older-farmhouse item; snow-removal equipment often includes a tractor-mounted blade/blower in addition to or instead of a walk-behind snowblower, reflecting the larger driveway length.

## 12. Building-to-lot relationship notes

- **`farmhouse_vernacular.md`**: The defining archetype for this entire file — expect the full farmyard cluster (house + detached garage + barn/machine shed + garden + orchard), gravel driveway, and the highest woodpile/firewood and garden rates in the catalog.
- **`mobile_home.md`**: Smaller lot footprint than the full farmstead but shares this tier's low-regulation, gravel-driveway, high-outbuilding-tolerance pattern; frequently paired with a small attached carport (uncommon on the farmhouse archetype), a storage shed, and a higher rate of stored utility trailers/ATVs relative to its income level specifically because there's no ordinance stopping it — see `low_income_lots.md` for the income-driven variant of this archetype's lot profile.
- Any **rural-village in-town lot** occupied by a Foursquare, Bungalow, or Cape Cod archetype (per the population-tier research noting rural villages skew toward older archetypes) should blend this file's low-regulation/large-garden/gravel-driveway pattern with a smaller, more `prewar_intown_lots.md`-style footprint — treat rural-village lots as roughly this file's item-pool percentages applied to a smaller (village in-town, not farmstead-scale) lot.
