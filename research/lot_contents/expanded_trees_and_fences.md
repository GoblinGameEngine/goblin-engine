# Expanded Trees/Vegetation and Fences — South Shore Great Lakes (OH/IN/MI/IL/WI)

Research-only file expanding the station's tree/vegetation and fence catalogs beyond what's
currently modeled. Existing catalog for reference:
- **Trees (7)**: tree_autumn, tree_bare_dead, tree_conifer, tree_round, tree_shrubby_small,
  tree_tall_narrow, tree_weeping (bushes/flowers/weeds already covered separately).
- **Fences (4 modeled + 3 identified-but-not-yet-modeled)**: fence_chain_short, fence_chain_tall,
  fence_picket, fence_privacy (modeled); farm field fence, corral/livestock fence, split-rail
  fence (identified as needed, not yet modeled).

This file proposes *additional* species/styles on top of both lists, mapped to the station's four
zones (Lake, Downtown, Residential, Farm), each with a one-line regional "why" and a source.

## Part 1 — Expanded tree/vegetation species

### Downtown street trees

US Midwest municipal urban-forestry departments (Chicago, Columbus, Minneapolis, and most
mid-size Midwest cities) plant from a well-documented shortlist chosen for narrow/upright form,
paving/root tolerance, salt tolerance, and — critically — **species diversity**, a policy adopted
directly in response to the two regional monoculture die-offs below (Dutch elm disease, then
emerald ash borer).

| Species | Zone/use | Why | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Honeylocust (*Gleditsia triacanthos*, thornless cultivars) | Downtown street tree | Classic American downtown street tree: light dappled canopy, tolerates compacted urban soil and road salt, thornless cultivars bred specifically for street use | Common Midwest municipal urban-forestry species list (Chicago, Columbus, and peer-city parkway programs) |
| Littleleaf linden (*Tilia cordata*) | Downtown street tree | Narrow pyramidal form suited to tight downtown tree-lawns/medians, heavy fragrant bloom, widely planted Main Street tree across the Midwest | Common Midwest municipal urban-forestry species list |
| London planetree (*Platanus × acanthifolia*) | Downtown street tree | Extremely pollution- and compaction-tolerant with distinctive mottled bark; a signature big-city downtown tree adapted for wide commercial streets | Common Midwest municipal urban-forestry species list |
| Red maple / Freeman maple hybrids (*Acer rubrum*, *Acer × freemanii*) | Downtown street tree, also residential yard | Reliable brilliant fall color, moderate size, widely substituted into downtown plantings after ash losses | Common Midwest municipal urban-forestry species list |
| Kentucky coffeetree (*Gymnocladus dioicus*) | Downtown street tree (diversity pick) | A genuinely Midwest-native species increasingly promoted by municipal foresters specifically to diversify plantings away from any single-species monoculture after the elm and ash die-offs | Post-EAB municipal diversification guidance (see EAB entry below) |

### Residential yard trees (by era)

| Species | Zone/use | Why | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Large mature American elm (*Ulmus americana*), surviving specimens | Residential, older/pre-war neighborhoods (rare, "legacy tree" variant) | Pre-1950s Midwest neighborhoods were planted almost exclusively with American elm as a street/yard tree for its vase-shaped canopy; the very few survivors (Dutch elm disease resistant escapees or treated specimens) read as a notable "old money" legacy tree in an older neighborhood | [Dutch Elm Disease — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_elm_disease): of ~77 million North American elms present in 1930, over 75% were dead by 1989; the fungus reached Detroit by 1950, Chicago by 1960 |
| Bur oak / red oak / white oak (*Quercus macrocarpa*, *Q. rubra*, *Q. alba*) | Residential, older neighborhoods; also farm zone (yard trees, woodlot remnants) | Large mature oaks are the classic surviving big shade tree of older Midwest neighborhoods precisely because oaks were largely unaffected by Dutch elm disease and emerald ash borer, so they now dominate the oldest, largest-canopy residential blocks | Regional urban-forestry history (elm/ash die-off context, above) |
| Sugar maple / Norway maple (*Acer saccharum*, *Acer platanoides*) | Residential yard tree, all eras | The other dominant Midwest yard shade tree alongside oak, valued for fall color; Norway maple especially common in mid-20th-century subdivisions as a fast-growing replacement tree | Common Midwest residential yard-tree species |
| White ash / green ash (*Fraxinus americana*, *F. pennsylvanica*) — as a **dead/removed-stump variant**, not a living tree | Residential and downtown, all eras (condition variant) | Ash was itself the dominant replacement species planted after Dutch elm disease wiped out street elms — making its own near-total loss to emerald ash borer a second, compounding regional die-off; a dead-ash-stump or freshly-removed-tree-pit prop is a highly period/region-accurate detail for any lot in a neighborhood with mature 20th-century plantings | [Emerald Ash Borer — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emerald_ash_borer): first identified in Canton, Michigan in 2002; without control, infested ash trees die within ~10 years; many of the ash lost had themselves been planted as elm replacements; Minneapolis's urban forest was >20% ash at the time of infestation |
| Eastern white pine / blue spruce (*Pinus strobus*, *Picea pungens*) | Residential yard tree (evergreen accent), postwar suburban | Common foundation/specimen evergreen planted in postwar-suburban (1950s-70s) yards for year-round screening and a distinct silhouette from the deciduous shade-tree canopy | Common Midwest residential landscaping practice |

### Farm zone

| Species | Zone/use | Why | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eastern redcedar (*Juniperus virginiana*) | Farm — windbreak/shelterbelt | A dense, drought- and wind-tolerant native conifer, one of the most commonly planted Midwest farmstead windbreak species for year-round wind protection on the exposed/upwind side of a farmstead | Regional USDA/extension windbreak guidance (conifer shelterbelt practice, general Midwest farmstead design) |
| Norway spruce / white spruce (*Picea abies*, *Picea glauca*) | Farm — windbreak/shelterbelt | Dense evergreen with strong lower-branch retention, a standard multi-row shelterbelt conifer planted specifically to block winter wind and reduce snow drift onto farmstead buildings and driveways | Regional USDA/extension windbreak guidance |
| Osage orange (*Maclura pomifera*) | Farm — fence-line volunteer / relic hedgerow tree | Historically planted as a deliberate thorny living-hedge fence ("horse-high, bull-strong, and pig-tight") to contain livestock before barbed wire became widely available in the 1870s-80s; surviving gnarled fence-line specimens along old field boundaries are a genuinely period-correct rural Midwest detail | [Osage Orange — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osage_orange): barbed wire (invented 1874) gradually superseded Osage-orange hedges; the trees were "widely naturalized throughout the United States" as a "crucial stop-gap measure ... until the introduction of barbed wire" |
| Apple (*Malus domestica*) | Farm — orchard | Michigan is a top-tier US apple producer; orchard rows of apple trees are a genuine, well-documented regional farm-zone feature, especially in the Lake Michigan fruit belt | Michigan fruit-belt agricultural context (Lake Michigan's moderating lake-effect climate supports commercial fruit growing) |
| Tart/sweet cherry (*Prunus cerasus* / *P. avium*) | Farm — orchard | The Grand Traverse Bay region of Michigan is nationally known as the "Cherry Capital of the World," with the region's cherry orchards and the annual National Cherry Festival (~500,000 visitors) a defining piece of regional agricultural identity | [Traverse City, Michigan — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traverse_City,_Michigan) |
| Black walnut (*Juglans nigra*) | Farm — fence-line/woodlot tree | A valuable native hardwood commonly left standing along fence lines and field edges rather than cleared, both for its timber value and because its allelopathic root chemistry (juglone) discourages competing crops right at the margin — a recognizable "farm edge" tree distinct from windbreak conifers | Common Midwest farm-woodlot practice |

### Riparian/waterside (Lake zone and creek/drainage features)

| Species | Zone/use | Why | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Black willow (*Salix nigra*) | Lake zone, streambank/shoreline | Willows are a classic, fast-colonizing streambank species across the eastern US, stabilizing eroding banks with dense root mats | General riparian-ecology confirmation (willow genus documented as a defining riparian tree type) |
| Eastern cottonwood (*Populus deltoides*) | Lake zone, streambank/floodplain | A defining Midwest riparian tree: naturally regenerates on fresh mud banks left by flooding, with a native range explicitly including the Great Lakes shoreline (documented near Lake Erie/Lake Ontario) | [Eastern Cottonwood — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_cottonwood): "usually grows near rivers, with mud banks left after floods providing ideal conditions for seedling germination"; range includes areas "near Lake Ontario and Lake Erie" |
| American sycamore (*Platanus occidentalis*) | Lake zone, streambank/floodplain | Distinctive mottled white-bark tree that is one of the largest native eastern US floodplain/streambank trees, visually distinct from upland species and a strong "waterside" silhouette marker | General riparian-ecology confirmation (sycamore genus documented as a classic riparian tree; western counterpart *Platanus racemosa* confirmed as California's riparian sycamore, same genus/habit as the eastern species used here) |

## Part 2 — Expanded fence styles

Existing catalog: fence_chain_short, fence_chain_tall, fence_picket, fence_privacy (modeled);
farm field fence, corral/livestock fence, split-rail fence (identified, not yet modeled). The
following add real regional variety spanning upper-income suburban to heavy industrial to
distinctly regional winter-climate infrastructure.

| Fence type | Zone/use | Why | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ornamental aluminum/wrought-iron-style fence | Residential (upper-income), Downtown (historic district, small civic lots) | Decorative metal picket fencing (real wrought iron historically, aluminum today as a low-maintenance substitute) is the standard upper-income/historic-district fence choice distinct from wood picket or chain-link — genuinely fills the "suburban decorative" gap above plain picket | Common American residential fencing material category (wrought iron historically favored for its ornamental strength; aluminum now the dominant modern low-maintenance substitute) |
| Snow fence (orange plastic mesh, temporary) | Farm, Lake zone, roadside open-field edges | A genuinely distinctive Great Lakes/upper-Midwest winter-climate item: temporary perforated-plastic or wood-lath fencing staked along the windward side of roads and open fields specifically to force snow to drift and deposit before it reaches the roadway — several Midwest/Plains DOTs (Iowa among them) run dedicated "living snow fence" programs pairing this practice with windbreak plantings | [Snow fence — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snow_fence): describes "perforated orange plastic sheeting attached to stakes" and permanent wood-slat fences, placed ~35+ fence-heights upwind of the road/building being protected, to control where snow drifts |
| Snow fence (wood-slat/lath, semi-permanent) | Farm, roadside | The more durable/semi-permanent counterpart to the plastic version — wood lath-and-wire construction used on the same wind-exposed field edges, sometimes left up year-round | [Snow fence — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snow_fence) |
| High-tensile electric fence | Farm — livestock pasture | Modern smooth high-tensile wire strung with an electric charge is the current standard for cattle/livestock containment on active Midwest farms, visually and functionally distinct from older woven-wire "field fence" and split-rail — pairs naturally with the farm zone's existing corral/field-fence entries as the "modern" option alongside them | Standard contemporary US livestock-fencing practice, distinct from woven/barbed wire predecessors |
| Deer fencing (tall woven mesh or multi-strand) | Farm — orchard/vineyard perimeter | Michigan's fruit belt (apple, cherry, and a growing wine-grape/vineyard industry around Traverse City's AVAs) makes deer exclusion fencing around orchard and vineyard blocks a real, regionally specific farm-zone feature distinct from livestock fencing | [Traverse City, Michigan — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traverse_City,_Michigan): confirms the region's cherry orchards and viticulture (wineries, AVAs) as major regional agriculture, which conventionally requires deer/wildlife exclusion fencing |
| Chain-link security fence, industrial-gauge with top rail (already-modeled fence_chain_tall is a good base, but industrial sites typically add razor/barbed-wire topping) | Downtown/industrial edge — grain elevator, water tower, substation, small factory yard | Chain-link topped with barbed or razor wire is the standard low-cost perimeter security fence for utility and light-industrial sites — directly applicable to the station's already-catalogued grain elevator, water tower, and small-factory building types | [Chain-link fencing — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain-link_fencing): documents "a chain-link privacy fence topped with razor wire protecting a utility power substation" as a standard industrial security application |
| Low iron/aluminum cemetery fence or perimeter wall segment | Downtown/Residential edge — cemetery, if included as a civic feature | Historic and contemporary American cemeteries commonly use low ornamental iron or masonry perimeter fencing distinct from both residential and industrial fence types; worth flagging as fence-adjacent set dressing if a cemetery is added to the civic building catalog | General American cemetery-design convention (ornamental iron/masonry perimeter, distinct low scale from residential fencing) |
| Farm gate (freestanding metal tube-and-panel livestock gate, not a full fence run) | Farm — field/pasture entries | Not a fence per se but the near-universal companion prop to field/corral/high-tensile fencing — every pasture or field fence run needs a gate, and the tube-steel livestock gate is the standard modern version, visually distinct from a wood farm gate | Standard contemporary US livestock-fencing practice |

## Priority notes

**Highest priority (most visually/functionally distinct, strong regional grounding):**
- Trees: Kentucky coffeetree / oak-dominance in old neighborhoods (visually reinforces the
  elm-die-off history uniquely), the dead-ash-stump condition variant (recent, ongoing, doubly
  regional since ash replaced elm), osage orange fence-line relic tree, cherry/apple orchard rows.
- Fences: snow fence (plastic and wood-slat) is the single most Great-Lakes-distinctive item in
  either list — nothing else currently planned signals "upper Midwest winter" as immediately.
  High-tensile electric fence and chain-link-with-razor-wire for industrial sites are also
  high-value since they're functionally distinct fence *behaviors*, not just re-skins.

**Lower priority (nice-to-have, more marginal differentiation):**
- Ornamental aluminum/wrought-iron fence (useful but visually adjacent to existing picket fence
  at a distance).
- Cemetery fencing (contingent on whether a cemetery is ever added to the civic building set —
  flagged as conditional, not a confirmed near-term need).
- Deer fencing (valuable but narrow — only relevant to orchard/vineyard-adjacent farm lots).
- Storm-damaged tree variant (adds flavor but is a low-frequency rare-event prop, not a
  core catalog item).
