# Pre-War In-Town Lots (c. 1870-1940 construction, narrow in-town lots)

## 1. Overview

This file covers the lot pattern that goes with the **narrow, gridded, in-town lots** platted before c. 1940: American Foursquare, Victorian/Queen Anne, Bungalow/Craftsman, Shotgun House, Rowhouse/Townhouse, and the older half of Duplex stock. These lots were platted on a dense street grid (typically 30-50 ft / 9-15m frontage, 100-150 ft / 30-46m depth) with a **service alley running along the rear lot line** — the single most defining structural feature of this lot type, and the reason its garage/outbuilding pattern differs sharply from postwar suburban lots (see `postwar_suburban_lots.md`).

Applies across income tiers (a modest shotgun-house lot and a grand Victorian lot share the same alley-and-narrow-frontage DNA even though item quality/quantity differs) — cross-reference `low_income_lots.md` and `upper_middle_income_lots.md` for income-driven variation layered on top of this era/setting baseline.

**Population tiers**: small town and small city (this lot type does not occur in the rural tier, which was never platted on a grid — see `rural_farm_adjacent_lots.md`).

## 2. Lot layout baseline

- Frontage: 25-50 ft (7.6-15m); shotgun/rowhouse lots at the narrow end (25-35 ft), Foursquare/Victorian at the wider end (40-60 ft).
- Depth: 100-140 ft (30-43m), with a rear service alley (12-20 ft / 3.7-6m wide) running behind the block, shared by all lots on the block face.
- Front yard: shallow (10-20 ft / 3-6m setback), largely ornamental — small lawn panel + foundation planting, not a use space.
- Side yards: narrow (3-10 ft), often just a mowing strip; frequently a single driveway strip carved into one side yard leading to the alley or a side street, especially on corner lots.
- Back yard: the real "use" yard — garden, garage, clothesline, doghouse, and (Victorian/Foursquare) sometimes an outbuilding, all packed into a lot maybe 25-45 ft deep behind the house.

## 3. Garage / driveway typology — THE defining item pool

- **Detached garage accessed from the rear alley**: present on ~70-85% of pre-1945 in-town lots on blocks that retain their alley (many small-city blocks do; some small-town blocks had alleys vacated/built-over later). Size 1-car (10x18 ft / 3x5.5m) to 2-car (18x20 ft / 5.5x6m), wood-frame, gable roof matching or plainer than the house, sited hard against the rear (alley) lot line.
  - *Why*: these lots were platted and the houses built **before mass automobile ownership** (garages retrofitted onto Victorian/Foursquare lots from the 1910s-30s onward). The alley was already there for coal/ash/trash service; sliding a garage onto the alley reused existing infrastructure instead of cutting a new curb cut and driveway through the shallow, ornamental front yard. A driveway to the street would have consumed the one formal, image-facing part of the lot.
  - No garage at all: ~15-25% of these lots (especially shotgun houses on the narrowest lots, and any block where the alley was closed/built over) — car is parked on-street or in a side-yard gravel pull-off instead.
- **Driveway to the street (front-facing)**: rare and usually a *later retrofit*, ~10-15% of lots, appearing where the alley was lost/never existed; narrow single-strip concrete "ribbon" driveway (two poured strips with a grass median) rather than a full paved apron — a visible tell that it was added after original construction.
- **Driveway material**: gravel or dirt through the 1920s-30s; concrete "ribbon strip" alley aprons and rear pads becoming common by the 1930s-40s as household income allowed; asphalt rare on these short alley runs (more a postwar/subdivision material — see below).
- **Carport**: essentially absent on this lot type (a postwar invention); do not sample for pre-war archetypes.

## 4. Outbuildings

- **Detached garage**: covered above (70-85% where alley intact).
- **Storage shed** (small, 6x8 to 8x10 ft): 20-35% of lots, sited at the rear lot line beside or behind the garage, often a converted coal shed/outhouse footprint on the oldest lots (pre-1920 construction sometimes literally reused the privy pad).
- **Detached workshop**: rare on these lots (~5%), and where present it's a lean-to off the garage rather than a stand-alone building — pre-war in-town lots are too small for a separate workshop building; hobbyist/tinkerer space instead lives in a garage bay or basement.
- **Greenhouse**: rare (~2-5%), small lean-to/cold-frame type against the house's sunniest wall rather than a free-standing glasshouse — a Victorian-era or upper-income Foursquare feature specifically (see `upper_middle_income_lots.md`).

## 5. Gardens & landscaping

- **Vegetable garden**: present on ~40-55% of pre-war in-town lots historically (higher among households one or two generations off a farm, which describes most of this tier's early-20th-century occupants), occupying a rear-yard bed 100-300 sq ft, usually beside or behind the garage where sun allows. Prevalence has declined generationally but the *bed itself* (edged, sometimes with a low wire fence against rabbits) often persists as a landscape feature even when unused.
  - *Why*: kitchen-garden habit carried over from rural/immigrant backgrounds; WWII victory-garden campaigns specifically targeted urban/in-town households (12 of 18 million US victory gardens in 1943 were in cities) and left a durable habit and infrastructure (garden beds, canning culture) in this housing stock.
- **Foundation plantings**: near-universal (~85-95%) on Foursquare, Bungalow, Victorian, and Cape-Cod-adjacent starter homes — low shrubs (yew, boxwood, hydrangea) hugging the foundation, a landscaping norm that crystallized as a national convention by the 1920s specifically to visually soften the tall, exposed foundations these house types have.
- **Hedge**: 15-25%, more common on Victorian/Foursquare corner lots as a property-line/privacy marker; low or absent on shotgun lots (too narrow to spare the width).
- **Street trees**: not lot contents per se, but near-universal on these blocks (city-planted, mature) and should visually frame the lot.

## 6. Leisure/recreation

- **Swing set/playset**: 15-25% of family-occupied lots (yard is small; playsets here are simple swing-only sets, not big multi-module play structures).
- **In-ground pool**: essentially 0% — lots too narrow/shallow, no room after garage+garden.
- **Above-ground pool**: 3-8%, small (12-15 ft round), squeezed into whatever back-yard space is left; a later (1960s+) retrofit, never original to the lot.
- **Deck**: uncommon as a *original* feature (decks are a postwar/1970s+ convention); where present (~15%) it's a small retrofit off a rear door.
- **Patio**: more common than deck on this tier (~20-30%) — a poured-concrete slab off the back door, often original or early (concrete patios were affordable and match the era's material palette).
- **Firepit/fire ring**: 10-20%, informal (no built masonry firepit — a portable ring or DIY block circle) given limited yard space.
- **Basketball hoop**: 20-30%, almost always alley-mounted (above the garage door or on a pole at the alley edge) rather than driveway-mounted, since the alley functions as this lot type's "driveway/hoop court" surface.

## 7. Fencing tied to yard use

- **No fence at all**: common in the oldest, most established in-town blocks (~35-45%) — an "open yard" norm typical of early-20th-century streetcar-suburb neighborhoods where yards were semi-public/visually continuous.
- **Low picket or low ornamental fence, front yard**: 15-25%, decorative rather than functional, Victorian/Foursquare tier.
- **Chain-link, back yard only**: 25-35%, usually added later for a dog or to fence off the garden from rabbits/kids, not original.
- **Privacy fence around a pool/patio**: only where a pool exists (see above), essentially rare on this lot type.

## 8. Utility items

- **Trash/recycling bin storage**: alley-facing side of the garage or directly against the alley fence — these lots almost universally use the **alley for service access** (trash pickup from the alley, not the street), a direct legacy of pre-automobile urban planning. Bins are visually screened from the *street* almost automatically by the depth of the lot, even where no ordinance requires it.
- **Mailbox**: wall-mounted beside the front door or a small post box at the foot of the porch steps — urban/in-town convention (USPS urban delivery standard), never a post-mounted roadside box or cluster box (those are rural/suburban conventions — see `rural_farm_adjacent_lots.md` and `_lot_contents_index.md`).

## 9. Climate items

See `great_lakes_climate_items.md` for the full cross-cutting pool; the in-town-specific notes are: snow removal equipment (shovel, occasionally a walk-behind snowblower) stored in the garage or a small vestibule, since there's no room for a shed; clotheslines strung garage-to-house or on a alley-facing pole, moderately common on older/lower-income lots in this tier (~25-35%) and increasingly removed on renovated/higher-income examples; woodpiles rare (in-town lots don't have space and most in-town households were never wood-heated after the coal-to-gas conversion of the mid-20th century).

## 10. Building-to-lot relationship notes

- **`foursquare.md`**: Wide-enough lot (40-60 ft frontage) supports the richest version of this pattern — alley garage, foundation planting, modest garden, occasional low ornamental fence. Highest rate of the "converted carriage house" garage among all archetypes here.
- **`victorian_queen_anne.md`**: Largest, oldest lots in this file; most likely to have a genuine outbuilding (converted carriage house/stable, sometimes a small greenhouse) and the most elaborate foundation/ornamental planting — see also `upper_middle_income_lots.md` for the higher-income end of this archetype.
- **`bungalow_craftsman.md`**: Narrower lot than Foursquare/Victorian; strong foundation-planting convention (this is literally the archetype most associated with the "bungalow foundation garden" landscaping norm); alley garage common but smaller (1-car).
- **`shotgun.md`**: Narrowest lots in the catalog (25-35 ft) — least likely to have any garage or outbuilding at all; yard use is almost entirely a shallow front stoop zone and a sliver back yard just big enough for a clothesline and trash cans.
- **`rowhouse_townhouse.md`** and **`duplex.md` (older, stacked/side-by-side units)**: Zero-lot-line or near-zero side yards; garage (if any) is a shared alley structure or a single small pad; front "yard" is often just a stoop/areaway with no planting bed at all in the 1890-1920 urban rowhouse variant. The 1980s+ townhouse variant instead follows postwar-suburban conventions (attached garage, small private patio) — treat only the pre-1930 rowhouse stock as belonging to this file.
- **`starter_home_minimal_traditional.md`** (1935-1955): transitional — built late enough that some examples have an attached or very-close single-car garage instead of a rear alley garage, but still on a narrow in-town-scaled lot; treat as ~50/50 alley-garage vs. small attached-garage depending on the specific subdivision's plat date (pre-1945 vs. post-1945 within the range).
