# Great Lakes Climate Items (cross-cutting, all tiers)

## 1. Overview

The brief specifically calls out that this region has real winter (lake-effect snow, sub-freezing months, occasional severe cold) alongside a real severe-weather season (spring/summer tornado exposure, being on the southern edge of "Tornado Alley"-adjacent activity extended east into Ohio/Indiana/Illinois). This file collects the climate-driven item pool that cuts across every era/setting/income file above — apply these as an additional sampling layer on top of whichever era/setting/income file governs a given lot, rather than re-deriving climate items per-file.

## 2. Item pool with cross-tier probability notes

### Snow-removal equipment
- **Snow shovel(s) visible/stored near an entry**: near-universal (~90%+) across all tiers — the baseline, lowest-cost snow tool.
- **Walk-behind snowblower**: present on ~35-50% of in-town/suburban lots (postwar suburban skews toward the higher end given garage storage space — see `postwar_suburban_lots.md`), stored inside the garage almost universally where a garage exists, or just inside a shed/entry vestibule where it doesn't.
- **Tractor-mounted blade/blower or a dedicated snow-clearing ATV attachment**: rural-tier only (~15-25% of rural lots, see `rural_farm_adjacent_lots.md`), reflecting the longer gravel driveways of that tier.
- *Why*: this is pure functional necessity — the Great Lakes snow-belt (lake-effect bands off Erie/Michigan/Huron/Ontario/Superior) regularly produces sustained multi-inch-per-event snowfall, and clearing a driveway/walk is a basic seasonal chore infrastructure need, not a discretionary purchase, hence its near-universal presence at some tier of equipment across all income levels.

### Salt / sand storage
- **Bag(s) of ice-melt salt or a sand bucket visible near the entry/garage in winter months**: ~40-55% of lots across all tiers, most visible in the garage or on a porch/entry landing.
- *Why*: municipal sidewalk-clearing ordinances in many Midwest towns require homeowners to clear and de-ice their own public sidewalk frontage within a set window after snowfall, making salt/sand storage a practical necessity independent of income.

### Storm windows / screens (seasonal swap)
- Combination (triple-track) storm/screen windows are common on pre-1945 and 1945-1965 construction specifically (~50-65% of that era range, declining sharply on 1970s+ construction which increasingly shipped with factory-sealed double-pane windows) — see the era-specific notes in `prewar_intown_lots.md` and `postwar_suburban_lots.md`.
- Where present as **separate** (non-combination) storm sashes rather than integrated triple-track units, expect them stacked/stored in the garage or basement during the off season (visible generator detail: a stack of glass-and-wood-frame sashes leaning against a garage wall in summer) — a genuinely period-correct Midwest detail for older housing stock.
- *Why*: storm windows/combination windows were the standard low-cost insulation upgrade for older single-pane housing stock before modern replacement windows became affordable; the seasonal swap ritual (screens off/storms on in fall, reverse in spring) is a well-documented regional household task specifically associated with older Midwest housing.

### Clotheslines
- Prevalence by tier (use these instead of a single flat rate): rural ~30-40%, low-income (any setting) ~35-45%, pre-war in-town ~25-35%, postwar suburban middle-income ~10-15%, upper-middle-income ~3-8%.
- *Why*: the clearest income/era-legible item in this whole category. Electric dryers became affordable and were aggressively marketed as a modern-convenience status symbol starting in the 1950s ("Live Better Electrically" campaigns); the clothesline survived strongest where either (a) the household economically benefits from avoiding dryer operating cost (rural, low-income), or (b) the housing stock predates or wasn't renovated away from the older habit (pre-war in-town). Note also that "right to dry" laws exist in several states (including Illinois, Indiana, and Wisconsin within this game's research region) specifically because many wealthier HOA-governed subdivisions banned clotheslines as unsightly/poverty-coded — a real regional legal detail worth reflecting in generator flavor text even where the item itself is absent.

### Woodpiles
- Prevalence by tier: rural ~35-50%, pre-war in-town ~5-10% (rare — most in-town households converted to gas/oil/electric heat decades ago), postwar suburban ~10-15% (supplemental/ambiance fireplace wood, not primary heat), farm-adjacent/rural-village ~30-40%.
- *Why*: wood heat (primary or supplemental) is disproportionately a rural practice nationally (rural households are the group most likely to use wood as a primary heat source, and it has the biggest positive household-budget impact in rural areas specifically) — reflecting both source-material access (own woodlot, windbreak trimmings, farm-adjacent timber) and a genuine cost-saving motivation absent in gas-heated in-town/suburban housing stock. Where present, stack near the house (covered/tarped) or near a wood-stove-equipped outbuilding; a full-season Midwest supply is roughly 2-3 cords per 1,000 sq ft of heated space for a primary-heat household, much less (a small decorative rick) for a supplemental/fireplace-only household.

### Basement/storm-prep culture
- Basement presence itself is not a "lot content" item but strongly conditions this pool: expect storm-prep visibility (a flashlight/radio near the basement stairs, occasionally a marked "storm shelter" corner) mainly on pre-1990s construction — new Midwest single-family construction has seen a measurable decline in basements built (roughly a 10% drop over 2013-2022 across the Midwest) even as tornado frequency in states like Ohio has risen sharply, a real and citable regional tension worth reflecting if the generator ever needs to vary basement presence by construction-era probability.
- **Root cellar / true storm cellar (a distinct small outbuilding or below-grade door, not just "has a basement")**: rural/farm-adjacent tier only, ~10-15% of older farmhouses — a genuine period structure with roots in produce-preservation before refrigeration, later dual-purposed as tornado shelter; treat as a rare, era-flavored item (pre-1960s farmhouse) rather than a common one.

## 3. Building-to-lot relationship notes

- All fourteen single-family archetypes in the building catalog are eligible for this file's items; the primary variable is **era** (pre-1970 construction gets the storm-window and higher woodpile/wood-stove-adjacent probabilities; post-1970 construction trends toward factory-sealed windows and gas/electric-only heat) crossed with **setting** (rural gets the tractor-blade/woodpile/root-cellar end of the pool; in-town/suburban gets the walk-behind-snowblower/garage-stored-storm-sash end).
- `farmhouse_vernacular.md` and `mobile_home.md` (via `rural_farm_adjacent_lots.md`) sit at the high-probability end of nearly every item in this file; `ranch.md`, `split_level.md`, `colonial_revival.md`, and other postwar/upper-tier archetypes sit at the low-probability end for woodpile/clothesline specifically while still carrying normal snow-removal-equipment rates.
