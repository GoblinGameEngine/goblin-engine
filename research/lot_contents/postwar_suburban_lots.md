# Postwar Suburban Lots (c. 1945-1990 construction, curvilinear-subdivision lots)

## 1. Overview

This file covers the lot pattern for houses built in postwar subdivisions: Ranch, Split-Level/Split-Foyer, Cape Cod (later/suburban-built examples), Colonial Revival, Tudor Revival Cottage (postwar-built examples), and the newer half of Duplex/Rowhouse-Townhouse stock (1980s+ townhouses). These lots were platted on curving subdivision streets **with no rear alley** — the single structural fact that flips the garage/driveway pattern relative to `prewar_intown_lots.md`. Frontage is wider and lots are shallower/more consistent than the older grid, and the **driveway apron facing the street becomes the dominant front-yard feature**, replacing the ornamental lawn panel of the pre-war lot.

Applies across income tiers within the "middle" range; the top and bottom of the income spectrum in postwar construction are broken out separately in `upper_middle_income_lots.md` and `low_income_lots.md`.

**Population tiers**: small town and small city, in the postwar-subdivision-style zones (curving streets, larger lots than the older grid) noted in the building catalog's ranch/split-level entries.

## 2. Lot layout baseline

- Frontage: 60-100 ft (18-30m), no rear alley — the block interior is private yard, not shared service space.
- Depth: 100-150 ft (30-46m).
- Front yard: dominated by the driveway apron and attached garage door — the single biggest visual difference from the pre-war lot, where the garage was invisible from the street.
- Side yard: wider than pre-war (10-15+ ft), sometimes enough for a side-yard gate to the back yard or a second parking pad.
- Back yard: private, fenced or open, the primary "use" yard — patio/deck, pool (income-dependent), swing set, garden.

## 3. Garage / driveway typology — THE defining item pool

- **Attached garage, 1-2 car**: present on ~80-90% of postwar ranch/split-level/Cape-Cod-suburban lots; garage door and the driveway apron leading to it are the dominant front-facade element — a hallmark the building catalog's `ranch.md` file already notes ("garage is directly adjacent to the kitchen or a mudroom/utility entry").
  - *Why*: mass car ownership was now the norm at time of construction, and subdivision plats had no rear alley to hide the garage on — architects and builders instead integrated the garage into the front elevation for direct street access, trading the old "garage as embarrassing utility shed" attitude for "garage as visible modern convenience."
- **Carport (open-sided, roofed, no garage door)**: 5-10% of the lower end of this tier, mostly 1950s-60s ranch/starter-adjacent construction in warmer-budget builds — cheaper than a full garage, still shelters the car from Great Lakes snow/ice.
- **Driveway apron**: near-universal (~95%+) where a garage or carport exists; typically a single or double-wide concrete slab, 16-24 ft (5-7m) wide at the garage, widening or staying constant to the street.
- **Driveway material by era/income**: concrete dominant for the front apron on middle-income and up construction from the 1950s onward (associated with a "finished," higher-status look); asphalt common as a cheaper alternative especially in colder-climate subdivisions of the 1960s-80s (asphalt is more common regionally in the snow-belt Midwest/Northeast than in warmer states) and on lower-income/rental postwar stock; gravel driveways are rare in *platted* postwar subdivisions (associated instead with rural/farm-adjacent lots — see `rural_farm_adjacent_lots.md`) except as a stopgap on the cheapest/oldest mobile-home-adjacent postwar lots.
- **Basketball hoop over/beside the garage door, or a portable pole at the driveway apron's edge**: 35-50% of postwar lots with children — this is the archetypal postwar-suburban basketball setup precisely because the driveway itself is now a paved, street-facing court that didn't exist in the alley-garage era.

## 4. Outbuildings

- **Detached garage**: much rarer here than pre-war (~10-15%) since the house was built with an attached garage; where a detached structure exists it's usually a *second*, smaller structure (workshop or shed) rather than the primary garage.
- **Storage shed**: 40-55% of postwar lots — much more common than the pre-war tier, because postwar lots are bigger and the attached garage is already full of the car + lawn equipment, pushing overflow (mower, garden tools, bikes) into a rear-yard shed. Typical size 8x10 to 10x12 ft, sited at or near the rear lot line, often a prefab metal or vinyl kit shed (Sears/mail-order shed kits and later big-box kit sheds are a genuinely period-accurate detail for this tier from the 1950s onward).
- **Detached workshop**: 10-15%, larger hobbyist/tinkerer households, usually a converted/expanded shed rather than a purpose-built workshop, more common on upper-middle-income examples of this tier (see `upper_middle_income_lots.md`).
- **Greenhouse**: 3-8%, small hobby greenhouse or hoop-style, a discretionary/hobby item correlated with income and gardening enthusiasm rather than era per se.

## 5. Gardens & landscaping

- **Vegetable garden**: present on ~25-40% of postwar suburban lots — lower than the pre-war/rural tiers; suburban culture shifted emphasis toward ornamental lawn over kitchen garden as supermarket produce became reliably cheap and available from the 1950s onward, though the habit never disappeared and rural-adjacent examples (subdivision at a small town's edge) skew higher.
- **Foundation plantings**: near-universal (~85-90%) — ranch houses in particular are strongly associated with foundation shrub beds to soften the long, low, exposed foundation line; informal, gently curving beds and looser/naturalistic plant forms are the period-correct look for ranch/split-level rather than the more formal Victorian-era hedge-and-symmetry approach.
- **Hedge (property-line)**: 20-30%, more common as a rear/side privacy screen than a front boundary marker (open-lawn front yards without hedges are the suburban norm).
- **Lawn as primary landscape feature**: near-universal — the large, uniformly mowed front lawn (no fence, no hedge at the front) is itself the dominant "item" of the postwar suburban lot and should be the generator's default front-yard ground cover for this tier.

## 6. Leisure/recreation

- **Swing set/playset**: 30-45% of family-occupied lots — bigger back yards support larger multi-module wood or metal playsets than the pre-war tier's simple swing frames.
- **In-ground pool**: ~5-8% of middle-income postwar lots nationally (national average across all housing is ~8% of households; in-ground vs above-ground splits roughly 59%/41% of all residential pools) — climbs toward `upper_middle_income_lots.md` rates for the top of this tier, near-zero at the bottom.
- **Above-ground pool**: ~8-12% of middle/lower-middle postwar lots — the accessible, budget-friendly pool option ($1,000-6,000 vs. ~$66,000 average for in-ground), making it the far more common pool type below the upper-middle-income line.
  - *Why the income split matters*: pool type is one of the strongest income-legible yard signals available to the generator — in-ground pool reads as upper-middle/high income, above-ground pool reads as middle/lower-middle income with a taste for the amenity but not the budget, and no pool at all is the default for the low-income tier.
- **Pool fencing**: wherever a pool (either type) is present, a 4-ft-minimum self-closing/self-latching fence or barrier around it is standard/code-required in the large majority of US jurisdictions — sample fenced-pool at ~90%+ whenever a pool exists, higher than the general "privacy fence" rate for the tier.
- **Deck**: 35-50% of postwar lots — the deck (attached to the rear of the house, off the kitchen/family room slider) is a genuinely period-correct postwar/1960s-80s feature the pre-war tier mostly lacks; wood deck attached at the sliding glass door the `ranch.md` catalog entry already calls out.
- **Patio**: 25-35%, poured concrete, often paired with or instead of a deck depending on budget/era (earlier postwar construction favors patio, 1970s+ favors deck).
- **Firepit/fire ring**: 15-25%, more often a purpose-built masonry or paver firepit on this tier's bigger back yards than the informal ring typical of the pre-war tier.
- **Basketball hoop**: 35-50% (see driveway section above).

## 7. Fencing tied to yard use

- **No fence, open front yard**: dominant norm (~60-70%) — the unbroken, hedge-and-fence-free front lawn is a hallmark of postwar suburban planning aesthetics (visually continuous yards across a block).
- **Privacy fence, back yard only**: 30-45%, concentrated around a pool, deck, or patio specifically (fence-the-use-zone, not the whole lot) — wood privacy fencing or vinyl privacy fencing depending on era/income.
- **Chain-link, back yard**: 15-25%, dog-yard use, more common at the lower-income end of this tier (see `low_income_lots.md`).

## 8. Utility items

- **Trash/recycling bin storage**: side yard beside the garage, or inside the garage itself, since there is no alley — municipal ordinances in this tier commonly require bins be stored out of street view except within ~24 hours of collection day (a real, citable ordinance pattern, e.g. Bloomington, IN's 24-hour visibility rule), making a side-yard garage-adjacent storage spot the functional default.
- **Mailbox**: post-mounted single box at the curb/driveway edge — the classic suburban curbside mailbox on a wood or metal post, USPS standard height 41-45 in, set back 6-8 in from the curb; distinct from both the in-town wall-mount (see `prewar_intown_lots.md`) and the rural cluster box (see `rural_farm_adjacent_lots.md`).

## 9. Climate items

See `great_lakes_climate_items.md` for the full pool. Postwar-suburban-specific notes: snowblower stored in the attached garage (far and away the dominant storage location for this tier, since the garage already exists and is climate-relevant infrastructure); storm windows/combination windows common on 1945-1965 construction specifically (triple-track combination storm/screen units, invented 1930s, became standard through the mid-century before being superseded by factory double-pane windows on 1970s+ construction — so sample storm windows at high probability for early-postwar builds, declining toward 1980s-90s builds); clotheslines much less common than pre-war tier (~10-15%), reflecting the postwar cultural shift toward the electric dryer as a status marker (1950s "Live Better Electrically" campaigns specifically targeted this housing stock).

## 10. Building-to-lot relationship notes

- **`ranch.md`**: The archetype most closely identified with this entire lot pattern — attached garage as a defining facade feature, foundation planting, rear patio/deck off the sliding glass door, driveway-mounted basketball hoop. Treat ranch-house lots as this file's default/baseline case.
- **`split_level.md`**: Same driveway/garage pattern as ranch but the garage is frequently tucked under the raised half-level (a distinctive "garage under the bedrooms" massing), still street-facing and attached.
- **`cape_cod.md`** (later/suburban-built examples, as opposed to any earlier in-town examples): narrower lot than ranch but still no alley; attached or very-close single-car garage, smaller driveway apron, modest foundation planting matching the smaller footprint.
- **`colonial_revival.md`** and **`tudor_revival_cottage.md`** (postwar-built examples): skew toward the upper end of this tier's income range — see `upper_middle_income_lots.md` for the fuller amenity set (three-car garage, in-ground pool) that appears at the top of these archetypes' lot profile.
- **`duplex.md`** and **`rowhouse_townhouse.md`** (1980s+ built): follow this postwar pattern in miniature — small attached garage per unit, small private patio, no shared alley, unlike their pre-1930 counterparts in `prewar_intown_lots.md`.
