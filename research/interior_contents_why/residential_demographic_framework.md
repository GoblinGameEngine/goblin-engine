# Residential Demographic Weighting Framework

Companion layer to `research/building_catalog/residential/`. That catalog defines, per archetype, a room list and a per-room content **pool** (item + count range). This file does NOT replace those pools — it defines a small set of **household roll dimensions**, each with **modifier rules** that bias which items from the existing pool get sampled, at what count, and in what condition, plus a short list of **new diagnostic items** worth adding to specific room pools. See `_synthesis.md` for the generator's overall apply-order.

## How to read this file

Each dimension below is a **rollable household attribute**. For a given generated house/apartment instance, the generator should:
1. Pick the archetype (as today, via `building_catalog/residential/`).
2. Roll **income tier**, **household structure**, and effective **construction era** (era is often fixed by the archetype itself, per the catalog's Overview section — see "Era interacts with archetype, not just income" below).
3. Apply each dimension's modifiers as **weight adjustments** to the base pool: nudging counts up/down within (or slightly beyond) the catalog's stated ranges, toggling "optional" (0-1 / 0-2) items on or off, and picking a condition/quality descriptor for rendering.
4. Where two dimensions modify the same item, treat effects as **additive/multiplicative on weight, not overriding** — e.g. a low-income + pre-1945 household doesn't "cancel out" to look middle-income; both effects push the same direction (older, sparser) and compound.

Population-tier terms (Rural/Small town/Small city) are as defined in the residential catalog index.

## Dimension 1: Income Tier

Three tiers: **Low**, **Middle**, **Upper-middle**. (No "wealthy/elite" tier — the tallest archetype in the catalog, Victorian/Queen Anne, already represents the local ceiling; treat its baseline as roughly upper-middle-to-affluent by construction, so income-tier rolls mostly matter for how *well-kept* and *how filled-in* that house is today, generations after its original builder.)

Framing: income tier is not just "more stuff," it is (a) which optional/discretionary items from the pool are present at all, (b) how many units of a countable item are present within the pool's stated range, (c) a quality/condition descriptor applied to rendering, and (d) a clutter/maintenance signal that is often a *more visible* income proxy than the furniture itself.

| Effect | Low income | Middle income | Upper-middle income | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Optional/discretionary items (home office desk, extra TVs, hobby/game equipment, higher-end small appliances) | Mostly absent; roll toward the pool's stated 0 end | Present at ~50% roll rate | Present at ~80-90% roll rate, often exceeding the pool's stated max by +1 | Discretionary spending is the first thing income constrains; BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey and EIA Residential Energy Consumption Survey (RECS) data show durable-good ownership scaling directly with income — e.g. dishwasher ownership rose from 18% of households in the lowest RECS income bracket to 83% in the highest bracket (EIA, 2001 RECS). |
| Countable item quantity (nightstands, small appliances, seating pieces, bookshelf count) | Roll toward the pool's stated **minimum** | Roll toward the pool's **midpoint** | Roll toward the pool's stated **maximum**, or +1 beyond it for "filled-in" archetypes (Victorian, Foursquare, Colonial Revival) | More disposable income converts directly into more discrete furniture/appliance units per room — corroborated by BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey furniture-spending-by-income breakdowns (higher-income households consistently report both higher furniture spend and larger homes to fill). |
| Item quality/condition descriptor (for rendering: material, finish, wear) | "Worn/mismatched": secondhand-looking upholstery, visible wear on high-touch surfaces (armrests, stair treads, cabinet pulls), mismatched furniture sets (pieces from different decades/sets), a few items obviously repaired (taped vinyl seat, patched screen door) | "Serviceable/mixed": mostly matching but modest-grade furniture, moderate wear, one or two "nicer" splurge items (a new recliner, a flat-screen TV) amid older pieces | "New/coordinated": matched sets, minimal visible wear, updated finishes (granite/quartz counters, stainless appliances in a kitchen retrofit), fewer visible repairs | Furniture and finish condition is one of the most legible, renderable proxies for household income in real interiors — it doesn't require changing the item *list*, only its material/wear state, which is cheap for a generator to vary. |
| Clutter/maintenance-level signal | Higher clutter in "overflow" surfaces (stacked mail/bills on kitchen counter, laundry baskets in living areas, deferred exterior maintenance — peeling paint, tarped roof section, a non-running car in the yard/driveway) | Moderate, "lived-in" clutter (some toys/shoes by the door, dishes in sink) but no deferred structural maintenance visible | Low clutter, "staged" surfaces, visible discretionary yard/exterior spend (landscaping, fresh paint, newer roof) | Clutter is partly a time-poverty signal (lower-income households more often work multiple jobs or shift work, less discretionary time for tidying/organizing) and partly a deferred-maintenance signal (repairs cost money and get postponed) — both are well-documented material-culture correlates of income independent of the underlying item list, and are cheap "set dressing" for the generator to vary without touching the pool itself. |
| Kitchen appliance tier | Older/base-model stove, no dishwasher (or portable/older unit), smaller single-door fridge, microwave present but basic/countertop | Standard fridge/range/dishwasher combo, mid-size microwave (built-in over range possible in 1990s+ construction) | Larger fridge (possibly side-by-side/French-door in 2000s+ builds), dishwasher standard, additional small appliances (stand mixer, air fryer 2015+, wine fridge as an upper-tier-only add) | Kitchen appliance ownership is one of the best-documented income gradients in US consumer data (EIA RECS appliance-by-income tables); it's also one of the easiest "silent" ways for the generator to signal income without adding exotic props. |
| Vehicle/garage content (where archetype has a garage) | Older/single vehicle, garage frequently used for storage overflow rather than car | 1-2 vehicles, garage used for both car + modest storage/workbench | 2+ vehicles (including a second "toy" — boat, ATV trailer, camper implied in exterior lot generation), garage more organized, higher-end tool/workbench setup | Vehicle count and condition is a classic Consumer Expenditure Survey income correlate and a strong exterior-visible income signal for a procedurally generated neighborhood. |

**Income tier by population tier (weighting note):** rural village households should skew low/middle (subsistence-farm-adjacent and fixed-income-retiree populations are common); small city households should have the fullest income spread including upper-middle; small town is the broadest mixed-income middle ground. This lets the generator vary income tier's base rate by settlement tier rather than rolling it uniformly everywhere.

## Dimension 2: Household Structure

Five household types: **Single person**, **Couple (no kids)**, **Family with kids**, **Retiree/empty-nester**, **Multigenerational/roommates**. This dimension mostly changes room *use* and which pool items get selected for a given room, more than it changes overall wealth signals (that's Dimension 1's job).

| Household type | Room-use effects | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Single person** | One bedroom fully furnished as primary; any additional bedrooms in the archetype's room list are repurposed — roll toward home office, guest room (sparse, "unused" descriptor: bed present but minimal personal items), or storage/junk room (boxes, unused exercise equipment). Kitchen: fewer place settings implied, smaller-format items (compact appliances) more likely even independent of income. Living areas: single-person seating bias (1 sofa + 1 armchair rather than 2 armchairs). | A single occupant doesn't need — and typically doesn't furnish — every bedroom an archetype's floor plan provides; extra bedrooms in real single-occupant households are the single most common source of "office," "craft room," or "junk room" conversions documented in real-estate/home-organization literature. |
| **Couple, no kids** | Similar to single but doubled personal-item counts in the primary bedroom (2 nightstands standard, not optional). Extra bedroom(s) roll toward home office/guest room/hobby room, same logic as single. Higher rate of "nicer" discretionary living-room items (the pool's 1-2 armchair range rolls toward 2) since two incomes are more often pooled here than in single-person households. | Two-adult, no-dependent households historically show the highest discretionary spending power per capita (DINK — "dual income, no kids" — is a recognized consumer-research household segment), which shows up as denser/more filled-in shared living spaces even without a formal income-tier bump. |
| **Family with kids** | Secondary bedrooms are actively furnished as **kids' bedrooms**, not generic guest rooms: bunk beds or twin beds (vs. the pool's default full/queen secondary-bedroom bed), toy storage/toy bins (new item, see below), study desk higher probability, more saturated wall-decor/posters, higher likelihood of a second TV/game system in an older kid's room (see new-items section). Kitchen: kid-height step stool, more visible pantry/snack stock. Living/family room: toy clutter overflow, child safety items in homes with younger children (outlet covers, baby gate — set-dressing only, low render priority). Garage/yard: bikes, sports equipment. | Room use is driven by the literal presence of dependents needing separate, age-appropriate sleeping/study space; kids'-room furniture and toy density is one of the most reliable "who lives here" visual signals in real interiors and differs categorically (not just in wear) from an adult guest room. |
| **Retiree/empty-nester** | At least one former "kid's bedroom" is repurposed into a **craft room, home office, sewing room, or den/hobby room** — roll this at high probability (60-80%) for any archetype with 3+ bedrooms. Primary bedroom furniture skews older/more traditional (matches Dimension 3's era effects, since retirees are more likely to have owned/furnished the house decades earlier and not fully refreshed it). Higher probability of single-level-living adaptations in multi-story archetypes (little-used upper floor, more items concentrated on the main floor) and of mobility-aid set-dressing (raised toilet seat, grab bars in bathroom, stairlift in 2-story homes at low probability) for the oldest cohort. Kitchen: smaller-batch cooking signals (fewer place settings actively in use, compact/older appliances kept well past their era-typical replacement window since the household isn't shopping for new construction-era upgrades). | Empty-nesters are, definitionally, households whose children's bedrooms outlived their original purpose — repurposing that space into a hobby/craft/office room rather than leaving it as a static shrine is the dominant real pattern (supported broadly in aging-in-place and home-organization literature); older cohorts also renovate/replace furnishings less frequently than younger households, so era-appropriate-but-dated furnishings persist here even at a healthy income tier. |
| **Multigenerational / roommates** | Requires MORE bedrooms furnished as **primary-grade** (adult-occupant, full furniture set: bed, dresser, nightstand, closet contents) rather than one primary + secondary/guest — apply this by upgrading 1-2 of the archetype's "secondary bedroom" pool entries to primary-bedroom-tier item sets. Higher probability of a repurposed common space (finished basement, dining room, or den converted to an additional bedroom, especially in starter homes/bungalows/ranches with only 2-3 bedrooms) — flag this as a "converted room" variant using the target room's furniture pool instead of the room type implied by the archetype's floor plan. Kitchen: larger-format pantry/bulk goods (ties into Dimension 1's chest-freezer new-item, below), more place settings, possible duplicate small appliances (two coffee makers, a mini-fridge in a converted bedroom for a semi-independent adult-child or grandparent unit). Bathroom: higher fixture-use wear regardless of income tier (more daily users). | Multigenerational households (record ~64 million Americans / ~20% of the US population per Pew Research analysis of Census data, up sharply since the 2007-2009 recession) form primarily for economic-necessity and caregiving reasons — adult children unable to afford independent housing, or aging parents moving in for care — both of which mean the household needs more *equal-status* bedrooms, not one primary bedroom and several child-scaled ones; the record growth trend also makes this a demographically meaningful, non-rare roll for the generator rather than an edge case. |

**Household structure by archetype (weighting note):** bias larger archetypes (Victorian, Foursquare, Colonial Revival, Split-Level, larger farmhouses) toward family-with-kids or multigenerational rolls; bias smaller archetypes (shotgun, starter home, small apartment units, mobile home single-wide) toward single/couple rolls, since real households self-sort into appropriately-sized housing — though income constraints (Dimension 1, low tier) should allow "family in a too-small house" as a plausible, and realistic, mismatch roll.

## Dimension 3: Construction Era

Three eras: **Pre-1945**, **1945-1980**, **1980-present**. Note this is normally **archetype-determined** (each `building_catalog/residential/*.md` file states its own era range in the Overview section) rather than independently rolled — but two situations make it worth modeling explicitly: (1) a pre-1945 house that has been partially modernized (renovated kitchen/bath) should roll a *later* "effective era" for just those rooms, and (2) this dimension's effects are the correct place to hang technology/norm changes that are independent of income, so income-tier modifiers (Dimension 1) apply *on top of* era's baseline rather than needing to re-derive it.

| Effect | Pre-1945 | 1945-1980 | 1980-present | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Closets** | Minimal or no built-in bedroom closets; wardrobes/armoires/chests substitute (already correctly modeled as a period-detail item in Victorian/Foursquare pools — extend it as the DEFAULT for all pre-1945 bedrooms, not just an optional detail) | Small built-in closets become standard (single sliding or hinged door, modest depth) | Larger built-in closets standard; primary-bedroom **walk-in closet** becomes common from the 1980s-90s onward, near-universal in 2000s+ construction | Built-in bedroom closets were not standard in American house construction until well into the 20th century; garment-hanging storage itself only became the norm after clothes hangers came into common household use following the Civil War, and even into the early 20th century clothing was more often folded into chests/dressers or hung in freestanding wardrobes than closeted — pre-1945 stock genuinely lacks the built-in storage that later eras take for granted, which is why real pre-1945 houses today are so often retrofitted with an added wardrobe or a shallow closet carved from an adjacent room. |
| **Cooling** | No central AC; window unit(s) in 1-2 rooms only if retrofitted (roll 20-30% for a single window unit in the primary bedroom or living room, higher in hotter-climate variants), otherwise ceiling fans/attic fans/open-window strategy implied | Central AC increasingly common by the 1960s-70s (roll ~50-60% present for houses built toward the end of this range), window units still common in the earlier part of the range or in lower-income retrofits | Central AC standard/near-universal (roll 90%+ present) | Central air conditioning did not reach mass-market US residential penetration until the postwar decades and became a standard builder-installed feature mainly from the 1960s-70s onward; pre-1945 housing stock predates it entirely and typically retrofits window units at best — this is a technology-adoption-timing effect independent of the occupant's current income. |
| **Kitchen appliances** | Icebox (very early substyle, pre-1930s) transitioning to an early electric refrigerator; gas or early electric range; no dishwasher; no built-in microwave; small, single-basin sink; minimal built-in cabinetry (freestanding "Hoosier cabinet"-style pantry units plausible as a period-correct furniture item, not built-in millwork) | Full-size refrigerator standard (refrigerators crossed 50% household penetration in 1942 and were near-universal within this era); electric range increasingly standard; dishwasher present in a minority of homes, skewing to the income-tier's upper end; built-in cabinetry standard by mid-era; microwave absent until the tail end of this era (microwaves only began entering mainstream home kitchens from the late 1970s) | Dishwasher standard (baseline expectation, still income-tier-modulated per Dimension 1); microwave standard/near-universal from this era onward; garbage disposal common; larger refrigerator formats (side-by-side, French-door) increasingly common in the later part of this range | Kitchen appliance ownership is one of the best-documented technology-adoption timelines in US consumer-economics literature (EIA RECS, historical Census appliance surveys) — refrigerator ownership alone moved from near-zero to majority penetration between roughly 1930 and 1942, and the dishwasher/microwave S-curves lag another one and two generations behind that, respectively; using era as the primary gate (with income as a secondary modifier on top) keeps the generator from putting a 2020s-standard kitchen in a 1920s-vintage, unmodified house. |
| **Formal dining room usage** | High — pre-1945 archetypes (Foursquare, Victorian, Colonial Revival, farmhouse) were built with a distinct, walled dining room as a core social/status room; roll it as actively furnished and used (dining table set, not just present) at high probability | Still present but usage softening — postwar ranch/split-level plans often present dining as an open "L" off the living room or kitchen rather than a fully walled room (already noted in the Ranch/Split-Level catalog files); roll it as present but less "special-occasion-only" formal in character | Declining as a dedicated, walled room in new construction (open-concept kitchen/living/dining favored from the 1990s-2000s onward), though a modest reversal/reappraisal of formal dining space has been noted in interior-design trend coverage since the COVID-19 pandemic (valuing separable/private rooms again) — roll a small chance (~15-20%) of a newer archetype instance still including a distinct formal dining room, biased upward for upper-middle-income rolls | The walled formal dining room was a defining status/hospitality room of pre-1945 middle-class American housing; the postwar shift toward open, informal family living (and smaller/less-staffed households no longer hosting formal multi-course dinners as routine social practice) pushed it toward an open "dining area" by the 1960s-80s, and late-20th-century open-concept construction norms all but eliminated it as a walled room in new building — a textbook case of a room's decline tracking changing domestic social practice rather than square-footage economics alone. |
| **Home office presence** | Absent as a dedicated room (a period-appropriate substitute is a "den/library" — already present in several pool files — used for correspondence/bookkeeping, not "work from home" in the modern sense) | Absent/rare; a spare bedroom or basement corner might hold a desk, but "home office" as a named, furnished room is not yet a mainstream expectation | Rising from ~2000 onward as broadband/laptop culture made home-based knowledge work more common, then **accelerating sharply starting 2020** — pre-pandemic, well under 5% of the US workforce worked from home 3+ days/week, versus a pandemic peak above 60% working from home at least weekly and a post-2022 plateau of roughly 35-40% doing so at least weekly; roll a dedicated/converted home-office room at a meaningfully higher rate for any 1980+ construction-era household, and especially for any household instance flagged as generated "in or after" 2020 in the game's internal timeline, modulated upward by income tier (remote/hybrid work is concentrated in higher-income, higher-education occupations) | This is the cleanest example in the whole framework of a technology-adoption-timing effect that is NOT explained by income or household structure alone — the room's very existence as a common household feature is bounded by broadband availability, laptop ubiquity, and (for the post-2020 acceleration) a specific historical shock, independent of what era the house itself was built in (an office can be retrofitted into any era's spare bedroom). |

**Era vs. archetype note:** because era is normally set by the archetype (per the catalog's Overview line), the generator should treat this dimension's table as "what the baseline furnishing/technology set looks like for a house of this vintage that has NOT been substantially remodeled." A renovation-roll (see `residential_why_notes.md`) can then upgrade specific rooms (most commonly kitchen and bathroom) to a later era's effects independent of the shell's original construction date — this is exactly what happens in real old-house stock and is why a pre-1945 Foursquare with a granite-counter kitchen remodel is a common, not contradictory, real-world instance.

## New diagnostic items (not yet in the existing per-room pools)

These are worth adding to specific archetype/room pools because they carry strong, cheap-to-render demographic signal. Each is scoped to the room/pool it should be added to and the roll conditions that should gate it.

| Item | Add to | Roll conditions | Why it's diagnostic |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Chest freezer** | Basement (finished/unfinished), garage, or a dedicated utility/mudroom in farmhouse, ranch, split-level, mobile home archetypes | High probability (40-60%) for rural-tier + low/middle income + any household structure; lower (10-20%) baseline elsewhere | About half of US households own a standalone chest or upright freezer separate from the kitchen fridge-freezer; ownership is strongest in rural/agricultural communities that source meat/produce in bulk (farm-direct, hunting, CSA, wholesale-club shopping) and freeze for long-term storage — a chest freezer is one of the single best rural-vs-urban, and secondarily income-tier (bulk buying is a documented lower/middle-income cost-saving strategy), material-culture markers available to the generator. |
| **Home gym equipment** (treadmill, weight bench, stationary bike, or a repurposed room fully dedicated to this) | Basement, garage, or a repurposed spare bedroom, in any archetype with 3+ bedrooms or a basement | Low probability (5-10%) at low/middle income; notably higher (25-35%) at upper-middle income, further boosted for any household instance generated "post-2020" per the game's internal timeline | Home fitness equipment ownership shows one of the clearest income gradients in recent US consumer data (roughly two-thirds of $100k+ income households report owning home exercise equipment, versus much lower rates further down the income scale) and saw a lasting step-change in adoption after the 2020 gym-closure period that only partially receded afterward — a strong, well-documented upper-income/post-2020 signal. |
| **Religious/cultural iconography** (a cross or crucifix, a saint's image, a Star of David, a small home altar/shrine corner, a framed religious text passage) | Living room, entry hall, or bedroom wall-decor slot, across most residential archetypes | Note PREVALENCE, not specific denomination/tradition — roll present at a moderate baseline rate (roughly a third to half of instances, higher for older/retiree households and rural-tier settlements, consistent with the Midwest's historically high rates of religious affiliation and the region's real Catholic/Lutheran/mainline-Protestant denominational mix already modeled in `church.md`) rather than the generator trying to model specific theology | Regionally common, low-cost, high-signal wall/shelf decor that reinforces the "Midwestern small-town" setting; keeping the roll at the level of "present/absent + generic visual category" rather than prescribing exact iconography avoids over-specifying a sensitive cultural detail while still giving the generator a real, well-grounded texture option. |
| **Multiple TVs / game systems in a kid's bedroom** | Secondary/kids' bedroom pool, in family-with-kids households, 1980+ era | Absent pre-1980 (no home game consoles/affordable second TVs yet); low-probability 1980s-90s; moderate-to-high probability (30-50%) 2000s+, scaling up with income tier | A second television and/or game console in a child's bedroom (rather than one shared household TV in the living room) is a specifically post-1980s phenomenon tracking both consumer-electronics price declines and rising per-capita device ownership, and its presence/absence is a legible combined era + income signal the generator can use without inventing new mechanics. |
| **Toy storage / toy bins** | Kids' bedroom and/or family/living room, family-with-kids households | Present whenever a bedroom is flagged as a kid's room (see Dimension 2) | Straightforward room-use-correct furnishing already implied by Dimension 2's kids'-bedroom logic but not explicitly named as an item in the base catalog pools — worth adding directly so the generator doesn't default a kid's room to adult-bedroom-style nightstand/dresser-only furnishing. |
| **Plastic furniture slipcovers / "good room" preservation signal** | Living room or formal parlor, older/retiree households, pre-1980 era, low/middle income | Low-moderate probability (10-20%), concentrated in retiree + pre-1980-furniture-vintage households regardless of current income tier | A well-documented mid-20th-century American (and especially Midwestern/immigrant-household) practice of covering "good" upholstered furniture in plastic to preserve it for guests/special occasions — a distinctive, era- and generation-linked material-culture detail that signals both the furniture's age/value-to-the-household and a frugality-driven maintenance mindset, independent of whether the household is currently poor or simply keeps inherited habits. |
| **Gun cabinet / gun safe** | Living room, den, bedroom, or mudroom, rural-tier farmhouse/ranch/mobile-home archetypes | Moderate-high probability (30-50%) rural tier, lower (10-15%) small town, lower still small city | A well-grounded rural-Midwest material-culture item (hunting culture, farm-pest control, rural self-reliance norms) that the existing catalog's rural archetypes (farmhouse, mobile home) don't yet list; a strong settlement-tier signal independent of income. |
| **Workshop/tool bench beyond the garage baseline** (dedicated hobbyist workshop: woodworking tools, small-engine repair bench) | Garage, basement, or a farm outbuilding | Higher probability for retiree/empty-nester households (a repurposed-room outcome under Dimension 2) and for middle-income rural/small-town households generally | Distinct from the baseline "workbench (0-1)" already in several pools — this is a fuller hobbyist buildout, more common where an empty-nester has converted freed-up space and time into an active hobby, or where rural self-repair culture makes a well-stocked home workshop a practical necessity rather than a leisure indulgence. |
