# Residential Item Placement — Broader Causal Notes

Companion to `residential_demographic_framework.md`. That file covers demographic *variation* (who lives there changes what's in the room). This file covers **general placement/existence logic** that applies across nearly all households and archetypes regardless of demographic roll — the "why" behind patterns the base `building_catalog/residential/` files already state as conventions, plus several not yet called out there. Use this as background reasoning the generator can draw on when it needs a placement rule beyond "sample from the pool."

## 1. Plumbing clustering (bathroom-kitchen adjacency, vertical stacking)

The base catalog already notes bathrooms are placed "stacked above the kitchen" or "near existing plumbing" in several files (Foursquare, Victorian). The underlying why: running water supply and drain-waste-vent (DWV) piping is the single most expensive, least flexible system in a house to install and maintain. Concentrating every water-using fixture — kitchen sink, bathroom(s), laundry — around one vertical "wet wall" or plumbing core minimizes pipe run length, minimizes the number of places a roof/floor penetration is needed for vent stacks, and lets multi-story houses reuse the same vertical chase on every floor. This is why:
- Upstairs bathrooms in 2-story archetypes (Foursquare, Colonial Revival, Victorian) are placed directly above the kitchen or a ground-floor half-bath, not above the living room.
- Additions (a retrofit half-bath, a converted mudroom laundry) overwhelmingly appear adjacent to the kitchen rather than on the opposite side of the house — it's the cheapest wall to tap into existing supply/drain lines.
- In split-level and ranch plans, the "wet" rooms (kitchen, bathrooms, laundry) tend to cluster on one structural bay even when the floor plan otherwise reads as open/zoned.
- Older houses retrofitted with indoor plumbing (Victorian, farmhouse, pre-1945 Foursquare) show this pattern most awkwardly — a bathroom shoehorned into a former closet or hallway nook specifically because that's where it was cheapest to run pipe up from the kitchen below, which is why these retrofit bathrooms in the catalog are correctly modeled as smaller/oddly-shaped than purpose-built ones.

## 2. Closet and storage placement (beyond the era effect already covered)

Dimension 3 of the demographic framework covers *whether* built-in closets exist by era. Independent of that: even where closets exist, they cluster at bedroom entries (not interior walls) because a closet doubles as an acoustic/thermal buffer between the hallway and the sleeping space, and because framing a closet at the entry wall lets the door swing into a zone that's already circulation space rather than eating into usable floor area. Linen closets specifically cluster next to the bathroom (for the same "keep related storage near its point of use" logic as the plumbing core above) rather than in a bedroom. Attic and basement storage is where *seasonal* items live (holiday decorations, off-season clothing, luggage) precisely because these are the lowest-value, least-climate-controlled square footage in the house — items that are needed only 1-4 weeks/year get relegated to the space that's worst for daily-use furniture but perfectly fine for a boxed-up artificial Christmas tree.

## 3. Basements: frost-line foundations and the storm-cellar legacy

Basements are unusually common in Midwestern residential construction (present as a stated feature in Foursquare, Colonial Revival, Split-Level, and implied in Farmhouse Vernacular) for a specific regional-engineering reason, not just tradition: the Midwest's continental climate requires foundations to be dug below the local frost line (roughly 42-52 inches / 3.5-4.5 ft deep in the northern Midwest) to prevent frost-heave from cracking the foundation over repeated freeze-thaw cycles. Once a foundation trench is already being dug that deep, excavating a full basement instead of a shallow crawlspace adds relatively little marginal cost for a large amount of usable/storage square footage — which is why basements are the regional default even in modest houses, unlike warmer-climate regions where a shallow frost line makes a crawlspace or slab foundation cheaper and basements comparatively rare. Separately, below-grade rooms in this region carry a second functional legacy: 19th-century root cellars (dug for food preservation before mechanical refrigeration) were the direct ancestor of the purpose-built storm/tornado cellar, since Midwestern settlers already had a below-grade room and tornado risk made it worth reinforcing; a finished or unfinished basement family room in a postwar ranch or split-level is the domesticated, non-agricultural descendant of that same below-grade-shelter tradition, and even purely "rec room" basements plausibly retain a few storm-safety props (flashlight, battery radio, stored water) as low-probability set dressing, especially in rural-tier households.

## 4. Mudroom / rear-entry service zone

Farmhouse, bungalow, ranch, and split-level plans in the catalog all note a rear/side service entry near the kitchen. The why is climate- and lifestyle-driven, compounding the plumbing-adjacency logic above: Midwestern winters mean mud, slush, and snow are tracked in for roughly half the year, and farm/yard-work households (which describes most rural and many small-town households historically) need a transitional space to shed boots, coats, and dirty outerwear *before* entering finished living space — not after. Locating this zone next to the kitchen (rather than the formal front entry) reflects that the rear/service door is the door actually used daily by the household, while the front door is reserved for guests and formal occasions; this front/back door social split is itself a durable Midwestern small-town convention worth modeling (a doorbell-answering front porch culture coexisting with a "just come round back" everyday-use culture) and explains why front porches (heavily emphasized in Foursquare, bungalow, farmhouse) function more as a social/street-facing display feature than as the household's actual primary circulation path.

## 5. Formal vs. informal living-room split, and its later collapse

Pre-1945 and immediate-postwar archetypes (Victorian's parlor + family sitting room, Foursquare's living room + optional den) reflect a genuine social-practice split: a "good" room kept presentable for receiving guests/clergy/business callers, and a second, lived-in room for the household's actual daily use. This wasn't purely a space-abundance luxury — it reflected a domestic-etiquette norm (unannounced callers were common before telephones made visits schedulable, so a presentable room had to be ready at all times) that faded as phone culture, then further as informal postwar suburban social norms, made "the good room kept for company" less necessary. The ranch/split-level family room and the later "great room" open-concept trend are the direct descendants of the informal room *winning* over the formal room as the norm, which is why the catalog correctly shows the formal/informal split narrowing over time even independent of the demographic framework's income-tier effects.

## 6. Staircase as circulation spine (multi-story archetypes)

Every multi-story archetype in the catalog (Foursquare, Victorian, Colonial Revival, Split-Level, Tudor Revival) places the staircase as a central organizing element visible from the entry. This is a structural-and-social convention, not an aesthetic accident: a centrally placed stair minimizes the total hallway length needed to reach every upstairs room (structural/cost efficiency), while a stair visible from the front door historically doubled as a "grand entrance" display feature for a household's best asset (Victorian) or, in more modest houses, simply as the most direct route to conserve floor area that would otherwise be lost to a dedicated corridor. Split-level takes this to its logical extreme, making the half-flight landing itself the circulation hub.

## 7. Focal-point furniture arrangement

Within any social/living room regardless of archetype or era, seating furniture (sofa, armchairs) clusters around one dominant focal point rather than being distributed evenly around the room's perimeter. The specific focal point shifts by era in a way that itself carries diagnostic value for the generator: pre-1945 rooms orient seating toward the fireplace mantel (the literal historic heat source and a status display surface for family photos/clock/mirror); mid-century rooms increasingly orient toward the picture window (the ranch archetype explicitly calls this out as its "showpiece" feature) or a new television; and from the 1980s onward the television becomes the dominant, near-universal focal point, with the entertainment-unit wall effectively replacing the mantel as the room's organizing furniture axis. A generator populating a living room should pick ONE focal wall per instance (fireplace, picture window, or TV wall, weighted by era) and arrange the seating pool around it rather than scattering pieces independently.

## 8. Laundry location as a technology-and-labor-history marker

Laundry appliance placement moves through three distinct historical zones that the catalog's archetypes already implicitly span: pre-1945 stock has no dedicated laundry room at all (washing was a basement, back-porch, or outdoor task using a wringer washer and clothesline, not a plumbed-in appliance room); 1945-1980 construction commonly places the washer/dryer in the basement (ranch, split-level) alongside the furnace/mechanical equipment, reflecting laundry's continued status as a "utility" task relegated to unfinished space; and 1980-present construction increasingly moves the washer/dryer to a **main-floor laundry closet or room near the bedrooms/kitchen** (already noted as a double-wide mobile-home and newer-construction feature), reflecting both smaller/appliance-efficient stacked units becoming available and a labor-convenience shift (why carry laundry up and down a full flight of stairs when the bedrooms — where dirty laundry originates and clean laundry gets put away — are on the main or upper floor). A generator can use laundry location as a free, already-implied era signal without adding new items: basement placement reads older/postwar, main-floor-near-bedrooms placement reads newer construction or a renovated older house.

## 9. Garage as workshop, not just car storage

Nearly every catalog file with a garage (ranch, split-level) lists a workbench and tool storage as part of the garage pool, at low-to-moderate probability. This is a genuine, well-documented Midwestern/rural material-culture pattern rather than filler: home/farm equipment maintenance, seasonal yard-tool storage, and a general regional self-repair ethos (worth noting as a soft cultural trait of the setting rather than a hard citation) make the garage function as a de facto second workspace in addition to vehicle storage, especially for owner-occupied single-family houses (much less true for apartment/rental households, which the generator should reflect by suppressing workshop-tier garage content for apartment archetypes even when a carport/parking structure exists).

## 10. Front porch as the social interface, driveway/garage as the functional one

Tying together points 4 and 5: pre-1945 and bungalow-era archetypes emphasize a large, furnished front porch (the catalog notes porch furniture and social use repeatedly) precisely because it was the primary interface between a household and its street-facing community before universal air conditioning and TV-centered evening entertainment pulled social life indoors and rearward. Ranch and split-level's shift toward a rear patio/sliding-glass-door arrangement, paired with a functional (not social) front-facing garage door, marks the inversion of this pattern — by the postwar era the *rear* yard became the informal social space (patio furniture, grill) while the front became purely a drive-up/curb-appeal facade. This is a useful era-keyed placement default: pre-1945/bungalow-era instances should bias toward front-porch furniture and social detail; 1945+ instances should bias that same "outdoor social furniture" budget toward the rear patio instead.
