# Commercial / Civic / Farm — Community Economic Condition Layer

Companion to `research/building_catalog/commercial_civic_farm/`. That catalog's per-space content pools are static regardless of the surrounding town's economic health. This file defines a **community economic-condition roll** and, for 10 priority archetypes, how that roll should bias stock mix, item condition, staffing/occupancy signals, and decor era — plus the causal WHY behind each variation. See `_synthesis.md` for how this combines with the residential demographic layer and how the generator should apply both without replacing the base catalog pools.

## The community economic-condition dimension

Roll **one** economic-condition value per settlement (not per building) from:

- **Thriving** — stable or growing population, diversified local economy (regional trade center, active farm-service economy, or tourism/lake-resort draw), low commercial vacancy.
- **Stable-but-plain** — flat population, a functioning but unremarkable local economy (the default/baseline case — most small Midwestern towns), moderate vacancy, businesses surviving but not flourishing.
- **Struggling / Rust-Belt-adjacent** — population decline, a lost or shrunken anchor employer (factory closure, rail decline, farm consolidation reducing the trade-area population), visible commercial vacancy, deferred maintenance across downtown.

A fourth **modifier flag**, independent of the tier above, can be layered on: **Lake-resort / tourist-adjacent** — applies a seasonal-tourism stock/decor bias on top of whichever economic tier is rolled, since a struggling manufacturing town and a struggling farm town look different from a struggling (off-season) resort town, and a thriving one likewise differs by what's driving the prosperity.

**Why this matters causally, in general:** every business on Main Street is a small enterprise whose owner makes real capital-allocation decisions (restock vs. not, renovate vs. defer, hire vs. not) driven by the trade area's actual foot traffic and disposable income. Main Street America's own research finds vacant storefronts cited as a top challenge by roughly a fifth of Main Street program leaders and as the second most-cited resident concern about downtown health — vacancy is not cosmetic, it's a direct, well-documented economic-health indicator that erodes the remaining businesses' foot traffic and the town's sales-tax base in a reinforcing spiral. The generator should treat "how full/well-stocked/well-maintained is this building" as a direct rendering of that same spiral, not an independent random roll per building.

## 1. Hardware Store

| Condition | Stock mix / condition | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Thriving (farm-service town) | Sales floor pool skews toward **full/deep implement-and-fastener stock** (the catalog's rural-tier "implement corner" becomes standard rather than optional), seasonal end-caps well-stocked and rotated (spring: garden/fencing; fall: heating/storm prep), loading bay shows active pallet turnover, paint-mixing counter well-used. New/updated shelving and signage. | A thriving farm-service town's hardware store is functioning as genuine agricultural infrastructure (fence wire, fencing hardware, small-engine parts, plumbing for farm buildings) as much as a retail shop — deep, current inventory is a direct function of a healthy surrounding farm economy actually buying. |
| Struggling / Rust-Belt | Sales floor shows **visible gaps on shelving** (roll shelving-unit count toward the pool's low end, with some units partially empty rather than removed — a store that can't afford to restock doesn't remove the fixture, it just doesn't refill it), reduced seasonal end-cap variety, older/faded signage, loading bay quiet (low delivery frequency). Possible second-income cross-stock (the store also sells something unrelated — feed, hunting licenses, key cutting for the whole county) as a diversification survival strategy. | A hardware store in a town that has lost its manufacturing or farm-consolidation-shrunk trade area sees genuinely lower unit sales; owners respond by thinning inventory breadth before closing outright (visible partial-empty shelving is the classic "declining Main Street business" visual, distinct from a thriving store's full shelves) and by diversifying into whatever else the remaining local customer base will actually buy. |
| Lake-resort modifier | Sales floor adds a **seasonal/tourist merchandise end-cap** (boat hardware, dock supplies, fishing tackle, citronella candles, cheap beach/camp gear) that swells in relative floor share in-season and shrinks (or gets covered/stored) off-season. | Resort-adjacent hardware stores serve two customer bases — year-round residents needing ordinary hardware, and seasonal cabin/lake-property owners needing marine and outdoor-recreation supplies — and the merchandising literally tracks the tourist season's presence or absence. |

## 2. Diner / Cafe

| Condition | Decor era / upkeep | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Thriving | Booths/counter stools show **recent reupholstery or replacement** (vinyl without cracking/tape repairs), full counter-stool occupancy pattern is plausible at peak hours, pie case kept full and rotated, updated (if still retro-styled) signage, coffee machine/urn is a newer unit. | A busy diner has enough covers-per-day turnover to justify reinvesting in the physical plant; fresh upholstery and a full pastry case are the most legible "business is good" signals in this archetype. |
| Struggling / Rust-Belt | Decor reads as **frozen in an earlier decade** relative to the town's actual current year — the classic "time capsule" declining-Main-Street diner look: original (not retro-revival) vinyl booths with visible wear/tape patches, a hand-lettered rather than printed menu board, a thinned pie case, fewer staffed counter stools in use. Booths/tables pool should roll toward the low end of its range if some have been removed to consolidate seating. | A diner surviving on a shrunken customer base defers reinvestment in furniture and equipment as long as the fixtures remain functional — the "decor era frozen in amber" look isn't nostalgia styling, it's the literal, unrenovated original decor from whenever the business last had money to spend, which is precisely why diner interior age is one of the most legible visual proxies for a town's economic trajectory along its whole Main Street. |
| Lake-resort modifier | Menu-board and pie-case content bias toward tourist-friendly fare signage (ice cream flavors board, "world famous" pie sign), seasonal outdoor-seating implication (even if not separately modeled), higher counter-stool occupancy variance between in-season and off-season. | Resort-town diners depend heavily on seasonal tourist traffic layered on top of a smaller year-round local base, which shows up as a business that looks "thriving" for part of the year and much quieter the rest — a useful variance signal distinct from the flat thriving/struggling read of a farm or rust-belt town's diner. |

## 3. General Store / Small Grocery

| Condition | Stock mix / condition | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Thriving | Dry-goods tables and shelving pool rolls toward full/high counts, produce/scale area active and fresh-looking, wider range of "extras" (the catalog's optional post-office window, candy case, hanging-goods rack all present together rather than the settlement having to choose). In a small-grocery instance specifically: multiple checkout lanes staffed, cart corral implied full. | In the smallest settlements the general store/grocery is often the only retail anchor and its full or thin stock is a direct read on whether the whole village's economy can support ANY commercial center — a thriving instance signals the village itself is holding population and farm-trade income. |
| Struggling / Rust-Belt | Stock rolls toward the **narrower survival core** (staples only: basic groceries, tobacco, a reduced dry-goods selection) with visibly thinner shelving, a single checkout, possibly reduced operating footprint (part of the sales floor curtained off or repurposed for the owner's other side-business). At the extreme low end for a rural-village instance, the store may be barely surviving on a Dollar-General-style bare-bones stock model. | A general store in a declining rural trade area is often literally the last commercial holdout in town (documented real cases: small Rust Belt towns left with no grocery besides a dollar store) — the generator should be willing to render a struggling-tier village general store as a bare, thinly-stocked survival operation rather than a smaller version of the thriving stock mix, since below a certain population/income threshold the business model itself changes, not just its fullness. |
| Lake-resort modifier | Adds a seasonal tourist/camp-goods end-cap (bait, ice, sunscreen, marshmallows/camp food, cheap souvenirs) alongside the year-round grocery stock; this share swells in-season. | Same seasonal dual-customer-base logic as the hardware store — the resort general store is simultaneously the year-round village grocery and the seasonal camp/cottage convenience stop. |

## 4. Church

| Condition | Fellowship-hall usage intensity / furnishing wear | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Thriving (larger/growing congregation, or simply a larger settlement tier) | Fellowship hall furniture (folding tables, stacking chairs) rolls toward the pool's higher counts and shows **active-use wear consistent with frequent use, not damage** (scuffed but not broken, well-organized storage of the table/chair stock rather than a haphazard pile), kitchen equipment more likely to include the small-city-tier institutional dishwasher, active bulletin board with a full weekly schedule of Sunday-school/community events implying a fuller sacristy/classroom count actually in regular use. | A larger or growing congregation uses its fellowship hall multiple times a week (potlucks, meetings, Sunday school, community rentals), which both wears the furniture faster (a data point distinct from "old" wear) and justifies the congregation actually buying the higher end of the equipment pool. |
| Struggling (small/shrinking congregation, common in a declining rural village) | Nave pew count rolls toward the low end relative to the building's built capacity (a visibly under-filled sanctuary — same pew ROOM but implied lower regular attendance), fellowship hall furniture count lower and shows **long-term-storage wear** (dusty, stacked rather than actively cycled, an older folding-table/chair stock never replaced), kitchen equipment older/residential-grade rather than institutional even at a tier where the base pool would suggest otherwise. | Rural population decline directly shrinks congregation size before it shrinks the building itself (churches are rarely downsized physically, they're simply used at lower capacity) — this makes "a large sanctuary serving a small, aging congregation" a genuine and visually renderable small-town decline signal, and ties directly to the church's real historic role as the small town's central social hub: a fellowship hall's use-intensity is as much a read on the TOWN's remaining social vitality as on the congregation's religious health specifically, since in many small Midwestern towns the church hall is also the default venue for funeral luncheons, election-day polling, and community meetings the base catalog's placement notes already call out. |

## 5. Library

| Condition | Technology level | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Thriving (larger settlement or well-funded small city) | Multiple current-generation computer terminals (the pool's "1-3 depending on era" rolls toward 3, modern flat-panel not CRT), no card catalog present (fully digitized), active meeting-room booking implied by a full events calendar on the bulletin/digital signage, current periodicals well-stocked. | Library funding in the US comes overwhelmingly from local government (property tax/municipal budget) — nearly 90% of funding per library-funding research — so a fiscally healthy municipality with a larger tax base directly funds better library technology; recent library-budget survey data shows real average technology spending in the hundreds of thousands of dollars annually among reporting libraries, concentrated at better-funded institutions. |
| Struggling / small-village or Rust-Belt-adjacent | Older computer terminals in reduced number (1, at capacity/waitlist implied), a **legacy card catalog retained** either as the primary system (poorest/smallest instances) or as a decorative/backup holdover next to a single newer terminal, static paper bulletin board rather than digital signage, visibly aged furniture (reading tables/chairs from an earlier decade, not replaced). | Library-funding research explicitly documents that "the smaller the town, the larger the technology funding gap" — small, rural, and fiscally stressed municipalities cannot keep pace with computer-replacement cycles, so equipment persists well past its normal service life; this makes library technology level one of the single most direct, well-documented proxies available for municipal budget health, and by extension the town's underlying economic-condition tier (library funding tracks the same local tax base that funds every other municipal service). |

## 6. Town Hall / Municipal Building

| Condition | Technology level / upkeep | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Thriving | Council chambers shows modern audio/recording equipment (implied, low render priority) or at minimum well-kept furnishings, clerk's/utility-billing offices show computer terminals as standard rather than optional, building exterior/lobby well-maintained (fresh paint, working clock/flagpole feature), records-archive area organized with modern filing systems. | A fiscally healthy municipality (larger tax base, growing or stable population, diversified local economy) funds municipal-office technology and maintenance at the same rate it funds the library above — town hall condition is a direct, unmediated read of the local government's own operating budget, with no private-business risk-taking layer in between (unlike a Main Street retailer), making it one of the CLEANEST economic-tier signals in the whole catalog. |
| Struggling / Rust-Belt | Offices show older equipment (typewriter/ledger-book holdovers alongside, not replacing, a single aging computer), visible deferred maintenance (peeling paint, a non-functional decorative clock or fountain, water-stained ceiling tile as a low-priority detail), records-archive area cluttered/disorganized (reflects understaffing — clerical hours cut before services are cut entirely), reduced or informally-staffed reception (a "back in 15 minutes" sign is a plausible, cheap detail). | Municipal budgets in declining towns contract directly with the property-tax and (where applicable) sales-tax base — a shrunken or lost employer doesn't just hurt private business, it directly cuts the town's own operating revenue, and municipal government responds to budget contraction the same way small businesses do: deferred maintenance and equipment held past its service life, visible in the built environment years before a town's population decline shows up in any other way. |

## 7. Bank Branch

| Condition | Fixtures / staffing signal | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Thriving | Full teller-line staffing implied (multiple active windows out of the pool's 2-5 range), safe-deposit box room present and full-looking, private loan-officer offices in active use (papers/nameplates present, not empty), lobby fixtures well-kept (working decorative clock, healthy potted plants as a small maintenance-budget tell). | A bank branch's own health is a lagging-but-reliable proxy for its trade area's economic health — loan-officer offices in active use specifically signal real local lending demand (farm-operating loans, small-business loans, mortgages), which dries up in a town losing population/income faster than the branch itself closes. |
| Struggling / Rust-Belt | Reduced teller-window staffing (1-2 active out of the pool's range, others closed/roped off), private offices reduced to a single shared one or removed entirely (matches the catalog's rural-village-tier reduction note, but apply it here even at town/city tier as a DECLINE signal rather than a settlement-size default), safe-deposit room present but visibly underfilled, a branch consolidation/reduced-hours sign as a cheap set-dressing detail. In the most severe instances, the building may be flagged as a former-bank adaptive reuse (a different business occupying the shell) rather than an active bank at all — a well-documented real pattern on struggling Main Streets. | Bank branch consolidation and closure is one of the most direct, board-level-decision-driven economic indicators a small town can display — regional/national banks make branch-staffing and closure decisions based on measurable local deposit and loan volume, so a bank branch's visible staffing/service level is a genuinely externally-validated (not just narratively implied) signal of the town's underlying economic trajectory, distinct from a locally-owned business's more personal survival calculus. |

## 8. Gas Station

| Condition | Stock / bay activity | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Thriving (including highway/through-traffic-served instances) | Multiple service bays active (lifts in use, tool chests well-stocked), office snack/road-map rack full and current, pump islands implied busy, canopy/signage well-maintained or recently updated. | A gas station's business is directly a function of local + through-traffic vehicle volume; a healthy local economy (more vehicles, more disposable income for road trips and vehicle maintenance) and/or a location on an active regional through-route both directly drive bay utilization and stock turnover. |
| Struggling / Rust-Belt | Reduced to a single active bay (others' pool count present as fixtures but visibly disused/storage-repurposed), older equipment (an aging hydraulic lift, hand tools rather than pneumatic), office stock thin, worn/faded signage, canopy showing rust/deferred paint. Plausible full closure/abandonment variant (boarded bays, removed pumps) as the most severe struggling-tier instance, consistent with real documented small-town cases of towns left without an operating gas station at all. | Small independent gas stations operate on thin per-gallon margins and are highly sensitive to local vehicle-ownership/travel-frequency declines that track population and income loss directly; they are also disproportionately vulnerable to being bypassed when a town loses through-traffic to a new highway alignment or to consolidation into larger chain stations elsewhere, both well-documented small-town retail-decline patterns. |
| Lake-resort modifier | Office stock adds tourist/boat-fuel-adjacent items (marine oil, bait cooler, tourist snack/drink selection), higher seasonal bay/pump activity variance. | Resort-area gas stations serve a seasonally spiking customer base (boat/vehicle traffic to and from the lake) layered on a smaller year-round local base, the same dual-market pattern as the hardware store and general store above. |

## 9. Bar / Tavern

| Condition | Decor / stock | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Thriving | Full beer-tap selection (rolls toward the pool's higher end), well-kept backbar, working jukebox/TV (whichever era-appropriate entertainment fixture the pool includes), consistent stool/booth occupancy pattern implied, back-room pool table in good repair. | As the default small-town "third place" alongside the church and diner, a tavern's investment in fixtures (taps, jukebox, TV) tracks disposable local income directly — a healthy trade area supports a fuller, more frequently updated stock and equipment set. |
| Struggling / Rust-Belt | Reduced tap selection (2-3 rather than the pool's higher end, cheaper/regional-macro-brand-only implied rather than variety), older/non-functional jukebox retained as decor rather than working equipment, worn bar top and stool upholstery, thinner weekday occupancy implied. In the most severe instances, this archetype is a strong candidate for being one of the LAST businesses standing on a declining Main Street (a well-documented real pattern — the tavern/bar often outlasts other retail because alcohol demand is comparatively inelastic to local economic decline, even as its fixtures and stock thin out). | A bar's survivability under economic decline differs structurally from retail (inelastic-ish demand for a modest number of loyal regulars keeps it open longer even as the town depopulates), but its REINVESTMENT in fixtures/stock still tracks the local economy closely — making "still open, but visibly threadbare" the single most realistic struggling-tier rendering for this archetype, rather than closure. |
| Lake-resort modifier | Adds tourist-season signage/merchandise (branded koozies, a lake-themed sign wall), stool/booth occupancy swings sharply between in-season (very busy, possibly crowded beyond the pool's normal seating count) and off-season (quiet, local-regulars-only). | Lake-resort taverns/supper-clubs (the catalog notes the upper-Midwest "supper club" variant) see the most dramatic seasonal demand swing of any archetype in this list, since their trade area literally multiplies during the summer tourist season. |

## Cross-archetype summary table

| Archetype | Thriving signal | Struggling signal | Resort modifier |

| Hardware store | Deep/current stock, active loading bay | Gapped shelving, faded signage, diversified side-stock | Seasonal marine/dock end-cap |

| Library | Multiple current terminals, no card catalog | Legacy card catalog retained, aged furniture | — |
| Town hall | Modern office tech, well-kept lobby | Deferred maintenance, understaffed archive | — |

| Gas station | Multiple active bays, well-stocked office | Single bay, rusted canopy, possible closure | Marine-fuel/tourist stock |
| Bar/tavern | Full tap selection, working fixtures | Threadbare but still open (last-business-standing) | Seasonal occupancy swing, tourist merch |
